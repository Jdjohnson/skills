# Green Supply Chain Management
## Purpose
Integrate environmental criteria into supply-chain design and operations and sequence a green SC improvement roadmap. It changes sourcing, transport, packaging, production interfaces, and return flows while respecting cost and service commitments.
## Use
- Embedding environmental criteria into sourcing, logistics, packaging, and reverse flows.
- Building a roadmap to cut supply-chain emissions, waste, and resource intensity.
- Responding to customer, investor, or regulatory pressure on supply-chain sustainability.
## Avoid
- You only need a corporate carbon inventory with no SC redesign—use carbon-footprint-assessment.
- The agenda is product take-back and closed loops as the primary model—use circular-economy-framework.
- You need enterprise ESG strategy above the supply chain—use esg-strategy first.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Baseline emissions, waste, and material-intensity data by SC stage; Supplier environmental performance and audit findings; Customer and regulatory requirements on green logistics and materials; Current network, packaging, and reverse-logistics design; Cost and service constraints that any green change must respect.

**Useful additions:** shipment lanes, bill-of-materials weights, energy factors, supplier contracts, and disposal charges.
## Procedure
1. Draw the physical flow from tier-one supplier through inbound freight, conversion, warehousing, delivery, returns, and disposal. Establish the unit of analysis, such as one shipped case or one tonne of finished product.
2. Build a stage-level baseline for carbon, material use, water, waste, and hazardous exposure. Reconcile high-impact lanes and materials before estimating minor activities.
3. Segment suppliers by spend, environmental intensity, and substitutability. Set supplier requirements, data requests, audit triggers, and escalation paths rather than treating all vendors identically.
4. Generate interventions at the point of impact: material substitution, yield improvement, load consolidation, mode shift, reusable packaging, network redesign, or recovery routing. Quantify environmental effect alongside landed cost, lead time, and service risk.
5. Prioritize the portfolio using abatement potential, feasibility, capital, customer value, and dependency constraints. Separate immediate operating changes from design changes requiring engineering or contracts.
6. Put approved actions into a roadmap with accountable owners, supplier milestones, measurement method, and a recurring review of both environmental and service performance.
## Output Contract
Produce a green supply-chain roadmap with a stage-impact baseline, hotspot map, supplier segmentation, initiative portfolio, quantified trade-offs, owners, and dates. Each initiative must identify its affected flow, environmental KPI, financial or service consequence, and evidence quality. The result is a change portfolio, not merely an emissions total or corporate sustainability statement.
## Evidence
Use measured fuel, material, and waste data where available. Label supplier estimates, emissions factors, and allocation choices as inferred or assumed, especially when data are spend-based. Keep Scope 3 supplier claims distinct from operationally controlled freight and facilities. When a proposed improvement shifts impact to another stage, show the net lifecycle direction rather than taking credit for a local reduction.
## Checks
- The baseline follows physical flows and does not omit returns or packaging.
- Material hotspots account for enough impact to justify the roadmap's focus.
- Supplier scorecards include remediation consequences, not a questionnaire alone.
- Every priority action has a cost, service, and environmental measure.
- A stated reduction can be traced to a unit, baseline year, and calculation logic.
## Failure Modes
- Optimizing empty truck space while ignoring the high-impact raw material.
- Replacing packaging with a heavier reusable format without accounting for return distance and loss rate.
- Mandating supplier disclosures that small vendors cannot produce, then accepting unverifiable values.
- Setting carbon goals that procurement can override whenever unit price rises by a few cents.
