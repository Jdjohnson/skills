# 360-Degree Feedback
## Purpose
Collect structured multi-rater evidence on how a person shows up from above, beside, and below to surface blind spots and development priorities. It creates a confidential behavioral picture for development, rather than a job grade or an organization-wide engagement score.
## Use
- Building a multi-source evidence base on leader or manager behaviors before coaching or succession decisions.
- Diagnosing gap patterns between self-perception and how peers, reports, and stakeholders experience someone.
- Feeding development plans with specific behavioral evidence rather than single-rater opinions.
## Avoid
- You need job requirements and relative role value rather than multi-rater behavior evidence—use job-analysis-and-evaluation.
- You only need anonymous workforce climate trends at scale—use employee-engagement-surveying.
- The person is in acute performance crisis where developmental multi-rater data will be misused as a case file—use performance-management-system first.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Named subject(s) and clear purpose (development vs. talent insight); rater map covering manager, peers, direct reports, and relevant stakeholders; behaviorally anchored competency or leadership items; confidentiality and feedback-delivery protocol; baseline self-assessment from the subject.

**Nice-to-have:** Prior coaching goals, demographic checks for rater groups, open-comment guidance, and a qualified feedback facilitator.

**When data is thin:** Use fewer, well-chosen behavior items and report rater-group patterns only when enough responses protect anonymity.
## Procedure
1. **Set the contract.** Specify development versus talent use, who can see raw data, minimum response thresholds, and what will never be inferred from silence. Secure the subject’s informed participation.
2. **Define observable behaviors.** Convert broad labels such as “leadership” into rateable actions: invites challenge, sets priorities, gives timely feedback. Avoid personality claims and double-barrelled items.
3. **Build the rater map.** Nominate manager, peers, direct reports, and selected stakeholders; check that each group has direct exposure to the behaviors. An administrator, not the subject, confirms the final invitation list.
4. **Collect and clean responses.** Issue the same scale and comment prompts to all groups, monitor completion, remove duplicates, and suppress groups below the confidentiality threshold.
5. **Compare perspectives.** Calculate item and theme averages by rater group, then compare self-rating with others. Read comments for recurring behavioral evidence, not isolated criticism.
6. **Deliver and convert.** A trained facilitator helps the subject choose two development priorities, define practice actions, and agree a 60- or 90-day follow-up with their manager.
## Output Contract
A confidential feedback report containing the purpose and protections, rater-group response counts, behavior scores by group, self-versus-other gaps, recurring anonymous themes, and two to three development priorities with follow-up actions. It must distinguish a pattern across raters from a single comment and must not be repurposed silently as disciplinary evidence.
## Evidence
Treat submitted ratings and response counts as observed. Treat a consistent pattern across independent rater groups as an inference, not a diagnosis. Record assumptions about exposure, anonymity, and scale interpretation. Do not report a group result below the agreed minimum; mark it unavailable. Separate quoted comments from the facilitator’s synthesis and remove identifying detail.
## Checks
- Every item describes behavior a rater could actually observe.
- Rater groups have adequate response counts and no one is identifiable through a small cell.
- Self, manager, peer, and report views remain separately visible where valid.
- Comments are screened for identifying or abusive material without deleting substantive themes.
- The debrief produces specific practice commitments rather than a scorecard argument.
## Failure Modes
- Letting a manager select only friendly raters, which turns the exercise into endorsement.
- Combining anonymous report feedback with an appraisal decision, destroying candor.
- Averaging rater groups together so a serious direct-report pattern disappears.
- Treating a low score as a trait rather than asking which observed behavior needs changing.
- Sending the report without facilitated interpretation or follow-through.
