# Strategic Group Mapping
## Purpose
Map firms into strategic groups on key dimensions to reveal cluster rivalry, mobility barriers, and open positions. The map narrows the relevant peer set and tests whether apparent whitespace is defensible.
## Use
- Plotting rivals on key strategic dimensions to reveal groups, mobility barriers, and unoccupied positions.
- Selecting the right competitive peer set for strategy and benchmarking.
- Identifying group-level rivalry intensity versus industry-wide averages.
## Avoid
- You need industry-wide structural forces, not within-industry clusters—use five-forces.
- You need operational KPI comparison rather than strategy-dimension clustering—use competitive-benchmarking or operational-benchmarking.
- You need firm-internal capability diagnosis—use resource-based-view.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** List of relevant competitors and their observable strategic choices; two or more discriminating strategic dimensions (e.g., price/quality, scope, channel); rough size or performance indicators per firm for map weighting; hypotheses on mobility barriers between positions.

**Useful additions:** customer-switching evidence, historical moves, ownership structures, capacity footprints, and sources for each competitor observation.
## Procedure
1. Set the industry perimeter, geography, customer segment, and time period. Include substitutes only if buyers compare them in the same purchase decision.
2. Generate candidate dimensions from choices that differentiate business models, such as breadth of assortment, channel control, price tier, geographic reach, or service intensity. Reject dimensions that simply restate market share.
3. Select two axes that are independent enough to expose meaningful positions and can be scored consistently from public or research evidence. Define anchors before plotting any firm.
4. Score competitors, size each marker by revenue, capacity, or share, and show uncertainty where data are estimated. Look for concentrations, isolated firms, and inconsistencies that require rechecking.
5. Name groups by their strategic configuration rather than by a vague location on the chart. Test mobility barriers: investments, brand permission, channel access, capabilities, contracts, or regulation needed to move between groups.
6. Evaluate potential open positions against customer demand and barriers to entry; a blank space is not an opportunity until both are credible.
## Output Contract
Provide an annotated two-axis map, competitor score sheet, group definitions, peer-set recommendation, and mobility-barrier analysis. Each proposed open position states demand evidence, incumbent response risk, entry requirements, and confidence. The output is a strategic landscape, not a generic competitor list or operating benchmark.
## Evidence
Record the source and date for every plotted score. Distinguish observable facts, such as channel presence, from inferred positioning. Use ranges or confidence symbols when competitors disclose limited information. Do not infer competitive intent from a single advertising campaign. Make the axis scale visible so a visual distance does not imply a false numerical difference.
## Checks
- Axes reflect strategic choices that matter to buyers and rivalry.
- Firms are scored using consistent rules rather than reputation.
- Marker size uses one disclosed measure.
- Group labels explain common configuration, not merely a color on the map.
- Every claimed whitespace opportunity passes a barrier and demand test.
## Failure Modes
- Choosing price and quality as axes when both measure the same premium position and create a diagonal of no insight.
- Mapping firms nationally even though competitors and channels differ sharply by region.
- Treating an empty quadrant as attractive without estimating what it costs to build the missing capability.
- Benchmarking against every plotted firm instead of the group that shares the focal firm's competitive logic.
