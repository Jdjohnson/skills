# Rapid Prototyping
## Purpose
Build quick, low-cost models of product concepts so teams can test desirability and usability before engineering investment. A deliberately incomplete artifact makes a proposed interaction concrete enough for users to react to.
## Use
- You need quick, low-fidelity models to test interaction, desirability, or comprehension
- Multiple concept directions must be compared before committing engineering
- Design sprints or discovery workshops need tangible test objects
## Avoid
- You need real usage economics or retention evidence—use minimum-viable-product and lean-startup
- The decision is purely technical architecture—use buy-vs-build-analysis or sdlc-methodology
- Requirements are already locked for delivery—use user-stories-and-persona-mapping
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Target user scenario or job to exercise
- Design constraints (channel, brand, tech limits) and fidelity budget
- Test protocol and success criteria for usability or desirability
- Access to a small set of representative users or proxies

**Nice-to-have:** prior research clips, accessibility requirements, and a content inventory.

**When data is thin:** test the riskiest interaction with credible proxies and label the result directional, not representative.
## Procedure
1. Choose one user moment and articulate the learning question, such as whether a buyer can find delivery dates without help. Exclude visual polish that cannot change that answer.
2. Sketch two or three competing flows at paper, wireframe, or clickable fidelity. Make the entry point, choices, system feedback, and end state visible enough to provoke behavior.
3. Define test tasks and success signals before recruiting. Record task completion, hesitation points, misconceptions, time on task, and participant language rather than asking whether participants like the design.
4. Put the model in front of representative users one at a time. Encourage them to think aloud, but do not explain controls or rescue them before the observation is recorded.
5. Cluster observations by screen and severity. Separate defects in the concept from artifacts caused by the prototype’s unfinished fidelity.
6. Revise the model, then retest the changed assumption. Stop when repeated participants complete the critical task or when evidence indicates the concept should be discarded.
## Output Contract
Provide the prototype link or artifact, the scenario it represents, task script, participant profile, observation log, revision history, and a recommendation on the next design decision. Every finding must point to a screen or interaction and distinguish observed behavior from participant interpretation. The result is a tested concept with revision notes, not a release specification or a claim that customers will buy the product.
## Evidence
Capture recordings, task outcomes, and verbatim behavior notes as observed. Mark interpretations such as “this label caused confusion” as inferred until repeated evidence supports them. State prototype gaps, proxy-user limitations, and moderator prompts as assumptions that may have affected the result. Do not convert a five-person usability session into a market-size estimate.
## Checks
- The prototype can be used to attempt the stated task without narration.
- Each test measures a predeclared behavior or comprehension criterion.
- Fidelity is sufficient for the hypothesis but no higher than necessary.
- Revision notes connect a change to a specific observation.
- Engineering constraints that invalidate a preferred flow are recorded before handoff.
## Failure Modes
- Testing a polished mockup after the team has already committed to the flow.
- Asking users to predict future use instead of observing a task.
- Changing navigation, copy, and pricing together so feedback has no clear target.
- Treating a missing back-end integration as evidence that a user dislikes the concept.
- Recruiting colleagues who already know the product vocabulary.
