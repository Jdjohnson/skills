# Buy vs. Build Analysis
## Purpose
Compare internal development, acquisition, and partner/source paths to recommend how to obtain a needed capability or position. The decision considers ownership and strategic control alongside price and delivery speed.
## Use
- Choosing among internal development, acquisition, and partnership/sourcing to obtain a capability, product, or market position
- Comparing total cost, time-to-capability, control, and risk across ownership modes
- Forcing a single recommended path before committing capital or integration resources
## Avoid
- The firm already committed to an acquisition program and needs target screening—use inorganic-growth-strategy then commercial-due-diligence
- The question is pure vendor/category sourcing economics with no strategic ownership choice—use strategic-sourcing or total-cost-of-ownership
- You need post-deal integration design after buy is chosen—use m-and-a-integration-framework
## Inputs
**Must-have:**

- Defined capability, product, or market outcome required and success criteria
- Internal build cost, timeline, and capability-gap estimate
- Acquisition or partner options with indicative price, readiness, and integration burden
- Risk and control preferences (IP ownership, speed, cultural fit, regulatory exposure)
- Comparable make/buy economics or total cost of ownership benchmarks where available

**Nice-to-have:** target pipeline, customer deadlines, talent availability, exit options, and integration capacity.

**When data is thin:** make uncertainty explicit in ranges and require diligence or a discovery sprint before committing to the irreversible path.
## Procedure
1. **Define the capability boundary.** State the outcome, minimum viable capability, strategic importance, deadline, and what must remain controlled internally. Distinguish buying software, partnering, licensing, and acquiring a company; they are not one alternative. Output: decision charter.
2. **Construct credible paths.** Build a common description for internal development, acquisition, and partnership/source options, including milestones, resources, dependencies, and failure points. Eliminate options that cannot meet non-negotiable constraints. Output: comparable option cards.
3. **Model full economics and timing.** Estimate build investment and ramp, purchase price or fees, integration, operating costs, synergies, switching costs, and time to benefit. Show cash timing and scenario ranges, not just headline prices. Output: lifecycle economics view.
4. **Score strategic fit and risk.** Assess control of IP and data, differentiation, cultural fit, regulatory exposure, vendor concentration, and reversibility. Weight criteria with the decision makers before scoring options. Output: transparent trade-off matrix.
5. **Recommend a path and contingencies.** State the preferred path, decision triggers, diligence still required, and a fallback if the price, schedule, or integration assumption fails. Output: ownership decision memo.
## Output Contract
The memo describes the needed capability, viable ownership paths, common decision criteria, economics and timing ranges, strategic-control trade-offs, key assumptions, and a recommendation with gates. It must make clear why a lower-cost option may lose on speed or control, and it must not masquerade as a vendor price comparison.
## Evidence
Use signed proposals, target financials, internal delivery history, customer deadlines, and legal constraints as observed inputs. Treat synergy, talent retention, time-to-market, and integration effort as assumptions until diligence supports them. Keep acquisition price separate from integration cost. Do not assign a numerical score to “control” without explaining what decision rights or assets it protects.
## Checks
- Each path delivers the identical stated capability or documents the gap.
- Timing includes procurement, diligence, integration, and adoption, not only build dates.
- Economics include recurring operating and exit costs.
- Weighting is agreed before the preferred option is revealed.
- Recommendation includes a reversible first move where uncertainty remains high.
## Failure Modes
- Comparing a fully staffed build with a vendor's license price only.
- Calling an acquisition “buy” without pricing integration and retention risk.
- Treating a strategic partner as if the firm owns its roadmap or data.
- Letting a sunk internal prototype force a build decision.
- Selecting the fastest option without checking whether it creates an unmanageable dependency.
