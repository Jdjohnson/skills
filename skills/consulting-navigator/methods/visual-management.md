# Visual Management
## Purpose
Make performance, standards, and abnormal conditions visible at the point of work so teams detect problems and act without delayed reports. It designs the visual signal and response routine that lets a team recognize normal versus abnormal in seconds.
## Use
- Making process status, standards, and abnormalities visible at the point of work
- Enabling real-time team huddles and escalation without waiting for reports
- Sustaining lean standards, flow, and safety through shared visual signals
## Avoid
- Critical metrics are not yet trustworthy—fix measurement methods before building boards; pair with statistical-process-control for process data
- The underlying method is undefined so visuals would encode chaos—use standard-work first
- You need statistical control limits and capability analysis rather than operational status—use statistical-process-control
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Defined metrics, statuses, or conditions that must be visible to the team
- Agreement on normal vs. abnormal thresholds and escalation rules
- Physical or digital location where the work actually happens
- Owners responsible for updating signals each cadence (hour, shift, day)
- Link to standard work or process so visuals reinforce the agreed method

**Nice-to-have:** huddle agenda, response targets, board photos, shift overlap, and accessibility needs.

**When data is thin:** start with directly observable conditions; avoid dashboards the team cannot verify.
## Procedure
1. **Observe the work and decisions.** At the work, note what the team needs now: plan versus actual, safety, missing material, quality defect, or escalation owner. Output: decision-at-point-of-work list.
2. **Define normal and abnormal.** Set expected state, threshold, cue, and required response for each signal. A red marker without an owner is an alarm without a system. Output: signal-response matrix.
3. **Choose the visual device.** Match the medium to the decision: an hourly production board, andon, floor marking, queue lane, standard-work display, or digital screen. Place it where the action happens and test visibility from normal working distance. Output: board and device design.
4. **Build the update and escalation routine.** Assign who updates each field, when it refreshes, who leads the huddle, and how unresolved abnormalities move to the next level. Output: daily management cadence.
5. **Pilot at the work area.** Run several shifts, watch whether people notice and act without explanation, remove unused metrics, and tune thresholds that create noise. Output: adopted visual control standard.
## Output Contract
A visual management system with point-of-work boards or devices, signal definitions, thresholds, update owners, huddle cadence, escalation, and audit checks. Each visual answers an operational question and specifies the required action. It supports real-time control, not a 5S audit or pull authorization scheme.
## Evidence
Display measures with known collection and refresh timing. Label manual entries with owner and time. Use observed misses, delays, or defects for thresholds; mark judgment thresholds for review. A green board does not prove control. Preserve recurring red history to distinguish isolated from systemic deviation.
## Checks
- A new team member can tell the current condition and next action within 10 seconds.
- Every abnormal signal has a response owner and escalation time.
- The board resides at the work, not only in a manager's slide deck.
- Visuals reinforce documented standard work rather than replace it.
- Measures are removed when they do not prompt a decision or behavior.
## Failure Modes
- Installing colorful boards that no shift owns or updates.
- Using color without defining the numerical or observable trigger beneath it.
- Displaying weekly management metrics that are too late for frontline action.
- Treating a persistent red signal as decoration instead of escalating root-cause work.
- Confusing a kanban card's replenishment authorization with a general status signal.
