# 80/20 Rule
## Purpose
Rank causes, segments, or opportunities so effort concentrates on the vital few that drive most of the measured result. The analysis tests concentration in a single outcome metric; it does not prove the ranked items are root causes.
## Use
- You need to concentrate scarce analytical or management effort on the vital few drivers.
- Symptoms are numerous but you suspect a small set of causes or segments dominate outcomes.
- Prioritizing a long backlog of issues, customers, defects, or cost lines before deep diagnosis.
## Avoid
- You still lack a clear outcome metric to rank against—define the dependent variable first.
- The problem is a single root cause to prove, not a ranked set of contributors—use root-cause-analysis or hypothesis-driven-problem-solving.
- You need structural industry or capability diagnosis rather than concentration ranking—use five-forces or value-chain-analysis.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** A defined outcome metric (revenue, cost, defects, cycle time, complaints); a complete or representative set of candidate causes, items, or segments; frequency, volume, or impact counts for each item; agreement on the unit of analysis (customer, SKU, process step, issue type).

**Nice-to-have:** Trend data, cost per occurrence, exposure denominators, and drill-down to individual items.

**When data is thin:** Use a time-boxed sample, state its coverage, and do not label an item “vital” until the ranking survives a second period or sample.
## Procedure
1. **Choose the outcome and unit.** Define exactly what is being accumulated: dollars of rework, complaint count, lost hours, or margin. Ensure categories do not overlap.
2. **Collect and clean the tallies.** Extract the complete measurement period, remove duplicates, normalize labels, and retain an “other/unknown” bucket rather than forcing ambiguous records into a favorite category.
3. **Calculate contribution.** Sum each item’s magnitude, sort descending, and compute each item’s share of the total.
4. **Calculate cumulative share.** Add shares down the ranked list and plot bars with a cumulative line or build an equivalent table. Identify the break where concentrated contributors give way to the long tail.
5. **Select a focus set.** Choose the smallest actionable group covering a justified share of the outcome; 80/20 is a heuristic, so the cut may be 68/17 or 92/9.
6. **Verify and hand off.** Check whether high contributors are real, controllable, and stable across periods. Send the focus set to root-cause analysis, bottleneck work, or an owner with a target.
## Output Contract
A prioritized list or Pareto chart containing the outcome definition, period, unit of analysis, total magnitude, each item’s magnitude and percentage, cumulative percentage, selected focus set, and next analytical action. It must distinguish observed contribution from causation and state what portion of the outcome is outside the listed categories.
## Evidence
Transaction records, defect logs, and counts are observed evidence after reconciliation. Category mappings and the selected cutoff are assumptions that must be visible. Do not combine frequency and financial impact in one ranking without a stated conversion. Where exposure differs sharply, consider a rate view alongside absolute magnitude so a high-volume category is not mistaken for a uniquely bad one.
## Checks
- Categories are mutually exclusive and cover the stated total.
- Magnitudes reconcile to the source total, including unknowns.
- Sorting and cumulative percentages are mathematically correct.
- The focus set is actionable and not chosen solely because it reaches a ritual 80% line.
- A second view tests whether exposure or time period changes the conclusion.
## Failure Modes
- Ranking complaint frequency when the decision concerns refund cost.
- Calling the highest bar a root cause before investigating the mechanism.
- Hiding an enormous “other” category that invalidates the ranking.
- Combining different units, such as minutes and dollars, without conversion.
- Launching improvement projects for every bar instead of protecting focus.
