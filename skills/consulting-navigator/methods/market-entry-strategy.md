# Market Entry Strategy
## Purpose
Select and sequence the best mode and path to enter a defined market given attractiveness, barriers, and firm capabilities.
## Use
- Choosing how to enter a new geography, segment, or category
- Comparing organic, partnership, JV, licensing, and acquisition paths
- Building a sequenced entry roadmap after attractiveness is established
## Avoid
- Industry attractiveness is still unknown—use five-forces and pestle-analysis first
- You only need a demand number without an entry mode—use market-sizing
- The product is already live and the question is go-to-market execution—use go-to-market-strategy
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Defined target market boundary (geo, segment, use case)
- Market attractiveness and competitive structure evidence
- Internal capability and capital constraints for entry modes
- Regulatory, channel, and partnership landscape in the target market
- Risk appetite and time-to-revenue expectations from leadership

**Nice-to-have:** a localized customer proposition, target list, acquisition screen, trade restrictions, legal-entity requirements, and lessons from prior entries.

**When data is thin:** use ranges for market access and capability gaps, then identify the lowest-cost diligence that distinguishes the leading modes. Do not convert an attractive market into an automatic direct-launch recommendation.
## Procedure
1. **Specify the entry arena.** Fix the geography, customer segment, offer, time horizon, and strategic objective so options face the same question. Output: entry mandate.
2. **Assess access conditions.** Examine demand quality, incumbent advantage, regulations, route-to-market control, local economics, and likely competitive response. Output: barrier and attractiveness profile.
3. **Assess the firm’s right to win.** Compare brand, product localization, capital, operating capability, relationships, and management bandwidth against what the arena requires. Output: capability-gap view.
4. **Construct entry vehicles.** Define credible organic, distributor, license, joint venture, acquisition, or staged options, including required commitments and exit points. Output: mode alternatives.
5. **Choose and sequence.** Score each vehicle against value, speed, control, risk, investment, learning, and reversibility; set gates that release the next country, partner, or capital tranche. Output: recommendation and roadmap.
## Output Contract
Produce an entry decision paper stating the arena, evidence on attractiveness and barriers, firm capability gaps, evaluated vehicles, decision criteria, recommended mode, first-market sequence, investment envelope, milestones, owners, and stop conditions. It must explain why the chosen vehicle fits the market and company better than the alternatives, rather than simply ranking countries.
## Evidence
Separate verified access facts such as licensing requirements and channel concentration from management estimates such as partner productivity or share ramp. Make the required local adaptations and cost of control visible for each mode. Treat synergy with the home market as an assumption until operational dependencies have been tested. When conditions vary by country, avoid a regional average that conceals a binding local barrier.
## Checks
- The target arena is narrow enough for a concrete vehicle decision.
- Every option is feasible under the stated capital and management constraints.
- Criteria distinguish speed, control, and reversibility instead of collapsing them into one score.
- The recommendation includes a trigger for advancing, pausing, or exiting.
- Channel and regulatory constraints change the selected route where material.
## Failure Modes
- Comparing a detailed acquisition case with a vague “organic” alternative.
- Choosing a distributor for speed without rights to customer data or brand control.
- Treating market size as proof that the company can win access.
- Copying the home-market offer despite local regulation or buying behavior.
- Launching several countries before validating the first entry model.
