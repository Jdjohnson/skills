# Resistance Management Plan
## Purpose
Identify, prioritize, and mitigate sources of change resistance as a governed risk workstream across cohorts. The plan turns vague labels of “resistance” into specific drivers, timed interventions, and observable adoption signals.
## Use
- Identifying sources, intensity, and form of resistance before or during roll-out
- Building cohort-specific mitigation actions tied to incentives, skill, and influence
- Governing adoption risk as an explicit workstream rather than informal hope
## Avoid
- You still lack a person-level readiness diagnosis—use adkar-model or change-readiness-assessment first
- The core issue is unclear stakeholder power and interest, not mitigation tactics—use stakeholder-mapping
- Resistance is mainly a communication vacuum—start with communication-strategy-for-change
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Stakeholder map with influence, impact, and current stance
- Evidence of resistance forms (passive delay, active opposition, workarounds)
- Root drivers by cohort (fear, capability, incentive conflict, trust, history)
- Planned change timeline and high-friction moments
- Available levers: sponsorship, incentives, training, process redesign, negotiation

**Nice-to-have:** pulse-survey trends, union or works-council obligations, and prior change lessons.

**When data is thin:** distinguish suspected resistance from confirmed behavior, and fund discovery conversations before selecting an intervention.
## Procedure
1. Segment affected people into cohorts with similar work, loss exposure, and influence; do not use one enterprise-wide “resistance score.”
2. Gather signals from adoption data, interviews, manager observations, attendance, workarounds, and rumor patterns. Describe behavior without assigning motive prematurely.
3. Diagnose the driver for each material cohort: capability gap, workload, identity or status loss, incentive conflict, mistrust, process defect, or credible downside. Test competing explanations with the cohort.
4. Rate each resistance item by likelihood, adoption impact, urgency relative to the rollout calendar, and ability to influence it. Put active blockers and latent risks in the same register but label their evidence levels.
5. Design an intervention matched to the driver: sponsor dialogue for trust, workflow redesign for burden, manager coaching for capability, or incentive changes for conflicting rewards. Assign one owner, a date, a cost, and a leading indicator.
6. Review the register at key change moments, check whether the intended behavior moved, and retire or escalate actions based on evidence rather than attendance alone.
## Output Contract
Deliver a resistance register organized by cohort. Each entry must state the observed behavior, hypothesized or verified driver, intensity, rollout moment, mitigation action, owner, due date, required sponsor decision, and leading and lagging adoption measures. Include an escalation path for high-impact opposition and a weekly view of intervention status. This is a risk plan for adoption, not a generic message calendar or a one-time readiness score.
## Evidence
Use adoption telemetry, documented delays, turnover, and direct cohort feedback as observed evidence. Attribute motives only as inferences and preserve competing explanations when evidence conflicts. State assumptions about manager reach, training attendance, and available incentives. Protect anonymity where disclosure could worsen retaliation fears; aggregate sensitive findings before reporting them to sponsors.
## Checks
- Every high-impact cohort has a named driver, not merely a negative stance label.
- Actions target the driver and precede the relevant rollout moment.
- Owners have authority and budget to carry out the intervention.
- Measures track behavior change as well as communication or training completion.
- The plan differentiates a legitimate design concern from opposition to be overcome.
## Failure Modes
- Calling every objection resistance and losing valuable warnings about a faulty rollout.
- Sending more communications to solve an incentive or workload problem.
- Recording anonymous rumors as facts about a named group.
- Assigning mitigation to a sponsor who lacks contact with the affected cohort.
- Discovering a union, compliance, or manager bottleneck only at cutover.
