# Three Horizons of Growth
## Purpose
Allocate attention and resources across core, emerging, and future growth horizons so the firm performs now and builds options later. It creates differentiated portfolio rules by maturity and time to impact, rather than a business-unit capital ranking or product-market matrix.
## Use
- Balancing resources across core performance, emerging growth, and future options
- Structuring a growth agenda so H1 delivery does not crowd out H2/H3
- Aligning leadership on time-horizon-specific metrics and governance
## Avoid
- You need a single-product growth vector (market vs product newness)—use ansoff-matrix
- The task is capital reallocation across legal business units—use portfolio-strategy-analysis or bcg-matrix
- You are sequencing a detailed project portfolio with dependencies—use stage-gate-process or pmo-setup-and-governance
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Current initiatives and revenue streams tagged by maturity and cash profile; ambition for growth rate and future option value over a multi-year horizon; resource envelope (capital, talent, leadership attention); risk appetite for exploratory bets versus core performance commitments; metrics available for H1 delivery vs H2 scaling vs H3 learning.
## Procedure
1. **Define horizon meanings for this business.** Set the time ranges and behavioral tests for H1, H2, and H3. H1 sustains and extends the current engine; H2 scales emerging engines with evidence; H3 explores options whose market and model remain uncertain.
2. **Inventory and classify initiatives.** Place revenue streams and investments according to maturity, time to material impact, and uncertainty, not according to who sponsors them. Split a program that contains both core modernization and speculative experimentation.
3. **Expose imbalance.** Compare current allocation of capital, talent, leadership time, and metrics with the growth ambition. Identify where H1 funds everything, H2 lacks a scaling owner, or H3 has ideas but no protected learning budget.
4. **Set horizon-specific rules.** Give H1 delivery, margin, and reliability metrics; H2 adoption, unit economics, and scale gates; and H3 learning milestones, option value, and kill criteria. Design different governance cadence and funding mechanisms for each.
5. **Choose the rebalance.** Make explicit resource shifts, initiative entry and exit decisions, named sponsors, and review dates. Revisit classifications when an H3 experiment proves repeatable or an H2 business fails its scale gate.
## Output Contract
A balanced growth roadmap showing initiatives by horizon, allocation of capital, talent, and leadership attention, horizon-specific metrics and governance, key dependencies, and decisions to fund, scale, hold, or stop. It must protect future options without masking H1 accountability. A single return-sorted list does not fulfill the contract.
## Evidence
Use current revenue, investment, capacity, adoption, and performance records as evidence. Treat time-to-impact, market potential, and option value as assumptions with ranges and review triggers. Do not classify an initiative as H3 simply to shield a weak project from ordinary performance scrutiny. The horizon label should reflect the maturity of the business model, not the seniority of its sponsor or the budget code that funds it.
## Checks
- Every material initiative has a stated classification rationale and a decision owner.
- Allocation is measured in money, talent, and leadership attention, not just project count.
- H1, H2, and H3 have materially different success measures and funding rules.
- The target mix is consistent with growth ambition and risk appetite.
- H2 scale gates and H3 kill or learning milestones prevent permanent incubation.
## Failure Modes
- Calling all digital projects H2 even when some merely protect the core operation.
- Using one annual ROI hurdle that kills experimentation before it produces evidence.
- Keeping failed H2 ventures alive because their horizon label obscures missed scale milestones.
