# Strategy Map
## Purpose
Visually link strategic objectives in cause-and-effect chains so the organization shares one value-creation hypothesis. The map supplies the logic before scorecards and target cascades.
## Use
- Linking strategic objectives in cause-and-effect chains across financial, customer, process, and learning perspectives.
- Building or repairing the logic layer before a balanced scorecard or hoshin cascade.
- Aligning leadership on how value is supposed to be created before metric selection.
## Avoid
- You still lack a strategic choice and are only scanning the environment—use pestle-analysis, five-forces, or swot-analysis first.
- You need a detailed project plan and critical path, not objective causality—use critical-path-method or benefits-realization-management.
- You need product/customer value design, not enterprise objective linkage—use value-proposition-canvas.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Draft strategic objectives and intended outcomes; perspective structure (typically financial, customer, internal process, learning/growth); hypothesized cause-and-effect links among objectives; candidate measures and initiatives to hang on the map later.

**Useful additions:** strategic choices, customer research, operating constraints, initiative inventory, and performance data that challenge causal beliefs.
## Procedure
1. State the strategic destination and the economic or mission result that defines success. Reduce overlapping objective statements until each expresses an outcome or capability rather than a project.
2. Place financial or mission outcomes at the top, customer outcomes beneath them, the critical internal processes next, and learning or capability foundations at the bottom. Use another perspective structure only when it fits the organization’s logic.
3. Draw directional links from enabling capabilities through processes and customer value to top outcomes. For every arrow, write the mechanism: what changes, for whom, and why that change should create the next outcome.
4. Challenge the map with counterexamples and trade-offs. Remove arrows based only on wishful correlation, identify missing prerequisites, and decide where alternative paths compete for constrained resources.
5. Add candidate measures and initiatives only after the causal graph is stable. Confirm that every initiative supports a named objective and that leading objectives plausibly precede lagging results.
6. Obtain leadership agreement on the hypothesis, version the map, and define when evidence will cause an arrow or objective to be revised.
## Output Contract
Provide a one-page causal objective graph, an objective dictionary, arrow rationales, and a list of measures and initiatives attached to map nodes. The dictionary defines each objective, owner, intended beneficiary, leading or lagging role, and evidence needed to test it. The map must show how value is created, not become a decorated list of departmental goals.
## Evidence
Clearly mark each arrow as evidence-supported, tested locally, or strategic hypothesis. Do not treat a measure as proof of causation merely because it moves with an outcome. Retain contradictory data and alternative paths in the map notes. Keep objectives at a strategic level; project milestones belong in implementation plans. When a customer or financial outcome lacks evidence, commission research rather than inventing a link.
## Checks
- Each arrow has an explicit mechanism, not only an upward direction.
- Bottom-layer capabilities connect to a process, process to customer value, and customer value to top outcomes where the chosen structure applies.
- Objectives are outcomes or enduring capabilities rather than initiative names.
- The map contains no orphan objective without a causal role.
- Measures and initiatives are attached after, not substituted for, the causal hypothesis.
## Failure Modes
- Starting with available KPIs and drawing arrows afterward to rationalize them.
- Mixing “launch a CRM” with “increase retention” as if both are comparable objectives.
- Linking every objective to every other, which hides the few pathways leaders must test.
- Treating a visually tidy map as validated when no evidence or mechanism supports its central arrows.
