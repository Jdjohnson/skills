# 3C's Model
## Purpose
Frame competitive strategy as the simultaneous balance of Customer, Competitor, and Company factors that must fit together. The result is a focused strategic hypothesis, not a broad inventory of strengths and weaknesses.
## Use
- Framing a competitive strategy problem before deep analysis.
- Checking whether growth bets balance customer value, rival response, and company strength.
- Aligning leadership on the three forces that must fit for a strategy to work.
## Avoid
- You need industry structure attractiveness rather than firm strategy focus—use five-forces.
- You already have a clear frame and need a concrete positioning choice—use generic-strategies.
- The question is portfolio resource allocation across many units—use bcg-matrix or ge-mckinsey-matrix.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined customer segments and what they value; map of key competitors and their apparent strategic intent; honest inventory of company strengths, constraints, and cost position; current strategy thesis or growth hypothesis to stress-test.

**Nice-to-have:** Segment economics, win/loss interviews, competitor price observations, and capability benchmarks.

**When data is thin:** State each claim as a testable hypothesis and commission only the research needed to break the leading hypothesis.
## Procedure
1. **Name the strategic arena.** Fix the segment, geography, time horizon, and decision. A triangle for “all customers” is too vague to guide investment.
2. **Start with Customer.** Describe the jobs, buying criteria, switching triggers, and value trade-offs for each meaningful segment. Identify the segment where a distinctive choice could matter.
3. **Map Competitor responses.** For each credible rival, record its target customer, offer, cost position, channel advantage, and likely reaction to the proposed move. Separate evidence from sales folklore.
4. **Audit Company reality.** List capabilities, assets, constraints, and economics that make the offer believable or difficult to deliver. Test whether the firm can sustain the promised advantage.
5. **Draw the fit statement.** Write one sentence linking a chosen customer need, a competitor gap or vulnerability, and the company capability that closes it. Add explicit trade-offs: whom not to serve and what not to build.
6. **Stress-test alternatives.** Ask how the triangle changes if the main rival matches price, the segment changes priorities, or the capability takes twice as long to build. Select the thesis with the most resilient fit.
## Output Contract
A one-page strategic triangle containing the scoped arena, customer insight, competitor implications, company capability assessment, a concise fit statement, key trade-offs, and the two or three assumptions that could overturn it. It should identify the strategic logic for a subsequent choice, not pretend to be a market-sizing model or launch plan.
## Evidence
Customer interviews, usage data, competitor artifacts, and internal operating metrics are evidence. Claims about intent or reactions are inferences and must name their basis. Mark capability claims as assumptions when no delivery proof exists. Do not score the three corners mechanically; the useful test is whether all three support the same strategic move.
## Checks
- The customer corner names a segment and a concrete value trade-off.
- Competitors are treated as strategic actors, not a logo list.
- Company strengths are relevant to the chosen need and can be evidenced.
- The fit statement includes a choice and a trade-off.
- At least one plausible competitive response has been considered.
## Failure Modes
- Beginning with internal assets and retrofitting a customer need afterward.
- Calling every market participant a competitor while missing the customer’s alternative behavior.
- Mistaking a feature list for a capability that can be repeatedly delivered.
- Selecting an attractive segment without considering how incumbents will react.
- Leaving incompatible customer needs in one undifferentiated triangle.
