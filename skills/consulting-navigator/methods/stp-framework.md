# STP Framework
## Purpose
Segment the market, select priority targets, and define differentiated positioning that guides the marketing mix. It turns customer heterogeneity into explicit choices about whom to win and what promise to own.
## Use
- Market is heterogeneous and you must choose which segments to win and how to position.
- Marketing mix decisions require a clear target and differentiated promise.
- Portfolio or brand strategy is diluted by unfocused customer definitions.
## Avoid
- You only need descriptive personas without strategic choices—use customer-persona-development.
- Industry structure attractiveness is the open question—use five-forces first.
- You already have targets and need execution tactics—use 4ps-of-marketing or go-to-market-strategy.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Market definition and customer base evidence; segmentation variables and data or research; competitive offerings and current positioning claims; economics or attractiveness criteria for target selection.

**Useful additions:** segment-level lifetime economics, channel access, switching triggers, brand tracking, and constraints on capacity or product fit.
## Procedure
1. Define the market boundary and purchase occasion. State whether the unit is an individual, buying center, account, or use case; avoid combining incomparable markets.
2. Choose segmentation variables that change needs, response, economics, or access. Build segments from observed behavior, needs, value, or context rather than a demographic label that does not alter the decision.
3. Profile every segment: jobs, purchase criteria, current alternatives, size, growth, willingness to pay, reachable channels, and evidence confidence. Test whether members are internally similar enough to serve coherently.
4. Score segments against explicit attractiveness and ability-to-win criteria. Make trade-offs visible: a large segment may be unattractive if it is expensive to reach or the offer lacks a credible advantage.
5. Select primary and secondary targets, state whom the business will not pursue, and assign the role each target plays in the portfolio.
6. Draft a positioning statement for each priority target: target, frame of reference, distinctive benefit, reason to believe, and competitive contrast. Check that product, price, channel, and message can substantiate the promise.
## Output Contract
Produce a segmentation definition, segment profile table, target-selection scorecard, and positioning platform for chosen targets. The scorecard includes criteria, weights or decision logic, evidence confidence, and explicit non-targets. The positioning platform gives a promise and proof, not a slogan or an undifferentiated persona deck.
## Evidence
Separate measured customer behavior from research interpretation and commercial judgment. Do not infer a segment's profitability from revenue alone. Label a segment provisional when sample size, data coverage, or qualitative evidence is weak. Keep competitors' claimed position distinct from customers' perceived position. A positioning promise needs product, service, or proof assets; unsupported aspiration remains an assumption.
## Checks
- Segments differ on a variable that changes the offer or route to market.
- Segment sizes reconcile to the market definition without double counting.
- Target scores use both market attractiveness and ability to win.
- A named non-target prevents “everyone” from becoming the real strategy.
- Positioning is distinguishable from the nearest credible alternative and testable with buyers.
## Failure Modes
- Calling age bands segments when buying criteria and channel response are identical.
- Selecting the largest segment despite an offer that cannot meet its decisive requirement.
- Writing the same “quality and service” promise for every target.
- Allowing sales territories to substitute for customer segments and obscure shared needs.
