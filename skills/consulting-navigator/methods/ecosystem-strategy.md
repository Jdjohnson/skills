# Ecosystem Strategy
## Purpose
Design the firm’s role, openness, and governance to create and capture value in an interdependent multi-actor network. It resolves who coordinates the system, what others are invited to contribute, and which rules make participation worthwhile.
## Use
- Designing how the firm creates and captures value in a multi-actor interdependent network.
- Choosing platform/ecosystem roles, openness, and governance rules.
- Aligning partner incentives, APIs, and go-to-market when success depends on complements.
## Avoid
- The competitive problem is classic industry structure among independent rivals—use five-forces.
- You only need a bilateral alliance or JV structure without network orchestration—use cost-benefit-analysis and stakeholder-mapping.
- The need is internal operating model only with no external complementors—use value-chain-analysis or business-model-canvas alone.
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Map of actors (users, complementors, suppliers, regulators) and dependency links.
- Current value creation and capture points across the network.
- Technical and contractual interfaces (APIs, standards, commercial terms).
- Partner incentive and multi-homing patterns.
- Strategic role options under consideration (owner, keystone, complementor, niche).

**Nice-to-have:** adoption data, partner economics, interface uptime, switching costs, and competitor ecosystem maps.

**When data is thin:** test the dependency map with representative partners before promising governance or revenue terms; label untested assumptions about their willingness to join.
## Procedure
1. **Draw the system boundary.** Name the end-user outcome and map every actor required to deliver it, including regulators and bottleneck suppliers. Trace what each actor contributes, receives, and depends on. Output: ecosystem map.
2. **Locate control points.** Identify scarce assets, trusted standards, customer access, data, or workflows that determine bargaining power. Separate ownership of an interface from actual ability to set its rules. Output: control-point analysis.
3. **Choose the focal role.** Compare owner, keystone, complementor, and specialist positions against desired economics, required investment, legitimacy, and risk. Do not assume platform ownership is attractive merely because it sounds strategic. Output: role decision.
4. **Design participation mechanics.** Specify admission criteria, API or data access, certification, revenue sharing, pricing, dispute handling, and how partners can signal quality. Model the consequences of openness and multi-homing. Output: partner rulebook.
5. **Sequence the launch.** Select an initial use case where both sides receive a clear benefit, recruit anchor participants, and set minimum liquidity or quality thresholds. Output: phased activation plan.
6. **Govern adaptation.** Assign who changes rules, reviews concentration and abuse, and measures network health. Output: governance charter and scorecard.
## Output Contract
An ecosystem positioning and governance model containing the actor map, focal role, value flows, critical interfaces, participation rules, partner economics, launch sequence, and decision rights. It must explain how the network creates more value together and how the firm captures a fair share without starving complements.
## Evidence
Record observed transaction volumes, signed terms, technical constraints, and partner interviews separately from inferred incentives. Treat forecasts of partner take-up and network effects as assumptions with explicit thresholds. If the map contains indirect actors, mark whether their incentives were validated; a missing regulator or standards body can overturn an otherwise elegant design.
## Checks
- Every promised customer outcome has a complete chain of contributing actors.
- The selected role matches the firm’s actual control points and investment capacity.
- Access, pricing, quality, and dispute rules are specific enough for a partner to evaluate.
- Multi-homing and disintermediation risks have a response.
- Launch metrics distinguish partner recruitment from active, useful participation.
## Failure Modes
- Declaring a platform while no participant needs the proposed intermediary.
- Opening an API with no commercial, quality, or data-use rules.
- Treating all partners alike when one bottleneck provider holds the system hostage.
- Subsidizing one side indefinitely without a path to reciprocal value.
- Copying a competitor’s governance model despite different control points.
