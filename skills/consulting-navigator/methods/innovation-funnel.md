# Innovation Funnel
## Purpose
Govern the flow of ideas from intake to commercialization with stage filters that progressively concentrate funding on the strongest concepts. It makes idea throughput, learning, and kill decisions visible before concepts become expensive projects.
## Use
- Managing a multi-stage pipeline from raw idea to commercial launch.
- Applying progressive investment and kill criteria as uncertainty falls.
- Giving leadership visibility into innovation throughput and bottlenecks.
## Avoid
- You need formal gated project governance with charters and phase reviews—use stage-gate-process.
- You are assessing organizational capability, not managing active ideas—use innovation-maturity-assessment.
- You only have one idea to test quickly—use lean-startup or design-sprints.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Incoming idea or concept inventory with owners; Defined funnel stages and entry/exit criteria; Resource or funding envelope by stage; Evidence requirements and kill/continue decision rights.

**Useful additions:** portfolio themes, historical conversion rates, experiment cost, customer evidence, and a panel of technical and commercial reviewers.
## Procedure
1. Set the funnel's strategic boundary: which customer problems, technology domains, and time horizon qualify for intake. Give every submitted idea an owner and a minimum problem statement.
2. Define a small sequence of evidence-building stages, such as screen, concept, feasibility, business case, and development handoff. For each stage, state the uncertainty to reduce rather than merely adding a calendar label.
3. Establish entry criteria, exit evidence, resource cap, and decision right for each filter. Early stages should be cheap and broad; later stages should demand stronger customer, technical, and economic proof.
4. Triage the inventory against strategic fit, novelty, evidence quality, and next-test cost. Merge duplicates, park out-of-scope ideas, and record why ideas are killed so the organization can learn.
5. Allocate funding in tranches to the concepts whose next experiment has the best learning value. Re-rank at each review as evidence changes rather than protecting the original sponsor's idea.
6. Monitor volume, conversion, age, investment, and bottleneck by stage. Escalate concepts that wait longer than their learning cycle or consume development resources without crossing a threshold.
## Output Contract
Provide a live idea-pipeline view with stage definitions, entry and exit criteria, owner, evidence status, funding tranche, decision history, and portfolio throughput metrics. It must show which concepts advance, pause, pivot, or stop and why. This output manages the conversion of ideas; it is not a project charter sequence or a high-level horizon map.
## Evidence
Separate customer observations, prototype results, technical tests, and market facts from sponsor assertions. Record the confidence and date of each evidence item because old validation can expire as markets change. A panel may use judgment at intake, but later advancement must meet pre-agreed evidence thresholds. Do not mistake the number of submitted ideas for innovation health if few ever produce validated learning.
## Checks
- Each stage removes a different uncertainty and has a measurable exit test.
- Funding increases only when the required evidence has improved.
- Killed and parked ideas retain a decision record and reusable insight.
- Throughput includes conversion and time-in-stage, not just idea count.
- Reviewers have authority to stop favored concepts and are not all their sponsors.
## Failure Modes
- Letting an executive-sponsored idea bypass early customer validation.
- Treating each stage as an approval meeting while no new evidence is required.
- Keeping weak concepts alive because prior spending is mistaken for proof.
- Measuring a large intake backlog as success while concepts wait six months for a review.
