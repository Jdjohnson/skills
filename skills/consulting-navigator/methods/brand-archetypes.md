# Brand Archetypes
## Purpose
Select and specify a brand character archetype so messaging, creative, and experience choices stay consistent and distinctive. It converts audience emotion and category conventions into a usable character platform, not a score of present brand strength.
## Use
- Choosing a durable brand personality to guide creative and messaging systems.
- Differentiating a brand when functional claims are commoditized.
- Aligning agencies, product, and leadership on one character platform.
## Avoid
- You need to measure brand strength or equity drivers—use brand-equity-model.
- Positioning is still undefined on target, category, and benefit—use stp-framework or value-proposition-canvas first.
- The need is a full go-to-market plan, not identity—use go-to-market-strategy.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Target audience insight and emotional jobs; category competitor brand personalities and claims; current brand assets, voice guidelines, and campaign history; business strategy and desired brand role (leader, challenger, niche).

**Nice-to-have:** qualitative interviews, semiotic audit, social-language analysis, creative testing, and employee perceptions.

**When data is thin:** form two or three candidate characters and validate them with the target audience; do not select the founder's favorite personality by workshop vote.
## Procedure
1. **Clarify the brand role.** Define the audience, category tension, emotional promise, and behavior the brand must invite.
2. **Audit current signals.** Inventory name, visual assets, voice, product behavior, service moments, and campaigns; identify contradictions between claimed and experienced personality.
3. **Map category territory.** Classify competitors' dominant characters and note crowded emotional codes, taboos, and white space.
4. **Generate archetype hypotheses.** Compare candidates against audience relationship, business role, cultural credibility, and ability to endure beyond one campaign.
5. **Specify expression rules.** Define the primary archetype, permitted secondary traits, voice, imagery, narrative roles, service behaviors, and do-not-do boundaries.
6. **Prototype and govern.** Test representative copy, visual work, and experience choices; appoint a steward to review new work against the character rules.
## Output Contract
An identity character framework containing the selected archetype, audience tension, competitive contrast, emotional promise, primary and secondary traits, verbal and visual cues, behavior examples, exclusions, and approval criteria. It must help a writer, designer, and service owner make consistent choices without guessing what the personality means.
## Evidence
Treat customer language, asset audits, and observed competitor communication as evidence. Mark cultural interpretation and desired emotional response as hypotheses. Do not infer an identity need from demographics alone. Keep a record of claims that cannot be expressed credibly through product or service experience.
## Checks
- The character supports the chosen target and positioning rather than replacing them.
- It differs clearly from the closest category personalities.
- Voice and visual rules include examples of what to avoid.
- The selected character can survive product, service, and employer touchpoints.
- Test participants recognize a consistent intended feeling without being told the label.
## Failure Modes
- Choosing the Hero because leadership likes bold language while the service behaves like a cautious expert.
- Combining so many characters that the resulting voice has no recognizable point of view.
- Copying an archetype's clichés instead of expressing a credible version in the category.
- Treating an ad campaign theme as a durable identity decision.
- Letting agencies reinterpret the character because no behavioral guardrails exist.
