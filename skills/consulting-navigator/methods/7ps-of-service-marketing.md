# 7Ps of Service Marketing
## Purpose
Design a service marketing system by extending the mix to people, process, and physical evidence alongside product, price, place, and promotion. It tests whether the operational experience can keep the commercial promise.
## Use
- Designing or fixing a service (or hybrid product-service) marketing system.
- Aligning commercial promises with frontline people, process, and environment.
- Diagnosing service failures that pure product-price-place-promotion analysis misses.
## Avoid
- The business is pure goods with negligible service delivery—use 4ps-of-marketing.
- You need a detailed end-to-end process map rather than mix design—use service-blueprinting or customer-journey-mapping.
- The problem is purely internal ops efficiency with no customer-facing mix—use value-stream-mapping.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Service offer definition and customer promise; current 4P baseline (product, price, place, promotion); roles and behaviors of customer-facing people; key service processes and failure modes; physical evidence cues (facilities, digital UI, materials, uniforms).

**Nice-to-have:** Mystery-shop findings, complaints, abandonment data, training materials, and location-level performance.

**When data is thin:** Walk the service as a customer and label each 7P decision as observed, inferred, or needing validation.
## Procedure
1. **State the promise and service moment.** Name the target customer, promised outcome, channel, and critical moment of truth. This anchors both the familiar four commercial levers and the three delivery levers.
2. **Review Product, Price, Place, and Promotion.** Specify what is being sold, what the price signals, where access occurs, and what promotion promises. Note every promise that operations must fulfill.
3. **Design People.** Define frontline roles, recruiting profile, service behaviors, authority limits, incentives, and escalation support needed to make the promise credible.
4. **Design Process.** Map the customer-facing steps at a practical level: arrival, request, handoff, recovery, and completion. Set service standards and failure recovery rules.
5. **Design Physical Evidence.** Choose tangible and digital cues—booking interface, signage, receipts, waiting environment, uniforms, confirmation messages—that help customers judge an intangible service.
6. **Test the seven-way fit.** Run scenarios for normal demand, peak demand, and a service failure. Correct mismatches such as premium advertising with a queueing process that feels dismissive.
## Output Contract
A service-mix design containing the customer promise, decision for each of the seven Ps, moments of truth, service standards, owner, key experience risks, and a short test plan. It should show how marketing claims translate into frontline behavior, process, and cues, rather than becoming a detailed process blueprint or a campaign calendar.
## Evidence
Use observed wait times, customer feedback, abandonment, training completion, and site audits as evidence. Treat interpretation of customer feelings and expected staff behavior as inference. Flag assumptions about staffing capacity, compliance, and peak demand. A polished environment is not proof of a reliable process; retain separate evidence for each.
## Checks
- Each advertised promise has a named people, process, and evidence implication.
- Frontline staff have authority and tools consistent with the promised recovery experience.
- Process standards include both normal and exception paths.
- Physical cues are consistent across digital and physical locations.
- Price and promotion do not stimulate demand beyond feasible service capacity.
## Failure Modes
- Marketing a warm, expert service while giving agents a script and no discretion.
- Treating staff training as the only answer to a broken handoff process.
- Renovating a lobby while leaving booking and complaint recovery confusing.
- Copying goods-oriented discount tactics that undermine trust in an expert service.
- Measuring average wait time while ignoring the worst handoff customers remember.
