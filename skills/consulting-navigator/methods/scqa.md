# SCQA
## Purpose
Frame a problem narrative as Situation, Complication, Question, then Answer so the audience shares context, urgency, and the decision question. Keeping the question explicit prevents a team from presenting a solution before it has agreed what must be decided.
## Use
- Opening a problem or recommendation with shared context, rising tension, the key question, then the answer
- Aligning a client and team on the single decision question before deep analysis
- Storyboarding an engagement kickoff or final presentation narrative
## Avoid
- The answer is already agreed and you only need hierarchical argument support—use pyramid-principle
- You want a shorter three-beat executive summary without an explicit question beat—use scr
- The work is still pure decomposition of an undefined problem space—use issue-tree first
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Stable facts the audience already accepts (situation); the change or pressure that creates urgency (complication); the decision or governing question the work will answer; a provisional or final answer once analysis allows.

**Nice-to-have:** audience concerns, decision deadline, supporting evidence, and alternative answers that were considered.

**When data is thin:** write only facts the audience can validate, make the uncertainty part of the Question, and avoid manufacturing urgency from untested claims.
## Procedure
1. **Collect the shared baseline.** List facts the intended audience already accepts: current performance, mandate, customer condition, or prior decision. Select only the facts necessary to orient the story.
2. **Name the destabilizing change.** State what has changed, failed, or become constrained and why it matters now. The complication must create a genuine tension with the situation, not simply repeat a negative fact.
3. **Formulate the governing question.** Convert the tension into one answerable decision question with a scope, criterion, and time horizon. Test whether two executives would investigate the same thing after reading it.
4. **Draft the answer at the right maturity.** State a recommendation when evidence is sufficient, or a provisional hypothesis and the analysis required when it is not. Do not hide a contested choice in vague action language.
5. **Test the story arc.** Read the four beats aloud to a representative stakeholder. Remove facts that do not advance the question and check that the answer directly resolves it.
## Output Contract
Create a four-part story spine containing a concise Situation, consequential Complication, explicit Question, and Answer labeled final or provisional. Include the audience, decision deadline, key evidence, and open analyses if unresolved. Keep it short enough to guide a memo or discussion without becoming the supporting argument.
## Evidence
Situation statements need audience-accepted evidence. Complications can combine observed facts with a stated inference, but the causal link must be visible. Label a proposed answer as a hypothesis until the required proof exists. Keep known disagreements in the Question or a note beside it; consensus cannot be created by writing as though it already exists.
## Checks
- The situation is neutral baseline, not a disguised recommendation.
- The complication changes the stakes or makes the prior approach inadequate.
- The question is singular, decision-oriented, and answerable in the named scope.
- The answer responds directly to the question and declares its confidence.
- A reader can distinguish this four-beat frame from the three-beat SCR opener.
## Failure Modes
- Calling every background fact a complication until the decision disappears.
- Writing “How can we improve?” instead of a bounded choice question.
- Smuggling the desired answer into the situation before evidence is reviewed.
- Leaving the Question implicit because the sponsor thinks it is obvious.
- Turning the Answer into a detailed evidence tree that belongs in a later argument.
