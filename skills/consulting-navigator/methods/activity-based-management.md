# Activity-Based Management
## Purpose
Convert activity cost and driver data into decisions that eliminate, reduce, or redesign work to improve profit and customer value. The focus is changing the work and its demand, rather than merely calculating a more accurate cost.
## Use
- Turning activity-based cost data into decisions that improve profit or customer value
- Identifying which activities to eliminate, reduce, share, or redesign
- Linking operational improvement priorities to true cost-to-serve economics
## Avoid
- You still lack reliable activity costs—build activity-based-costing first
- The need is a one-time capital project appraisal—use cost-benefit-analysis or npv-and-irr-analysis
- You want industry structure or positioning insight—use five-forces or value-chain-analysis
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Activity cost model with cost drivers (from ABC or equivalent); process or product/customer volumes linked to activities; definition of value-added vs non-value-added for the process set; profit or margin targets by product, customer, or channel.

**Nice-to-have:** Process-cycle time, defect and rework records, customer service commitments, and an estimate of change cost.

**When data is thin:** Start with the highest-cost activities and use a bounded pilot; do not claim savings until the driver volume, capacity release, or spend removal has been observed.
## Procedure
1. **Select the activity domain.** Choose a process or service chain where driver-level cost is material and name the margin, service, or capacity outcome to improve.
2. **Read the activity economics.** Plot cost by activity, volume, rate, and consuming product or customer. Separate costly work caused by demand from costly work caused by defects, handoffs, or policy.
3. **Classify the work.** With process owners, mark each activity as customer-value-adding, required but non-value-adding, or avoidable. A compliance check may be required even if a customer would not pay for it.
4. **Generate activity treatments.** For each priority activity, test elimination, demand reduction, simplification, automation, sharing, outsourcing, or a changed service tier. State how the treatment changes the driver and who loses or gains work.
5. **Build the economics of each treatment.** Calculate recurring benefit from reduced driver demand or released capacity, one-time implementation cost, customer impact, and constraints. Distinguish a lower rate from a real cashable saving.
6. **Commit an action portfolio.** Rank treatments by value, feasibility, and risk; assign an owner, date, driver target, and review cadence.
## Output Contract
Provide an activity-level action plan: baseline cost and driver, value classification, proposed treatment, customer or control consequence, expected driver change, benefit type, implementation cost, owner, and milestone. It must show why a particular activity is a redesign target and how the organization will verify the outcome, not simply repeat an activity cost map.
## Evidence
Use the cost model and transaction history as observed evidence. Mark claims about customer value, workaround behavior, and capacity redeployment as assumptions unless verified with customers or managers. Savings are only realized when a removed activity releases a budget line, avoids a future hire, or increases constrained capacity; otherwise label them capacity gains.
## Checks
- Every recommended treatment names the activity and driver it changes.
- Value-added labels were tested with the customer promise and regulatory obligations.
- Benefit calculations avoid double counting a driver reduction across teams.
- The plan distinguishes cost avoidance, capacity release, and cash savings.
- Owners can see the weekly or monthly measure that proves progress.
## Failure Modes
- Cutting service activities that protect profitable customers because their cost is visible while their retention value is not.
- Declaring savings after moving an approval task to another department.
- Automating a broken exception process and increasing demand for it.
- Treating all non-value-added work as removable despite controls, contracts, or safety requirements.
