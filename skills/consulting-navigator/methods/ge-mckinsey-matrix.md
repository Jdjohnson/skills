# GE-McKinsey Matrix
## Purpose
Prioritize business-unit investment by scoring industry attractiveness against competitive strength on a nine-box grid. It supports corporate resource allocation when growth and market share alone cannot capture the economics, structure, and capabilities of diverse businesses.
## Use
- Prioritizing investment across business units with multi-factor industry and strength scores.
- Moving beyond simple growth-share placement when drivers are multi-dimensional.
- Building a defensible corporate portfolio investment thesis.
## Avoid
- You only have growth and share data and need a fast first cut—use bcg-matrix.
- A single business needs competitive positioning design—use generic-strategies.
- Scoring factors would be pure politics without evidence—use competitive-benchmarking and market-sizing first.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Business units with defined markets; weighted industry attractiveness factors (e.g., growth, margins, structure); weighted competitive strength factors (e.g., share, brand, cost, capability); evidence to score each unit on those factors; investment constraints and corporate objectives.

**Unit rule:** score each business unit in one coherent market; aggregates spanning unrelated markets are invalid.
## Procedure
1. Set the portfolio decision, planning horizon, available capital, and business-unit definitions. Clarify whether the purpose is annual budget allocation, acquisition screening, turnaround triage, or divestiture review.
2. Choose a short list of industry-attractiveness factors, such as market growth, profit pool, cyclicality, barriers, regulation, and competitive intensity. Assign weights that total 100% and record why each factor matters to the parent.
3. Choose separate competitive-strength factors, such as relative share, cost position, brand, route-to-market, technology, and management capability. Again weight them and establish an evidence-based scoring rubric, for example 1 through 5.
4. Score every unit against each factor using market data and internal evidence. Calculate weighted attractiveness and strength scores; keep the factor sheets so leaders can challenge a score rather than only the resulting box.
5. Plot units by the two weighted scores on the nine-box grid and size bubbles by revenue, invested capital, or another declared scale. Interpret broad zones as invest/grow, selective/manage for earnings, or harvest/divest, without treating a boundary line as automatic.
6. Test factor weights and strategic scenarios. Resolve units that move zones under plausible changes, then make specific capital, capability, partnership, or exit choices subject to corporate constraints.
## Output Contract
Provide the nine-box chart, factor weights, evidence-backed scorecards, sensitivity results, bubble metric, and investment agenda by unit. The agenda states capital range, intent, conditions, and review date. A colored grid without scoring evidence is insufficient.
## Evidence
Market size, margins, share, and internal capability evidence are observed or sourced inputs. Weighted scores and relative strength judgments are inferred from the rubric. Factor weights, planning horizon, bubble metric, and corporate return thresholds are assumptions approved by the portfolio owner. Treat uncertain markets with score ranges or scenarios, not a falsely exact 3.4.
## Checks
- Attractiveness and strength factors are not duplicates of one another.
- Weights sum to 100% on each axis and are applied consistently to all units.
- A unit’s market definition matches the data used for its score.
- Bubble size is visible and does not imply investment priority by itself.
- Zone recommendations account for cash, interdependencies, and parent advantage before funds are assigned.
## Failure Modes
- Giving a large legacy unit high attractiveness solely because it generates current cash.
- Scoring competitive strength from internal confidence without a rival comparison.
- Moving the grid boundaries until a preselected business lands in “invest.”
- Treating a harvest zone as an instruction to starve a unit that must maintain customers or regulatory obligations.
