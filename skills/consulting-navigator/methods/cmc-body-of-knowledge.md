# CMC Body of Knowledge
## Purpose
Assess consultant and firm practice against the essential professional competencies defined in the CMC body of knowledge. It uses a common profession-wide reference to expose capability and practice gaps without substituting a firm’s unique role architecture.
## Use
- Defining or auditing the essential competencies for professional management consultants
- Building firm training, certification, or quality standards against an open consulting BOK
- Evaluating engagement practice against recognized professional expectations
## Avoid
- You need a project-management process standard rather than consulting competencies—use pmbok-framework or prince2
- The task is role design for non-consulting jobs—use competency-mapping or job-analysis-and-evaluation
- You only need a single engagement retrospective—use post-implementation-review or client-satisfaction-surveying
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Target population (firm, practice, or individual consultants); scope of competencies to assess (ethics, client engagement, analysis, change, commercial); evidence sources (work samples, reviews, certifications, observed behaviors); intended use (hiring bar, development plan, quality audit, or firm standard).

**Nice-to-have:** existing competency framework, professional-development records, engagement-quality data, and assessor calibration samples.

**When data is thin:** assess a limited set of observable competencies and return an evidence-collection plan; do not declare broad professional competence from a résumé alone.
## Procedure
1. **Set the assessment use and population.** Decide whether the result will govern hiring, certification, development, or a quality audit. Define the consultant level, practice, geography, and period so evidence is comparable.
2. **Select the relevant knowledge domains.** Translate the common body of knowledge into an assessment matrix spanning professional conduct, client and organization understanding, management processes, consulting skills, and the business of consulting. Mark domains out of scope with reasons.
3. **Define observable indicators.** For each selected domain, specify what a reviewer can see in a proposal, work product, client interaction, decision log, or observed behavior. Establish performance anchors before sampling evidence.
4. **Collect and calibrate evidence.** Sample work across engagements, anonymize where needed, and have assessors score common cases to reconcile differing interpretations. Separate a missing artifact from demonstrated weak practice.
5. **Decide actions at the right level.** Report individual gaps confidentially, aggregate firm patterns, and assign responses such as standards, coaching, certification evidence, or quality-review changes. Reassess after the response has had time to operate.
## Output Contract
Produce a competency assessment that shows scope, selected knowledge domains, observable indicators, evidence sample, scoring anchors, assessor calibration, gaps, confidence, and development or governance actions. It must distinguish a gap against professional consulting practice from a gap against a particular firm’s commercial preferences.
## Evidence
Observed evidence includes dated work samples, review records, certifications, and directly observed client practice. Ratings from a single assessor, self-reports, and inferred intent need labels. Record sampling bias: an assessor cannot generalize from only showcase engagements. Keep individual results confidential and aggregate reporting at a level that cannot expose sensitive performance records. When a standard is interpreted locally, document the interpretation before scoring.
## Checks
- Each score is tied to an observable indicator and evidence item.
- Assessors calibrate on shared examples before scoring independently.
- The selected knowledge domains match the intended decision.
- Professional ethics and client responsibility are assessed as practice, not as a box-ticking exercise.
- Firm-wide conclusions distinguish common patterns from isolated cases.
## Failure Modes
- Copying a competency list into an appraisal form without defining observable consulting behavior.
- Treating a prestigious certification as proof of current client-delivery capability.
- Combining senior-partner proposals and junior-consultant analyses in one uncalibrated score.
- Using the assessment to rank people publicly when its stated purpose is development.
