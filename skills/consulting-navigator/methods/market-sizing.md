# Market Sizing
## Purpose
Estimate total, serviceable, and obtainable market revenue with transparent top-down and bottom-up logic.
## Use
- Estimating revenue potential before entry, investment, or product bet
- Building a defensible TAM/SAM/SOM for board or investor materials
- Comparing addressable opportunity across segments or geographies
## Avoid
- You need industry structure and power dynamics, not a revenue envelope—use five-forces
- The question is how to enter, not how large—use market-entry-strategy
- You only have anecdotes and no secondary or operational data path—use secondary-research first
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Clear market definition and unit of demand (customers, seats, volume)
- Top-down industry or category spend sources where available
- Bottom-up drivers (accounts, usage, price, conversion assumptions)
- Company-specific filters for serviceable and obtainable share
- Time horizon and currency/ baselining conventions

**Nice-to-have:** customer interviews, competitor revenue clues, installed-base data, procurement records, unit economics, exchange-rate assumptions, and historical category growth.

**When data is thin:** construct a driver tree with low, base, and high cases. State the denominator and unit at every branch; an unsupported TAM should remain a range, not acquire extra decimal places.
## Procedure
1. **Draw the market boundary.** Define customer, need, geography, product scope, time period, currency, and revenue basis. Output: sizing charter.
2. **Build the top-down path.** Start from a credible broad spend pool and apply explicit filters for the defined segment, geography, and relevant category. Output: top-down estimate.
3. **Build the bottom-up path.** Multiply the number of eligible buying units by penetration, usage, and price, or another transparent demand equation. Output: bottom-up estimate.
4. **Reconcile the two paths.** Compare their definitions, years, inclusion rules, and drivers; investigate material gaps rather than averaging incompatible values. Output: reconciled market range.
5. **Apply serviceability and obtainability filters.** Remove customers beyond the company’s offer, route-to-market, capability, or time-horizon reach, then model a credible adoption path. Output: TAM, SAM, and SOM model.
## Output Contract
Deliver a market model that displays the market definition, units, base year, top-down bridge, bottom-up driver tree, reconciliation narrative, TAM, SAM, SOM, assumptions, sources of uncertainty, and sensitivity cases. Each headline number must trace to a formula or a stated filter. The artifact informs an investment decision; it is not an industry-attractiveness assessment or an entry-mode choice.
## Evidence
Classify published spend and internal customer counts as observed inputs, adjusted category shares as inferred, and penetration, price, and future adoption as assumptions. Do not mix revenue, shipment volume, and number of companies without converting units. Normalize currencies and calendar periods before comparison. When two independent paths diverge materially, retain the range and explain the hypothesis causing the gap rather than selecting the more convenient result.
## Checks
- The definition excludes adjacent markets unless they are separately shown.
- Every filter has a numerator, denominator, or transparent rationale.
- Top-down and bottom-up estimates use compatible time, geography, and revenue concepts.
- SAM reflects actual product and channel capability, not a wish list.
- SOM is constrained by a plausible ramp and capacity, not an arbitrary percentage of TAM.
## Failure Modes
- Naming a global category total as TAM for a narrow product without filters.
- Counting the same spend twice through overlapping segments.
- Treating a research publisher’s label as identical to the client’s market definition.
- Applying a convenient 1% share to produce SOM without sales capacity or adoption logic.
- Hiding an order-of-magnitude reconciliation gap behind a single midpoint.
