# Integrated Business Planning
## Purpose
Govern a rolling integrated plan that reconciles demand, supply, product, and financial outcomes under one executive cadence. The plan makes trade-offs visible in dollars as well as units, so leaders decide rather than allow separate functional forecasts to coexist.
## Use
- Extending S&OP into a monthly integrated plan linking volume, value, and strategy
- Aligning commercial, supply, product, and finance on one rolling decision cadence
- Governing scenario trade-offs that affect both capacity and financial outcomes
## Avoid
- You only need a demand number or forecast process—use demand-planning-and-forecasting
- Volume balancing without financial integration is enough—use sales-and-operations-planning
- The need is pure project portfolio governance outside the operating plan—use pmo-setup-and-governance
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Demand and supply plans with capacity and inventory positions; Financials (revenue, margin, cost) mapped to planning hierarchies; Strategic initiatives and new-product pipeline with timing and resource needs; Decision rights, calendar, and escalation rules for the IBP cycle.

**Nice-to-have:** Prior plan-versus-actual bridges, commercial assumptions by customer segment, and a constrained-capacity model.

**When data is thin:** Reconcile at the product family level first, retain an explicit “unmapped” line, and defer capital commitments until owners validate the mapping.
## Procedure
1. **Set the planning spine.** Agree the rolling horizon, monthly cut-off, product and geographic hierarchy, financial calendar, and decisions reserved for the executive review. Publish one baseline version.
2. **Review demand and product changes.** Commercial teams revise demand, price, mix, launches, and phase-outs. Separate an approved change from an upside hypothesis; translate both into units, revenue, and gross margin.
3. **Reconcile supply and inventory.** Operations tests feasible output, supplier limits, labor, capacity, and inventory against the demand view. Identify the exact periods and families where the plan cannot be met.
4. **Build integrated scenarios.** Finance converts each feasible response—overtime, allocation, price action, delayed launch, or capital spend—into P&L, cash, and working-capital effects. Keep a common assumption set across scenarios.
5. **Run the executive decision meeting.** Present unresolved trade-offs with a recommended decision, owner, trigger, and latest decision date. Record decisions in the plan rather than in meeting minutes alone.
6. **Close the loop.** Track decision execution and explain variance from the prior approved plan. Escalate only exceptions beyond agreed tolerance, then refresh the next rolling horizon.
## Output Contract
A monthly integrated plan containing the approved demand, supply, inventory, initiative, revenue, margin, cash, and capacity view; a scenario comparison; and a decision log with owners and dates. It must show how a unit change reaches financial outcomes and distinguish the approved plan from contingencies.
## Evidence
Label booked orders, capacity records, and actual costs as observed. Treat forecast conversions, yield changes, and price realization as assumptions with an accountable owner. Calculate financial effects from documented mappings, not a separately maintained spreadsheet. Where an initiative has no timing or resource estimate, show it outside the committed plan rather than silently absorbing it.
## Checks
- One hierarchy reconciles volume and value; totals tie to the finance baseline.
- Each material gap has a decision owner, deadline, and financial consequence.
- Scenario differences trace to named assumptions rather than changed formulas.
- The meeting reviews future decision windows, not only last month’s variance.
- Inventory and capacity actions retain their effects in later months.
## Failure Modes
- Treating the finance view as a late presentation layer after volume choices are fixed.
- Allowing sales, supply, and finance to maintain competing “official” forecasts.
- Approving a launch without its ramp, working-capital, and capacity consequences.
- Escalating every variance instead of using defined decision thresholds.
