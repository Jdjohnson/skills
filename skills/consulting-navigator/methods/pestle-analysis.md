# PESTLE Analysis
## Purpose
Diagnose the macro-environment by systematically scoring political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental forces. The result is an external-force map tied to a strategic question; it does not diagnose industry rivalry or combine external observations with the firm’s internal capabilities.
## Use
- Scanning political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental drivers before strategy choices
- Comparing macro contexts across markets or time horizons
- Surfacing external risks and opportunities that industry structure alone misses
## Avoid
- You need industry rivalry and power structure, not macro factors—use five-forces
- You need firm-internal strengths and weaknesses integrated with external factors—use swot-analysis after the scan
- You need quantified scenarios or option values—use scenario-planning or real-options-analysis
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined geography, industry, and time horizon for the scan; evidence or expert input across political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental factors; prior strategy or investment question the scan must inform.

**Nice-to-have:** regulatory calendars, macro forecasts, demographic data, competitor announcements, and local expert interviews.

**When data is thin:** keep the factor register short, state which category lacks evidence, and commission targeted research before making an irreversible market commitment.
## Procedure
1. **Frame the decision boundary.** Name the geography, customer arena, planning horizon, and decision that the scan will influence. Separate home-market conditions from export-market conditions. Output: scan charter.
2. **Populate six factor lenses.** Gather distinct signals under political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental headings. Record the event or trend, affected mechanism, source, and expected timing. Output: factor register.
3. **Translate factors into exposure.** For each credible factor, describe the pathway to demand, costs, access, operating permissions, or reputation. Avoid listing headlines without a business mechanism. Output: exposure statements.
4. **Score materiality and uncertainty.** Rate magnitude, likelihood or confidence, time-to-effect, and reversibility. Cluster linked factors so an election, regulation, and subsidy are not counted as three independent shocks. Output: prioritized force map.
5. **Derive strategic implications.** Identify no-regret moves, watch indicators, and questions that need scenario modelling or market analysis. Output: decision brief with monitoring triggers.
## Output Contract
A six-lens register and visual map that state scope, each material force, evidence source, causal pathway, timing, materiality, uncertainty, and implication for the named decision. It must distinguish a change in the broad environment from a competitor’s maneuver and leave a traceable basis for every prioritization.
## Evidence
Use enacted laws, official statistics, and dated announcements as observed evidence. Treat forecasts, expert interpretations, and likely behavioral responses as inferences. Mark assumed time horizons and geographic spillovers explicitly. If a factor could affect the decision but cannot be sized, retain it as a watch item rather than assigning a spurious numerical score. Do not present advocacy, press speculation, or one executive’s view as independent corroboration.
## Checks
- All six lenses are considered, including a documented “no material factor found” where appropriate.
- Each high-priority item has a mechanism, not merely a trend label.
- Time horizons distinguish immediate compliance changes from structural shifts.
- Scores reflect both impact and uncertainty; certainty is not mistaken for importance.
- The final implications answer the original decision instead of becoming a general news digest.
## Failure Modes
- Filing the same regulation under political, legal, and environmental headings without linking the entries.
- Calling a competitor price cut an economic force rather than an industry action.
- Treating technology adoption as inevitable without considering infrastructure or regulation.
- Ignoring social acceptance when a technically feasible product enters a new market.
- Producing a long catalogue that offers no priority or monitoring trigger.
