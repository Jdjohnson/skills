# Adjacency Expansion
## Purpose
Generate and prioritize related new-market options that extend the firm’s core customers, capabilities, or economics into adjacent spaces. It asks whether a candidate is close enough to reuse an advantage while attractive enough to warrant investment.
## Use
- Generating and ranking growth options that extend from the current core into related customer, product, channel, or geographic spaces
- Testing whether a proposed new market is a true adjacency versus unrelated diversification
- Building a sequenced expansion roadmap after core position is stable
## Avoid
- The growth path is primarily acquisition-led inorganic scale—use inorganic-growth-strategy
- You still cannot name the firm’s distinctive capabilities—use core-competencies first
- The question is industry attractiveness of a wholly new sector with no link to the core—use five-forces and market-entry-strategy
- You need a full multi-business portfolio rebalance rather than core-extension options—use portfolio-strategy-analysis
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Clear definition of the current core (customers, offerings, capabilities, economics); inventory of candidate adjacencies across product, customer, channel, and geography; evidence on capability transferability and required new skills or assets; relative attractiveness data for each adjacency (size, growth, margin structure); competitive intensity and barrier signals in each candidate space.

**Nice-to-have:** Customer journey evidence, share-of-wallet data, distribution coverage, and examples of prior extensions.

**When data is thin:** Keep candidates as hypotheses, score only the dimensions with evidence, and fund discovery before committing to an entry mode.
## Procedure
1. **Describe the defensible core.** Map the customers, assets, routines, channels, and economics that currently create advantage. State the mechanism that makes the core transferable.
2. **Build a named adjacency inventory.** Generate specific spaces from customer needs, adjacent purchases, channel gaps, geographic overlap, and reusable capabilities. “Healthcare” is too broad; “maintenance analytics for existing hospital clients” is a candidate.
3. **Measure distance from the core.** Assess customer overlap, capability reuse, brand permission, channel reuse, and economic similarity. Note the new capabilities that cannot be borrowed.
4. **Assess external attraction.** Estimate demand pool, growth, margin structure, competitive intensity, barriers, and time to credible entry for each candidate.
5. **Score and plot trade-offs.** Use a transparent weighted score or two-axis map of attractiveness against distance or transferability. Do not hide a large capability gap inside a single average score.
6. **Select a learning sequence.** Choose a small number of near adjacencies, define the proof required, and reserve distant moves until the core reinforcement or capability build is complete.
## Output Contract
Produce a ranked adjacency option map with a precise candidate definition, core linkage, transferability assessment, missing capabilities, market economics, competitive barriers, evidence confidence, and recommended next experiment. The deliverable must distinguish an adjacent extension from a generic growth idea and identify why its proximity matters.
## Evidence
Customer overlap, installed-base access, and demonstrated capability reuse are observed where records exist. Market size and margin estimates are often inferred and should state the comparison set. Treat brand permission and cross-sell uptake as assumptions until customers validate them. Present weakly evidenced candidates in a separate discovery queue, not beside well-substantiated options as equals.
## Checks
- Each candidate names a buyer, offer, route to market, and reused core asset.
- Scores expose both attractiveness and distance rather than rewarding market size alone.
- Missing capabilities have cost, time, and ownership implications.
- The shortlist contains alternatives rather than variants of one customer idea.
- Recommended experiments test the riskiest transferability claim first.
## Failure Modes
- Calling any fast-growing market adjacent because it is strategically appealing.
- Counting the same distributor relationship as proof of customer need and competitive advantage.
- Ranking a large market first while ignoring a two-year capability build.
- Treating a single successful pilot customer as evidence that the core travels across the category.
