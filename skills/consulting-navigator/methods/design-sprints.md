# Design Sprints
## Purpose
Compress map-sketch-decide-prototype-test into a fixed multi-day sprint that yields a user-validated answer to one critical question. The timebox creates evidence about a consequential bet before a delivery team commits to building it.
## Use
- Answering a critical product or service question in roughly one focused week
- Moving from divergent ideas to a prototype tested with real users
- De-risking a major bet before full build investment
## Avoid
- You need continuous backlog delivery, not a one-week spike—use agile-product-management
- The problem is open-ended exploration without a single critical question—use design-thinking
- You already have a prototype and only need statistical A/B evidence—use a-b-testing
## Inputs
**Must-have:** One well-framed critical question or challenge; Cross-functional sprint team with decision authority; Access to target users for end-of-sprint tests; Time-boxed five-day (or compressed) schedule and facilitation materials.

**Nice-to-have:** journey research, comparable solutions, and a clear recruiting screener.

**When data is thin:** narrow the question to one customer moment and treat the test as directional learning, not demand measurement.
## Procedure
1. **Map Monday:** agree the long-term goal, write sprint questions, map the customer journey, and select the moment where failure is most costly. The decider makes the target call.
2. **Sketch Tuesday:** have participants review useful precedents and create detailed solution sketches individually. This prevents the loudest voice from becoming the concept by default.
3. **Decide Wednesday:** critique sketches, use structured voting, resolve disagreements with the decider, and create a storyboard for the winning path.
4. **Prototype Thursday:** divide production tasks and create a realistic facade that can provoke authentic user behavior without building the underlying service.
5. **Test Friday:** conduct one-on-one interviews with target users, record reactions against sprint questions, and synthesize patterns immediately after the sessions.
## Output Contract
An assessment containing the sprint question, journey target, competing sketches considered, storyboard, prototype scope, participant profiles, interview observations, and a decision on the next bet. Separate what users did from facilitator interpretation, and state whether the finding supports build, redesign, further research, or abandonment.
## Evidence
Interview quotations, task behavior, and recording timestamps are observed. A pattern across a handful of participants is an inference, not a market-size estimate. Prototype shortcuts, recruiting criteria, and the assumed production experience are assumptions. Unreached segments, purchase behavior, and long-term retention are unknown; keep them outside the conclusion.
## Checks
- There is one decision owner and one answerable sprint question.
- The map ends in a chosen target, rather than a wall of unprioritized journeys.
- The prototype tests the riskiest assumption rather than visual polish.
- Participants match the intended user and have not been coached by the team.
- Interview notes distinguish a repeated pattern from one memorable comment.
## Failure Modes
- Starting with a feature wishlist so no critical question can be answered in five days.
- Letting a group brainstorm replace independent sketches and losing competing solutions.
- Calling polite reactions to a click-through mockup validation without observing a meaningful task.
