# Product Roadmap Development
## Purpose
Translate strategy and evidence into a visual, time-phased product direction that sequences themes, outcomes, and bets. It aligns stakeholders around the logic and uncertainty of planned investment without falsely promising a fixed feature schedule.
## Use
- Communicating multi-horizon product direction tied to outcomes, not just tickets
- Aligning exec, sales, and delivery on sequenced themes and capacity bets
- Translating strategy and PMF insights into a visible plan stakeholders can challenge
## Avoid
- Direction and fit are still unknown—use product-market-fit-analysis or lean-startup first
- You only need sprint-level task breakdown—use scrum-framework or user-stories-and-persona-mapping
- The program is a fixed-scope waterfall delivery—use stage-gate-process or critical-path-method
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Product vision, target segments, and strategic themes or outcomes; prioritized opportunities or problems (from PMF, JTBD, or discovery); capacity, dependency, and release constraints; success metrics per theme or horizon.

**Nice-to-have:** confidence scores, technical-health needs, commercial commitments, revenue scenarios, platform dependencies, and customer advisory input.

**When data is thin:** show fewer bets, use outcome hypotheses rather than feature names, and mark the horizon as exploratory until discovery or architecture work reduces uncertainty.
## Procedure
1. **Frame the strategic intent.** Translate vision into a small set of measurable outcomes for named segments. State the decisions the roadmap must coordinate, such as investment, customer communication, or partner preparation.
2. **Shape opportunities into themes.** Group problems and bets by outcome rather than source or requesting executive. For each theme, describe the customer change sought, success metric, and evidence of need.
3. **Sequence by horizon.** Place validated, capacity-ready commitments in the near term; place outcome bets with dependencies in the middle horizon; reserve the far horizon for directional options. Do not give distant hypotheses artificial release dates.
4. **Test capacity and dependencies.** Compare planned work with team capacity, architectural prerequisites, legal constraints, and launch windows. Resolve conflicts by changing scope or sequence, not by labeling all work critical.
5. **Set decision rules.** Define the metric, review point, and trigger that advances, changes, or stops each significant bet. Preserve a clear distinction between committed work, target work, and ideas.
6. **Publish and govern.** Create audience views that retain the same underlying logic, review on a fixed cadence, and record why bets moved. Use the roadmap to make trade-offs visible before the backlog absorbs them.
## Output Contract
A multi-horizon product view that names strategic themes, target segments, desired outcomes, measures, capacity assumptions, dependencies, confidence, and decision rules. It must separate commitments from options and tell stakeholders why one bet precedes another; it is not a dated catalog of every backlog item.
## Evidence
Customer evidence, usage outcomes, capacity allocations, and signed dependencies are observed. Expected impact and effort estimates are forecasts; show ranges where uncertainty is material. Mark executive requests without supporting evidence as hypotheses. Never equate a feature’s inclusion with proof that it will deliver the theme’s outcome.
## Checks
- Every near-term commitment traces to an outcome and a named success measure.
- Horizons use a consistent time convention and clearly label confidence.
- Capacity is tested against the same teams expected to deliver the work.
- Dependencies have owners and resolution dates.
- The view contains explicit “not now” or stopped bets to demonstrate prioritization.
## Failure Modes
- Turning a roadmap into a backlog screenshot with no customer or business outcome.
- Publishing far-horizon feature dates that sales treats as contractual promises.
- Hiding platform work so capacity appears available for every customer request.
- Moving a theme repeatedly without documenting the evidence or trade-off.
- Letting one executive’s urgent ask bypass the sequencing criteria.
