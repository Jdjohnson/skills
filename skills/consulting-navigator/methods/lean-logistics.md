# Lean Logistics
## Purpose
Eliminate waste and stabilize flow across logistics and distribution using lean principles. It applies lean work design to docks, warehouse movements, transport handoffs, and dispatch rather than limiting improvement to production cells.
## Use
- Removing waste, waiting, and excess motion from logistics and distribution flows
- Stabilizing dock, warehouse, and transport processes with pull and standard work
- Connecting lean thinking from plant or DC operations into inbound and outbound logistics
## Avoid
- You need mathematical safety-stock and ROP design—use inventory-optimization
- The problem is demand signal distortion across partners—use bullwhip-effect-analysis
- You are selecting outsourcing partners rather than improving flow—use 3pl-4pl-selection-framework
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Current-state logistics value stream (material and information flow); lead time, touch counts, dwell, and expedite data by process step; service requirements and non-negotiable constraints; cross-functional team access to docks, DCs, and transport partners.

**Nice-to-have:** trailer arrival logs, labor rosters, slotting data, damage codes, and carrier appointment adherence.

**When data is thin:** conduct timed walk-throughs across several shifts and retain a separate estimate for off-shift queues rather than averaging them away.
## Procedure
1. **Walk the physical and information flow.** Follow a representative shipment from appointment through receipt, putaway, pick, stage, load, and proof of delivery. Time touch work separately from waiting and identify handoffs between teams or systems.
2. **Identify logistics waste.** Mark unnecessary movement, double handling, search, staging, empty travel, damaged product, early release, and expedites. Connect each waste to an observed cause such as appointment bunching or unclear staging rules.
3. **Design the future flow.** Set pull points, fixed routes, standard container locations, dock schedules, visual triggers, and work sequence. Balance local utilization against end-to-end lead time; a busy forklift may signal a poor layout.
4. **Trial one flow segment.** Test the design at a dock door, pick zone, or route with an hourly board. Resolve practical exceptions with operators and carriers before extending the standard.
5. **Standardize and govern.** Publish the work sequence, replenishment triggers, appointment rules, and daily measures. Assign owners for deviations and a cadence for additional improvement experiments.
## Output Contract
Provide a logistics improvement roadmap with current and future flow, measured waste, redesigned material and information paths, standard-work elements, trial results, owners, and service safeguards. It must quantify expected lead-time, touch, or expedite changes by process segment, not only promise aggregate savings.
## Evidence
Use scans, time observations, route records, and direct counts for dwell and touches. Preserve non-value-added time instead of allocating it into productive labor. Label proposed labor savings as capacity released until a staffing decision is approved. Confirm carrier-caused waiting with appointment and arrival evidence, not warehouse impressions alone.
## Checks
- The map follows both product movement and the information that releases it.
- Future-state changes reduce a named delay or touch while preserving the service requirement.
- Dock, warehouse, and transport owners agree on the handoff rule.
- Trial metrics include flow time and defects, not just units per labor hour.
## Failure Modes
- Improving pick rate by staging finished orders for an extra day.
- Eliminating an inspection step without tracing the damage it prevented.
- Optimizing a warehouse aisle while outbound trailers still wait for paperwork.
- Calling an expedite reduction successful when missed orders were simply excluded.
