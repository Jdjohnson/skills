# IT Due Diligence
## Purpose
Assess a deal target’s technology estate, cyber risk, IT economics, and integration feasibility for pricing and PMI design. The objective is to surface value, liabilities, and closing or transition constraints early enough to affect the deal.
## Use
- Evaluating a target’s applications, infrastructure, data, security, and IT cost base pre-deal
- Estimating integration, separation, and tech investment required to deliver the thesis
- Flagging cyber, compliance, and license risks that can reprice or kill a deal
## Avoid
- You need enterprise digital strategy without a transaction—use digital-maturity-assessment or digital-transformation-framework
- The primary unknown is plant and process capability—use operational-due-diligence
- You are already post-close sequencing workstreams—use post-merger-integration-roadmap
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Application and infrastructure inventory with owners and criticality; IT cost run-rate, capex plan, and major vendor/license contracts; Architecture diagrams, integration points, and known technical debt; Security posture, audits, incidents, and compliance obligations; Planned deal perimeter (carve-out vs full acquisition) and synergy assumptions.

**Nice-to-have:** Data-room index, separation agreements, management forecasts, penetration-test summaries, and buyer architecture standards.

**When data is thin:** Use a request log and risk-ranked sampling plan; report which high-value systems or contracts were unavailable rather than treating management assurance as evidence.
## Procedure
1. **Translate the deal thesis into IT questions.** Define the acquisition perimeter, day-one continuity needs, planned synergies, separation obligations, and decisions that must be made before signing or closing.
2. **Build the evidence request and coverage map.** Inventory applications, infrastructure, data, suppliers, people, security, and cost. Mark each item confirmed, management-represented, unavailable, or contradictory so missing evidence remains visible.
3. **Assess estate and operational dependency.** Determine business criticality, lifecycle state, integration points, hosting concentration, key-person dependencies, data flows, and technical debt. Identify systems that cannot be separated or integrated on the proposed timetable.
4. **Test risk and contractual exposure.** Review incidents, vulnerabilities, compliance obligations, cyber insurance, change-of-control clauses, license assignment, outsourced service terms, and data-location commitments.
5. **Model cost and investment implications.** Normalize run rate, capex, stranded cost, required remediation, separation, and integration investment. Build downside ranges around the few assumptions that could reprice the deal.
6. **Synthesize for deal governance.** Rate findings by value impact, likelihood, urgency, and owner; distinguish a pre-close condition, a purchase-price adjustment, an indemnity issue, and a post-close workstream.
## Output Contract
An IT diligence report with scope and coverage limitations; a critical-system map; risk findings; contract and cyber exposures; normalized IT economics; integration and separation estimates; and a prioritized transaction action list. Each finding must state its deal consequence, confidence, and evidence location.
## Evidence
Treat data-room files, contracts, invoices, and independent audit reports as observed only after checking date and completeness. Interviews are management representations, not confirmation. Record sample coverage for security and application reviews. State whether a cost estimate includes TSA duration, duplicated licenses, retained staff, and remediation; uncertainty in any of these belongs in the valuation range.
## Checks
- Scope follows the legal perimeter and identifies excluded entities or shared services.
- Critical applications are tied to revenue, regulatory, or operational dependency.
- Findings distinguish current defect from transaction-specific exposure.
- Cost estimates separate recurring run-rate from one-time integration or separation spend.
- Red flags have an owner and a recommended deal mechanism or closing action.
## Failure Modes
- Accepting a polished application inventory without checking interfaces or business criticality.
- Treating a vulnerability scan as a complete cyber assessment.
- Missing assignment restrictions that make a supposedly transferable license unusable at close.
- Reporting technical debt as a vague risk without a time, cost, or synergy consequence.
