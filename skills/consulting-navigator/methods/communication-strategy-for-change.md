# Communication Strategy for Change
## Purpose
Design the audience-specific narratives, messengers, channels, and cadence that make a change understandable, credible, and actionable throughout the journey. The goal is not broadcast volume; it is timely clarity about why the change matters, what changes for each group, and what to do next.
## Use
- Designing audience-specific narratives, channels, cadence, and messengers for a change initiative.
- Aligning sponsor and manager messages so the story stays consistent through the rollout.
- Turning a strategic case for change into sequenced communications that drive understanding and action.
## Avoid
- You only need a general stakeholder outreach plan with no change narrative—use stakeholder-communication-plan.
- You need message structure for a one-off executive recommendation—use scqa or pyramid-principle.
- The core problem is resistance mechanics or incentives, not message design—use resistance-management-plan.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Case for change and non-negotiable decisions already made; stakeholder map with information needs, concerns, and preferred channels; messenger network (sponsors, managers, peers) and constraints; timeline of change milestones that communications must support.

**Nice-to-have:** baseline pulse data, manager readiness, previous-message performance, and local-language requirements.

**When facts are unsettled:** say what is decided, what is being designed, and when the next answer will be available; silence creates a competing rumor system.
## Procedure
1. Frame the change arc: current problem, destination, decisions made, milestones, and the behaviors that demonstrate adoption.
2. Segment audiences by impact and information need. For each, write the answers to “why now,” “what changes for me,” “what remains,” and “where do I get help.”
3. Build a message house with a stable core narrative and sanctioned local adaptations. Test that sponsor, executive, and manager versions do not contradict one another.
4. Match the sender to the message: senior leaders own business direction; direct managers explain local work and personal impact; peers demonstrate practical use.
5. Sequence channels around decision and adoption moments, using two-way forums before irreversible dates and manager briefings before employee-wide announcements.
6. Equip messengers with talking points, FAQs, escalation paths, and a listening loop. Revise messages from questions and pulse data, not only from the communications calendar.
## Output Contract
Provide a change communications roadmap: audience segments, message house, named senders, channel and cadence calendar, manager cascade pack, FAQs, feedback routes, and measures of understanding and action. It must connect every material milestone to a message and owner, rather than offering a generic list of emails and town halls.
## Evidence
Mark approved decisions as facts, expected effects as forecasts, and unresolved design choices as open. Attribute employee concerns to listening data or clearly label them as hypotheses. Do not promise job, process, or timing details that decision makers have not authorized. Record where confidentiality prevents specificity and schedule a follow-up.
## Checks
- Every impacted audience receives an explicit personal-impact and next-action message.
- Managers are briefed before they are expected to answer team questions.
- Business-case and local-impact messages name appropriate, credible senders.
- Calendar timing leads key milestones rather than explaining them after the fact.
- Feedback questions receive owners, response dates, and visible updates.
## Failure Modes
- Giving frontline managers the all-hands slides after employees have already seen them.
- Repeating the strategic rationale while never explaining changed shift, system, or approval routines.
- Asking an executive to answer individual-impact questions that a supervisor can answer credibly.
- Treating attendance at a town hall as evidence of understanding.
- Publishing a FAQ once and leaving disproven rumors in circulation.
