# Theory of Constraints
## Purpose
Identify the system’s primary limiting constraint and reorient policies, schedules, and investment so global throughput (or the chosen system goal) improves, rather than local efficiencies that do not raise finished output.
## Use
- Resources are busy yet finished goods, services, or projects remain late.
- Local efficiency metrics look healthy while end-to-end throughput is stuck.
- Work-in-process piles before one resource and starves the next.
- Capacity is added broadly but the same bottleneck reappears.
- Lead time and due-date performance resist overtime and firefighting.
- Scheduling does not currently protect the constraint with buffers or release control.
## Avoid
- The issue is quality-system certification rather than flow—use iso-9001.
- Broad waste elimination across many steps is needed first—use value-stream-mapping or lean-manufacturing.
- The problem is pure capital allocation with no operational constraint—use capital-budgeting-framework.
- Multiple independent products share almost no linked resources; local capacity analysis may suffice first.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** System goal metric (throughput, due-date hit rate, cash generation); process flow and demand pattern across linked resources; capacity/utilization evidence by stage; policy constraints (batch rules, metrics, release policies) that may bind flow.

**Nice-to-have:** WIP profiles by queue; changeover and downtime logs; current buffer locations; financial impact of late or lost throughput.

**When data is missing:** Observe queues and starvation at short intervals; treat “everything is a bottleneck” as a signal to examine policy and release rules, not just machines.
## Procedure
1. **Identify the constraint.** Using flow, queues, utilization, and due-date misses, name the single most limiting resource, policy, or market demand; produce a constraint statement tied to the system goal.
2. **Exploit the constraint.** Remove waste of constraint time (idle, wrong mix, defects, unnecessary setups); produce an exploit plan that raises effective constraint output without major capital.
3. **Subordinate everything else.** Align release rates, batch sizes, metrics, and non-constraint schedules to feed and protect the constraint rather than maximize local utilization; produce subordination rules and buffer policy.
4. **Elevate the constraint.** Only after exploit and subordinate, add capacity, technology, or outsourcing that expands constraint capability; produce a business case tied to throughput gain.
5. **Repeat and prevent inertia.** Reassess whether the constraint has moved; reset metrics so yesterday’s fix does not become a new policy constraint; produce the next-cycle focus statement.
## Output Contract
A throughput-oriented diagnosis and operating policy: named constraint with evidence; exploit actions; subordination and buffer/schedule rules; elevation options with expected throughput effect; and a decision to invest, reschedule, or change metrics—not merely a list of busy resources.
## Evidence
- **Observed:** queue lengths, starvation events, constraint downtime causes, actual release vs. completion.
- **Inferred:** policy constraints when utilization is high everywhere but flow still fails (often release or batching).
- **Assumptions:** market demand as constraint only when internal capacity clearly exceeds demand.
- **Unknowns:** unmeasured stages stay candidates until sampled.
Thin data → time-box a constraint hunt with visual management before capital recommendations.
## Checks
- Exactly one primary constraint is named for the current goal horizon (or market constraint is justified).
- Exploit actions come before elevation spend.
- Non-constraint KPIs that drive overproduction are identified and proposed for change.
- Buffer or release rules are specific enough to schedule against.
- Expected throughput or due-date improvement is stated directionally with evidence links.
- A re-identify trigger is defined for when the constraint shifts.
## Failure Modes
- Calling every busy station a bottleneck and diluting focus.
- Elevating with capital before exploiting lost constraint time.
- Keeping local efficiency targets that make non-constraints look productive while flooding or starving the constraint.
- Stopping after one cycle and letting organizational inertia become the new constraint.
