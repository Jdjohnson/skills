# Competitive Benchmarking
## Purpose
Gather and normalize external performance evidence to show where the firm leads, lags, or matches industry and rival benchmarks. The result converts competitive claims into comparable targets and investment priorities across more than price alone.
## Use
- Gathering comparable external performance evidence against rivals or industry leaders.
- Quantifying competitive gaps before redesign or investment decisions.
- Setting ambitious but evidence-based performance targets.
## Avoid
- You need internal process waste discovery without rival comparison—use value-stream-mapping or gemba-walks.
- The issue is industry structure power, not relative performance—use five-forces.
- You already have gap numbers and need root causes—use root-cause-analysis or gap-analysis as the action design step.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined metrics and processes to compare; peer or best-in-class set with access path (public data, panels, partners); normalized definitions so comparisons are apples-to-apples; internal baseline performance on the same metrics; time window and confidentiality constraints.

**Nice-to-have:** process documentation, third-party surveys, investor disclosures, and independent customer experience data.

**When peer data is partial:** compare only the common denominator and identify the limitation instead of manufacturing a rank.
## Procedure
1. Start from a strategic decision and select a compact metric set, such as on-time delivery, cost per order, defect rate, share, and NPS.
2. Define each metric’s formula, unit, scope, time period, and inclusion rules before collecting any rival figure.
3. Select direct competitors and an aspirational best-in-class comparator; distinguish a comparable peer from a company that merely has attractive results.
4. Collect evidence through public filings, published service terms, panels, mystery shopping, partners, or licensed datasets. Record source date and confidence for every observation.
5. Normalize scale, currency, geography, product mix, and service level. Calculate an absolute and percentage gap against internal performance and the selected reference.
6. Rank gaps by strategic importance and feasibility; set a target range with an owner, rather than copying a competitor’s metric without its operating context.
## Output Contract
Provide a benchmark scorecard that names the peer set, metric definitions, raw sources, normalization choices, internal baseline, comparable external values, gap, confidence, and target recommendation. Include a short narrative on the two or three gaps most likely to affect competitive position. It is not a broad market opinion survey or a competitor-price map.
## Evidence
Use dated, attributable observations for external values. Mark modeled estimates, currency conversion, and scale adjustments as assumptions. Do not imply precision when competitors report a range or a different accounting boundary. Respect confidentiality agreements; an anonymous aggregate may support a quartile comparison but not a named claim.
## Checks
- Every compared value uses the same numerator, denominator, and service boundary.
- Peer choice reflects the customer’s alternatives, not only familiar companies.
- Dates are recent enough to represent the decision period.
- Normalization does not erase a material business-model difference.
- Targets state whether they are parity, catch-up, or leadership ambitions.
## Failure Modes
- Comparing unit cost with a rival’s total cost and announcing a productivity advantage.
- Benchmarking a premium, white-glove offer against a self-service competitor without adjusting service level.
- Treating a single analyst estimate as a hard fact.
- Selecting only weak rivals so every metric looks favorable.
- Turning a 15% gap into a target without identifying the process or investment that could close it.
