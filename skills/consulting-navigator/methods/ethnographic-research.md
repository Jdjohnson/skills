# Ethnographic Research
## Purpose
Gather in-context evidence of how people actually behave by observing practice in natural settings. Sustained fieldwork exposes tacit routines, artifacts, workarounds, and local meanings that surveys and decontextualized conversations often miss.
## Use
- Understanding in-context behavior, workarounds, and meaning requires direct observation.
- Stated preferences or lab settings systematically miss real practice.
- Early problem framing needs thick description before quantification.
## Avoid
- You need statistically generalizable attitude estimates—use survey-design-methodology.
- You need moderated group reaction to concepts at scale—use focus-groups.
- You only need a short expert view without field immersion—use expert-interviewing.
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Access to real settings and consent for observation/participation.
- Field guide focused on activities, artifacts, and social context.
- Observation and interview notes (or recordings) over sufficient time in situ.
- Sampling of roles, sites, or moments that span the behavior of interest.
- Analysis approach for coding practices, tensions, and opportunities.

**Nice-to-have:** photographs of consented artifacts, journey data, translated materials, and a participant advisory contact.

**When data is thin:** make a narrow contextual claim and schedule return observation; do not generalize a single unusual visit to the whole population.
## Procedure
1. **Frame the field question.** State what practice, setting, population, and decision require understanding. Define what researchers will observe without turning the guide into a rigid checklist. Output: field brief and ethical protocol.
2. **Choose sites and moments.** Sample contrasting roles, locations, and times when the behavior changes, including handoffs and exceptions. Secure consent, privacy arrangements, and safe ways to record notes. Output: fieldwork plan.
3. **Observe participation in context.** Watch actual activity, environment, tools, interruptions, language, and informal coordination. Ask short contextual questions after a moment rather than interrupting consequential work. Output: fieldnotes and artifact log.
4. **Code practices and meaning.** Tag repeated actions, artifacts, tensions, improvisations, social norms, and contradictions between stated and observed behavior. Compare cases while preserving important differences. Output: codebook and evidence set.
5. **Synthesize patterns.** Develop findings that connect context to behavior and distinguish observed episodes from interpretation. Select vivid but non-identifying evidence. Output: insight themes and opportunity hypotheses.
6. **Validate without flattening.** Review interpretations with participants or knowledgeable reviewers, correct factual errors, and retain minority patterns where they matter. Output: validated field insight report.
## Output Contract
An assessment containing the setting and sample, observation protocol, evidence-backed practice patterns, artifacts and contextual factors, coded themes, caveats on transferability, and opportunity hypotheses. It must show how actual behavior unfolded in context, not reduce fieldnotes to a quote list or a leader walkthrough.
## Evidence
Time-stamped observations and consented artifacts are observed evidence. Interpretations of intent, norm, or cultural meaning are inferences that require a trace to episodes and participant explanation. Mark access limitations and observer effects. Protect identities by removing details that would expose a participant or sensitive site.
## Checks
- Sites and moments cover meaningful variation in the practice.
- Notes distinguish what was seen, heard, and later interpreted.
- Themes cite multiple episodes or explicitly state that they are tentative.
- Artifacts and workarounds are connected to the surrounding constraints.
- Participants have a channel to correct harmful factual misunderstandings.
## Failure Modes
- Watching a scripted demonstration and calling it everyday practice.
- Collecting colorful quotes while ignoring tools, timing, and handoffs.
- Treating the observer’s cultural interpretation as a participant’s stated meaning.
- Sampling only convenient sites and missing the conditions where work breaks down.
- Sharing field detail that lets managers identify a vulnerable participant.
