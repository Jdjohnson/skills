# Ansoff Matrix
## Purpose
Generate and classify growth options along product-newness and market-newness axes to surface distinct risk profiles. The four cells create a language for comparing growth directions before the organization commits to a route.
## Use
- Generating a full set of growth directions before selecting bets
- Explaining risk differences across market penetration, product development, market development, and diversification
- Structuring corporate growth conversations with a simple shared language
## Avoid
- You need timing of near/mid/long-term innovation horizons—use three-horizons-of-growth
- You already chose a growth path and need entry mechanics—use market-entry-strategy or go-to-market-strategy
- The problem is industry structure or internal capability, not growth direction—use five-forces or core-competencies
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Current products/services and served markets; growth ambition and risk appetite; evidence on market saturation and unmet demand; capability constraints for new products or geographies; competitive intensity in current vs adjacent markets.

**Nice-to-have:** Revenue by segment, customer needs research, market size estimates, innovation pipeline, and investment capacity.

**When data is thin:** Classify ideas first and use the matrix to expose unknowns; do not convert a quadrant label into a claim that a move will succeed.
## Procedure
1. **Define the current product and market boundary.** Specify what counts as the present offer and served market, including customer segment and geography. Ambiguous boundaries produce misleading quadrant labels.
2. **List concrete growth moves.** Collect candidate initiatives in plain language: increase share with existing distributors, sell the current offer in a new country, add a new service to present accounts, or launch into both a new category and buyer base.
3. **Classify every move.** Place each candidate in market penetration, market development, product development, or diversification according to whether product and market are existing or new.
4. **Expose risk sources by cell.** Consider unfamiliar customers, new value proposition, channel, capability, capital, regulatory, and competitive risks. Diversification is not automatically rejected; it requires the strongest evidence and safeguards.
5. **Compare the portfolio.** Evaluate expected value, investment, capability stretch, time to result, and risk appetite across cells. Preserve diversity instead of allowing near-term penetration options to crowd out all learning bets.
6. **Choose next investigations.** Select the options worth sizing, validating, or entering, and name the next method required for each.
## Output Contract
Deliver a four-cell growth option set containing a definition of the current base, each named initiative, its classification rationale, key risk sources, expected value range, capability implications, and next investigation. The artifact must show the portfolio’s exposure across product and market newness, not pretend to be a detailed market-entry plan.
## Evidence
Current sales, customer mix, and existing offer boundaries are observed. Market saturation, unmet needs, and competitor response should cite their data source or be marked inferred. Treat “new” as relative to the firm, not universally new to the industry. Where an initiative could fit two cells, document the boundary assumption and test the classification with the sponsoring business owner.
## Checks
- Each item has a specific product, buyer or market, and starting point.
- All four cells were considered before choosing a growth narrative.
- Risks are tied to the source of newness, not summarized by a generic score.
- Expected value is not compared without investment and capability requirements.
- Every selected option has a next validation or entry decision.
## Failure Modes
- Calling a new sales channel “market development” when the buyer and market are unchanged.
- Treating diversification as a single bucket without distinguishing related from unrelated moves.
- Filling the matrix with vague labels such as “international growth.”
- Choosing only low-risk penetration ideas when the stated ambition requires new products or markets.
