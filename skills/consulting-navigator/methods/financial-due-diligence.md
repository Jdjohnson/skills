# Financial Due Diligence
## Purpose
Verify and restate a target's earnings quality, net debt, and cash conversion into a buyer-ready financial risk assessment. It translates reported accounts into the economics a buyer will actually acquire, including the closing adjustments that can change consideration.
## Use
- Validating a target's historical financial performance before binding offer or close.
- Isolating one-time items, accounting policies, and cash conversion risks that restate value.
- Establishing a buyer-ready view of net debt, working capital, and earn-out metrics.
## Avoid
- You need strategic market attractiveness rather than financial verification—use commercial-due-diligence.
- The question is post-deal operating integration design—use post-merger-integration-roadmap or m-and-a-integration-framework.
- You only need a relative market price check with no books access—use valuation-multiples.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Historical financial statements and management accounts; accounting policies, audit reports, and key reconciling schedules; net debt and debt-like item schedules; working capital history and proposed closing mechanism; budget/forecast with bridge to historical run-rate.

**Useful additions:** monthly trial balances, customer and supplier aging, tax returns, board packs, covenant agreements, and sale-and-purchase-agreement definitions.

**Access limitation:** state which ledgers, legal entities, and months were unavailable; unresolved access is a finding, not a zero-risk conclusion.
## Procedure
1. Set the deal perimeter, reporting currency, period cut-off, materiality, and definitions of EBITDA, debt, cash, and normal working capital. Reconcile the entity list to the transaction perimeter.
2. Tie management accounts to audited statements, trial balances, and bank or debt schedules. Build a reconciliation log for every material difference rather than accepting a management bridge.
3. Construct a quality-of-earnings bridge from reported EBITDA to maintainable EBITDA. Test revenue cut-off, unusual margins, add-backs, related-party transactions, capitalization policies, and non-recurring income or expense.
4. Analyze monthly trading, cash conversion, receivables, inventory, payables, capex, and seasonality. Separate a temporary working-capital swing from the run-rate level needed to operate on day one.
5. Rebuild net debt under the agreed definition. Search leases, accrued interest, factoring, tax exposures, deferred consideration, guarantees, and other debt-like obligations in schedules and notes.
6. Challenge the forecast against the historical bridge, order book, and stated assumptions. Quantify findings as EBITDA, cash, purchase-price, covenant, or execution exposures and identify who must resolve each before signing or closing.
## Output Contract
A buyer decision pack containing a reported-to-maintainable EBITDA bridge, monthly cash and working-capital analysis, debt/debt-like schedule, forecast challenge, findings register, and a proposed purchase-price or contractual response. Each finding states the amount, period, evidence, confidence, and deal consequence. It is not a valuation model; it supplies validated inputs and downside cases to one.
## Evidence
Mark figures tied to ledgers, statements, contracts, or bank evidence as observed. Mark analytical normalizations, such as a recurring bonus estimate, as inferred and show the calculation. Treat deal definitions, materiality, and forecast cases as assumptions agreed with the buyer. Keep management explanations without corroboration in an open-question log. Never offset a debt-like item against a speculative synergy.
## Checks
- The EBITDA bridge reconciles exactly back to reported accounts before adjustments.
- Every adjustment has a period, owner, calculation, and supporting record.
- Net debt and working-capital definitions match the latest transaction documents.
- Cash conversion is examined monthly, not only as an annual average.
- Findings distinguish price impact from an issue that needs a warranty, indemnity, or closing condition.
## Failure Modes
- Accepting a “one-off” add-back repeatedly booked in several prior years.
- Treating factoring proceeds as operating cash while ignoring recourse or fees.
- Using the latest month’s working capital as normal despite a seasonal inventory build.
- Missing liabilities held in a non-trading entity outside the management reporting pack.
