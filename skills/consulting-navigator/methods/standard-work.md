# Standard Work
## Purpose
Design and document the agreed most efficient, safe, and repeatable method so work is consistent, trainable, and improvable.
## Use
- Codifying the current best-known method after waste and variation are understood.
- Stabilizing a process so improvement gains hold and training is consistent.
- Creating the baseline against which kaizen, SPC, and audits will run.
## Avoid
- The process is still unstable, undefined, or full of unmapped waste—use sipoc-analysis or value-stream-mapping first.
- You need real-time signals of abnormal conditions more than a written method—use visual-management or statistical-process-control.
- The problem is equipment reliability rather than human method consistency—use total-productive-maintenance.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Observed current work sequence and takt or expected cycle time; work content elements, tools, and quality checks at each step; safety, quality, and customer requirements the method must protect; operator input on what works and where variation or strain appears; agreement on process owner accountable for updating the standard.

**Nice-to-have:** Task photos, ergonomic assessment, training records, and audits.

**When data is thin:** Observe competent operators and write a provisional standard for the stable sequence. Verify uncertain timing before staffing.
## Procedure
1. Observe work across operators and shifts. Record sequence, elapsed time, walking, tools, safety exposures, quality checks, and variation; ask why exceptions occur.
2. Compare the observed work to takt or expected cycle time and to the required quality and safety conditions. Remove obvious waste only when it does not introduce a new ergonomic, compliance, or downstream defect risk.
3. Select the safest best-known sequence and specify each key point: trigger, action, tool or material, time expectation, quality confirmation, and stop-or-escalate condition. Separate normal work from rare exception handling.
4. Create visual instructions at the station: concise steps, photographs or diagrams, locations, and revision identifiers. Test them with an operator who did not help write them.
5. Train to the standard using demonstration, guided practice, and observed return demonstration. Record competence and collect deviations that indicate the standard does not match reality.
6. Install ownership, audit cadence, and change control. Improve the method through kaizen, then revise the controlled document rather than allowing informal local variants.
## Output Contract
Deliver a controlled standard-work package containing scope and trigger, required sequence, expected time or takt relation, tools, safety precautions, quality checks, visual instructions, exception and escalation rules, training method, owner, revision history, and audit cadence. It must let a trained person perform the job consistently and expose deviations. It is not a 5S checklist, a management dashboard, or a generic policy document.
## Evidence
Base the sequence on direct observation and operator confirmation, not an outdated procedure alone. Label target times separately from observed times. Preserve safety and regulatory requirements even when faster work appears possible. When conditions differ by product, skill level, or equipment, create a variant or an explicit decision rule; do not conceal the difference in a single average instruction.
## Checks
- Steps can be followed at the point of work without an expert translator.
- Each critical quality point has an observable check and response.
- Safety controls appear at the relevant step, not only on a cover page.
- Timing is feasible for the specified product and staffing conditions.
- Revision ownership prevents unapproved local copies from becoming the norm.
## Failure Modes
- Capturing the fastest operator’s shortcuts while omitting safety checks.
- Writing paragraphs that a new operator cannot scan during the task.
- Training once, then never observing whether the method is actually used.
- Freezing an instruction after a tool or product change has made it inaccurate.
