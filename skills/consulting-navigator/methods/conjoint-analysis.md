# Conjoint Analysis
## Purpose
Estimate part-worth value of product attributes from forced trade-off choices to guide offer design and pricing. By making respondents choose among realistic alternatives, it reveals the value they surrender when they select one feature, price, or service level over another.
## Use
- You must quantify relative value of product attributes and levels under forced trade-offs.
- Offer design or pricing depends on part-worth utilities and share simulations.
- Stated importance ratings are inflated and unusable for prioritization.
## Avoid
- You can run a live head-to-head on one change with real traffic—use a-b-testing.
- You need open-ended needs discovery before attributes are known—use jobs-to-be-done or ethnographic-research.
- The decision is purely technical feasibility, not preference—use a different method.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined attributes and levels that form realistic product profiles; respondent sample representing the target market; choice or rating task design (profiles, choice sets, constraints); estimation plan for part-worth utilities and fit checks; market scenario assumptions for share or willingness-to-pay simulation.

**Nice-to-have:** competitor offers, prior segmentation, purchase incidence, cost data, and a pilot survey sample.

**When respondents cannot make credible choices:** simplify attributes or return to qualitative research; an elaborate but implausible profile measures survey fatigue.
## Procedure
1. Turn research and product constraints into a small set of attributes with mutually exclusive, feasible levels, including price where it is a decision variable.
2. Draft realistic alternatives and prohibited combinations. Pretest comprehension, attribute balance, task length, and whether an opt-out is needed.
3. Construct an experimental design that varies levels efficiently and assigns multiple choice tasks to each respondent without repeatedly showing dominated offers.
4. Field the survey to a defined market sample; screen for relevant buyers and monitor straight-lining, speeders, and failed attention checks.
5. Estimate respondent or segment part-worth utilities, then inspect holdout-task fit and the sign and ordering of price utilities.
6. Simulate named offer and competitor scenarios under stated availability assumptions. Convert utility results into decisions on bundle, feature, price, and segment—not a universal feature ranking.
## Output Contract
Deliver an attribute-and-level design record, sample description, model diagnostics, part-worth utilities, relative attribute importance, and scenario simulations. The report must show the assumptions behind predicted share or willingness to pay and specify which offer configurations are feasible. A list of self-reported feature ratings is not a conjoint result.
## Evidence
Respondent choices are observed survey data; utilities and scenario shares are model estimates. Label the market frame, competitive set, availability, and purchase-frequency assumptions. Do not treat stated choice as booked demand. Keep impossible combinations out of the design and disclose exclusions that limit a simulation.
## Checks
- Attribute levels are actionable and span the choices product leaders might make.
- No level is confounded with several hidden changes.
- Task design has sufficient variation to identify each attribute.
- Holdout predictions and price-response direction are plausible.
- Scenario outputs use the same market and availability assumptions as the input design.
## Failure Modes
- Asking respondents to choose an offer with a premium feature at a price the company could never deliver.
- Including 14 attributes until people answer randomly.
- Calling an attribute unimportant because levels were too similar to create a trade-off.
- Simulating share against a competitor with a missing or invented configuration.
- Treating aggregate utilities as permission to ignore a high-value segment.
