# Industry Evolution Stages
## Purpose
Locate an industry or category on its evolution path and translate stage dynamics into competitive and investment implications. The analysis explains why the economics and winning moves of an earlier period may no longer apply.
## Use
- Diagnosing whether an industry or product category is emerging, growth, maturity, or decline.
- Aligning investment intensity, pricing, and competitive posture to lifecycle stage.
- Explaining why historical growth rates or margin patterns no longer hold.
## Avoid
- You need structural power analysis of buyers, suppliers, and rivalry rather than stage dynamics—use five-forces.
- The question is firm-level capability or cost structure, not industry maturity—use resource-based-view or value-chain-analysis.
- You already know the stage and need portfolio allocation across businesses—use bcg-matrix or ge-mckinsey-matrix.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined industry or category boundary and time series of market size/growth; Evidence on competitive intensity, concentration, and entry/exit rates by period; Indicators of technology diffusion, buyer sophistication, and product standardization; Rough map of leading firms' strategies and investment postures.

**Useful additions:** price history, capacity additions, channel data, regulation timeline, and substitute adoption signals.
## Procedure
1. Define the category boundary, geographic scope, and time horizon. Explain which adjacent offers or substitutes are inside the analysis and why.
2. Plot market size, growth, price, margin, entry, exit, concentration, and capacity across time. Look for inflections rather than assigning a stage from one year's growth rate.
3. Examine stage indicators: technology uncertainty, product variety, buyer education, standardization, channel structure, and basis of competition. Compare evidence that supports different stage interpretations.
4. Diagnose the dominant stage and any split condition, such as mature core demand with an emerging premium subcategory. State the confidence and the signals that would change the diagnosis.
5. Translate the stage into implications for investment pace, product breadth, pricing, cost position, channel commitment, and likely competitive moves.
6. Identify leading indicators to monitor and set decision triggers for revisiting the stage view when growth, consolidation, or disruption shifts.
## Output Contract
Deliver a stage diagnosis with the defined market, time-series exhibits, evidence for and against the selected stage, implications by strategic lever, and monitoring triggers. The package must distinguish a category lifecycle view from a static assessment of supplier or buyer power. It should tell leaders what changes in posture are warranted, not merely label the market mature.
## Evidence
Use consistent market definitions across the time series; label breakpoints caused by revised sources or currency conversions. Treat analyst forecasts and proxy measures for adoption as assumptions. Do not infer decline from slowing percentage growth in a rapidly expanding base. When subsegments move at different speeds, preserve that heterogeneity rather than averaging it into an artificial stage.
## Checks
- The category boundary is stable enough for comparisons over time.
- Stage assignment relies on multiple indicators, not a single growth statistic.
- Counterevidence and stage-transition signals are explicit.
- Implications differ from a generic strategy checklist and fit the diagnosed dynamics.
- Monitoring metrics can reveal a transition early enough to alter investment.
## Failure Modes
- Calling a market mature because one incumbent is old while the category is still adding new buyers.
- Treating a temporary recession as permanent decline.
- Combining premium and commodity segments whose adoption and margins move differently.
- Recommending consolidation without checking whether fragmentation is caused by local regulation or product variety.
