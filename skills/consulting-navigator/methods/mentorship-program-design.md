# Mentorship Program Design
## Purpose
Design a governed mentorship system—purpose, matching, cadence, and metrics—that transfers judgment and expands access beyond informal networks. The design makes relationship quality and access manageable rather than assuming willing volunteers will create both.
## Use
- Building a structured mentoring system for capability transfer, career growth, or inclusion of access to networks
- Moving from informal mentorship to governed matching, expectations, and outcomes
- Supporting succession and knowledge continuity in critical roles
## Avoid
- You need formal skill curricula and learning pathways first—use training-needs-analysis
- The gap is unclear role competencies, not transfer relationships—use competency-mapping
- The problem is first-90-day integration of new hires—use onboarding-framework
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Program purpose (career, skill, reverse, sponsorship) and target populations; mentor supply, readiness criteria, and time capacity; matching criteria and relationship duration model; guidance for goals, cadence, confidentiality, and escalation; success metrics and feedback loop design.

**Nice-to-have:** demographic access analysis, critical-knowledge maps, manager expectations, and an employee-resource-group partner.

**When data is thin:** launch a small cohort with explicit capacity limits and collect match-quality feedback before opening enrollment more broadly.
## Procedure
1. **Choose the program promise.** Select one primary purpose and eligible population. Separate mentoring, sponsorship, reverse mentoring, and technical apprenticeship because each requires different mentors and outcomes.
2. **Size supply and guard capacity.** Estimate mentor hours, cohort size, relationship length, and coordinator workload. Screen mentors for willingness, relevant experience, listening skill, and manager support.
3. **Design matching rules.** Collect participant goals and preferences, define must-match criteria and conflict exclusions, then decide whether matching is algorithm-assisted, facilitated, or self-selected. Include a rematch route that does not stigmatize either person.
4. **Create the relationship operating model.** Provide a kickoff, goal-setting guide, recommended cadence, confidentiality boundaries, escalation contacts, and a midpoint check. Specify what managers may know without exposing private discussion.
5. **Measure access and transfer.** Track activation, meeting continuity, rematches, goal progress, and differential outcomes by target population. Review lessons after each cohort and revise the matching pool or support materials.
## Output Contract
A mentorship program blueprint with target cohort, program type, capacity model, mentor eligibility, matching rules, participant journey, confidentiality and escalation rules, coordinator roles, and scorecard. It must show how a relationship begins, is supported, and ends or rematches, not simply invite employees to find a mentor.
## Evidence
Use workforce data and participation records as observed. Label self-reported career outcomes and manager perceptions as reported evidence, not proof of causality. State assumptions about mentor availability and pair compatibility. Protect conversation content: program reporting should use aggregated themes, while individual concerns follow the escalation policy. Check access measures for disparities rather than treating enrollment as equitable access.
## Checks
- The stated purpose determines matching criteria and metrics.
- Mentor capacity can cover the planned cohort without recurring overload.
- Both parties receive expectations for cadence, confidentiality, and rematching.
- Sponsorship is not mislabeled as mentoring when advocacy is expected.
- Outcome reporting distinguishes completed meetings from demonstrated knowledge transfer.
## Failure Modes
- Matching only senior volunteers to people who already have network access.
- Letting a mentor carry twelve mentees with no protected time.
- Treating a silent or mismatched pair as success because no complaint was filed.
- Publishing personal conversations in a program dashboard.
- Measuring enrollment but never checking whether the relationships meet.
