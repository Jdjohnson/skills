# Core Competencies
## Purpose
Diagnose which collective learning capabilities create customer value across businesses and are hard for rivals to imitate. The method looks beneath products and functional labels to identify a small number of enterprise-spanning advantages that can guide investment and growth choices.
## Use
- Identifying cross-business collective learning that underpins multiple products.
- Testing whether growth or M&A builds on genuine competence advantages.
- Explaining durable advantage beyond current product lines.
## Avoid
- You need a full VRIO resource audit of all assets—use resource-based-view.
- You need industry power structure, not internal advantage—use five-forces.
- You already know the competence and need market positioning—use generic-strategies or blue-ocean-strategy.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Product/market footprint and shared underlying technologies or know-how; evidence of customer value delivered by those capabilities; indicators of imitability and transferability across businesses; organizational locations of deep skills and learning systems; competitor capability comparisons where available.

**Nice-to-have:** patents, training pipelines, supplier relationships, product architecture maps, and post-merger integration lessons.

**When evidence is anecdotal:** treat candidate competencies as hypotheses and seek customer, performance, and competitor evidence before using them in capital allocation.
## Procedure
1. Inventory products, customer benefits, technologies, processes, and specialist communities across business units. Ask what repeatable learning, rather than which product, enables each benefit.
2. Cluster recurring know-how into candidate competencies, combining technical, operational, and coordination capabilities where all are needed to produce the advantage.
3. Test every candidate against three questions: does it open access to multiple markets, make a meaningful contribution to customer benefit, and resist imitation?
4. Trace the learning system behind strong candidates: talent development, proprietary routines, architecture, scale, relationships, and the locations where expertise accumulates.
5. Challenge candidates with counterexamples. Determine whether a competitor can buy, hire, license, or replicate the capability quickly, or whether it is merely a current product’s feature.
6. Select a deliberately small portfolio, map it to products and opportunities, identify erosion risks, and name the investments needed to preserve or deepen each one.
## Output Contract
Deliver a corporate competence map with the selected collective-learning capabilities, supporting evidence, products and markets enabled, customer-benefit mechanism, imitation assessment, ownership locations, and development priorities. It must distinguish core competencies from functional necessities, assets, and attractive product lines. The result is an upstream diagnosis, not a market-entry plan.
## Evidence
Customer outcomes, repeat deployment, and observable learning routines are evidence. Claims of uniqueness, market portability, and competitor difficulty are judgments unless tested against credible comparison. Do not confuse a patent, brand, plant, or individual expert with the organization’s ability to repeatedly combine learning. Record gaps where competitors’ capability is unknown.
## Checks
- Each selected competence underpins more than one current or credible future offering.
- Customer value is specific enough to verify, not “quality” or “innovation.”
- The map identifies collective routines and talent systems, not only named star employees.
- Imitability considers time, dependencies, and substitutes.
- The final list is focused enough to direct enterprise investment choices.
## Failure Modes
- Calling a profitable product a competence without identifying the reusable learning behind it.
- Elevating every functional department to “core,” leaving no investment trade-off.
- Mistaking a short-lived regulatory license for difficult-to-imitate capability.
- Assuming a patented component opens unrelated markets without proof of customer benefit.
- Ignoring that a competence is concentrated in one retiring expert or acquired team.
