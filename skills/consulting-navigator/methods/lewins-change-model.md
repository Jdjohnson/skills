# Lewin's Change Model
## Purpose
Frame organizational change as unfreeze–change–refreeze so leaders sequence disruption, transition, and stabilization deliberately.
## Use
- Framing a multi-phase change so sponsors share one high-level sequence
- Diagnosing why prior change efforts unfroze behavior but never refroze new norms
- Scoping readiness work before detailed step plans or workstreams are designed
## Avoid
- You need a detailed multi-step leadership playbook—use kotters-8-step-process
- The issue is individual psychological transition rather than organizational force fields—use bridges-transition-model
- You already have the stage frame and need role-level adoption mechanics—use adkar-model
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Clear description of the current steady state and what must unfreeze
- Desired future behaviors, processes, or structures to institutionalize
- Known restraining and driving forces (or proxies from stakeholder feedback)
- Sponsor intent on how long the transition window can last

**Nice-to-have:** prior change history, a timeline of triggering events, workforce sentiment, union or regulatory constraints, and measures that reveal whether the new routine persists.

**When data is thin:** call the force-field ratings hypotheses and test them with a small set of affected roles. Avoid assigning motives to resistance without hearing from the people doing the work.
## Procedure
1. **Describe the present equilibrium.** Specify the routine, incentives, relationships, and informal workarounds that keep the old pattern stable. Output: current-state portrait.
2. **Map forces around the proposed state.** List forces pushing movement and forces holding the system in place; estimate relative strength and identify who experiences each force. Output: force-field map.
3. **Design the unfreeze.** Remove or weaken restraints through a credible case for change, visible sponsor choices, protected time, and revised incentives. Do not begin by broadcasting the future process. Output: readiness actions.
4. **Guide the move.** Introduce new roles, process trials, training, and feedback channels while keeping decisions reversible where possible. Output: transition plan.
5. **Refreeze the workable routine.** Embed the adopted behavior in measures, job aids, supervision, hiring, and reward practices; decide which legacy practices are retired. Output: stabilization plan and review dates.
## Output Contract
Deliver a three-stage roadmap showing the current equilibrium, the desired steady state, major driving and restraining forces, actions by stage, accountable sponsors, and indicators that the new practice has become normal. The roadmap must explain why the sequence starts before rollout and how leaders will know that change has stuck.
## Evidence
Ground force assessments in interviews, observed workflow, performance trends, and policy evidence. Mark power or intensity ratings inferred from limited conversations as provisional. Distinguish a stated objection from a structural restraint such as pay, capacity, or system design. When the desired state is still vague, freeze detailed actions and document only the conditions needed to safely unfreeze.
## Checks
- The current stable pattern is described, not reduced to “people dislike change.”
- Each high-strength restraint has an owner and a planned response.
- Unfreeze actions occur before the behavior they enable is required.
- Transition support reaches the roles whose routine must change.
- Refreeze measures test sustained use after project attention declines.
## Failure Modes
- Announcing a new process while incentives still reward the previous one.
- Treating training as the only answer to a workload or authority constraint.
- Ending the project at launch before supervisors, scorecards, and onboarding reinforce the new routine.
- Calling every concern resistance and missing a legitimate operating risk.
- Locking the new state before pilots reveal necessary design corrections.
