# Focus Groups
## Purpose
Elicit group-generated language, reactions, and themes through moderated multi-participant discussion. Interaction is the instrument: participants explain, challenge, and borrow from one another while reacting to a shared topic or stimulus.
## Use
- You need moderated group discussion to surface language, reactions, and social dynamics around a topic or concept.
- Exploratory insight or concept feedback is needed before larger quantitative work.
- Diverse participant perspectives in interaction are more valuable than isolated interviews alone.
## Avoid
- You need in-context observed behavior rather than talk—use ethnographic-research.
- You need individual expert depth without group influence—use expert-interviewing.
- You need population estimates or validated measurement—use survey-design-methodology.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Clear research questions and stimulus materials (concepts, mockups, messaging); recruitment criteria and 6–10 participants per session composition plan; discussion guide and skilled moderator; recording/notes protocol and analysis plan for themes; awareness of social desirability and groupthink risks to mitigate.

**Useful additions:** a note-taker, pilot session, accessibility plan, incentive rules, and pre-session screeners.

**Sampling caution:** recruit for the comparison the decision needs; a group of existing enthusiasts cannot establish how skeptical prospects respond.
## Procedure
1. Turn the decision into a small set of learning questions and decide which participant characteristics should be similar enough for conversation and different enough for useful contrast.
2. Recruit separate groups by the material segmentation variables. Screen out professional respondents and conflicts, confirm consent, and prepare neutral stimuli that do not signal the preferred answer.
3. Write a funnel-shaped guide: opening context, broad experience questions, transition questions, stimulus exercises, comparative probes, and a final reflection. Pilot it with colleagues without substituting them for participants.
4. Open each session by explaining confidentiality, turn-taking, and the moderator’s neutral role. Start with independent written reactions when anchoring would otherwise be likely.
5. Moderate for interaction rather than a sequence of mini-interviews. Ask participants to compare views, request examples, invite quieter people, and redirect a dominant speaker without defending the concept.
6. Debrief immediately using recordings, notes, and stimulus artifacts. Code themes by group and participant type, preserve disconfirming comments, and distinguish repeated language from a single persuasive voice.
## Output Contract
Provide a thematic feedback report with the research questions, recruitment and session profile, discussion stimuli, themes with supporting paraphrases, differences across groups, dissenting views, design implications, and limitations. It should state what language participants used and how conversation altered reactions. It does not estimate market size, preference share, or prevalence.
## Evidence
Recorded statements and observed interactions are evidence of what occurred in a session, not proof of population behavior. Moderator interpretation and theme coding are inferred; retain a trace from conclusion to session notes. Sampling eligibility, incentive level, and concept fidelity are assumptions that can shape results. Mark unanswered questions for subsequent interviews, observation, or survey work.
## Checks
- The guide separates discovery questions from concept-evaluation questions.
- Group composition avoids avoidable status or reporting relationships.
- Stimulus order is rotated when alternatives could suffer order effects.
- Findings name how many groups expressed a theme and record meaningful disagreement.
- Recommendations do not convert an anecdote into a quantified demand claim.
## Failure Modes
- Allowing the client sponsor to defend a concept in the room, which changes the social pressure.
- Mixing buyers and sellers whose relationship prevents candid discussion.
- Coding the loudest participant’s phrasing as the group’s consensus.
- Asking participants to predict their future purchase instead of probing present behavior and trade-offs.
