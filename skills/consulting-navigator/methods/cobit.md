# COBIT
## Purpose
Establish enterprise IT governance goals, decision rights, and control objectives that link IT value, risk, and resources. It creates a tailored governance system that connects board intent to accountable management practices and measures.
## Use
- Designing or remediating enterprise IT governance, decision rights, and control objectives
- Aligning IT value creation, risk, and resource management for board and audit scrutiny
- Establishing goals, cascade, and performance measures across IT processes
## Avoid
- You need service-management operating procedures rather than governance goals—use itil
- The gap is specifically cyber control frameworks—use cybersecurity-framework
- Architecture standards are the issue, not governance—use togaf
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Enterprise goals and IT-related goals; current IT decision rights, policies, and control landscape; audit findings and regulatory obligations affecting IT; stakeholder map of board, risk, business, and IT leadership.

**Nice-to-have:** portfolio metrics, risk appetite, regulatory examination results, process inventory, and current performance dashboards.

**When data is thin:** start from the highest-risk enterprise goals and audit findings, documenting unvalidated control operation rather than marking it effective by default.
## Procedure
1. **Identify stakeholder needs and enterprise priorities.** Clarify value, risk, resource, compliance, and assurance expectations with the board, executives, risk leaders, and business owners. Resolve conflicting priorities before selecting controls.
2. **Cascade enterprise goals to IT-related goals.** Map each material enterprise aim to the information and technology outcomes it requires, such as reliable service, protected information, transparent investment value, or capable people.
3. **Select governance and management objectives.** Assess the current decision rights, practices, policies, information flows, skills, and culture against the objectives needed for the chosen goals. Tailor the scope rather than adopting every control equally.
4. **Design accountability and measures.** Assign governing-body oversight and management ownership, define decision forums, escalation paths, control activities, and goal metrics. Distinguish a metric that reports performance from one that proves a control operates.
5. **Implement and monitor the system.** Prioritize remediation, test operating evidence, review outcomes at the appropriate governance forum, and adjust objectives as enterprise strategy, risk, or technology changes.
## Output Contract
Deliver an IT governance design containing stakeholder needs, enterprise-to-IT goal cascade, selected objectives, current-state gaps, governance components, decision rights, accountabilities, measures, implementation roadmap, and assurance cadence. The design must show how a board-level expectation becomes an owned practice and measurable outcome, not merely list IT processes.
## Evidence
Policies, committee minutes, audit findings, system records, and control-test results are observed. Mappings between goals and objectives, maturity judgments, and anticipated benefits are analytic judgments that need rationale. State where a control is designed but not tested in operation. Preserve regulatory and audit context; do not declare a framework conformance level from policy existence alone. Review assumptions when risk appetite or the technology portfolio changes.
## Checks
- Each selected objective traces to a material enterprise or IT-related goal.
- Governance oversight and management execution have distinct accountable roles.
- Measures include value, risk, and resource outcomes rather than service metrics alone.
- The design identifies evidence required to test control operation.
- The roadmap focuses on material gaps and includes a review forum and cadence.
## Failure Modes
- Installing a large process catalogue without linking any objective to board priorities.
- Assigning the same executive to approve, operate, and assure a critical control.
- Measuring ticket closure while leaving investment-value decisions ungoverned.
- Calling a written policy an effective control before testing whether teams follow it.
