# Turnaround Strategy
## Purpose
Design a sequenced stabilize-and-recover strategy that restores cash control, resets economics, and rebuilds a viable core business. It converts a distressed situation into linked cash, operating, portfolio, and stakeholder actions with milestones.
## Use
- Designing a full stabilize-fix-grow path for a distressed or underperforming business.
- Aligning board, lenders, and management on sequenced operational and portfolio actions.
- Moving beyond emergency cash control to a recoverable economics model.
## Avoid
- Cash runway is days-to-weeks and uncontrolled—use liquidity-management first.
- The only decision is sell a discrete non-core asset—use asset-divestiture-strategy or divestiture-analysis.
- The firm is healthy and seeking growth options—use portfolio-strategy-analysis or ansoff-matrix.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Cash and liquidity status with near-term covenants and facilities; segment economics, cost structure, and root causes of underperformance; portfolio options (keep, fix, harvest, exit) with rough value and feasibility; stakeholder constraints (lenders, owners, unions, regulators); management capacity and time horizon for recovery milestones.

**Nice-to-have:** customer churn by segment, workforce agreements, covenant model, asset sale options, and leadership succession plans.

**When data is thin:** protect liquidity first, use a 13-week cash view, and stage irreversible closures behind rapid commercial and operational validation.
## Procedure
1. **Establish the survival perimeter.** Confirm cash runway, covenant dates, essential payments, lender conditions, and decision authority. Create a fact base that distinguishes immediate liquidity actions from structural recovery work.
2. **Diagnose the economic break.** Analyze segment contribution, customer and site performance, fixed-cost absorption, working capital, and operational root causes. Identify which losses are temporary shocks versus persistent economics.
3. **Choose the viable core.** Classify businesses, products, sites, and capabilities as protect, repair, harvest, divest, or exit. Test options against cash effect, execution speed, stakeholder constraints, and strategic coherence.
4. **Build linked recovery waves.** Sequence stabilization actions, cost reset, commercial repair, portfolio moves, and capability changes across 30, 90, and 365 days. Quantify owners, milestones, cash impact, and contingency triggers.
5. **Mobilize and govern.** Agree the plan with board and lenders, launch a weekly value-delivery cadence, track leading indicators, and revise quickly when milestones or assumptions fail.
## Output Contract
The plan contains a liquidity guardrail, diagnosis of value destruction, viable-core choice, portfolio decisions, operating and commercial initiatives, milestone cash bridge, stakeholder commitments, risk register, and governance calendar. It states which actions are reversible and which require formal approval. It must offer a credible route to sustainable economics, not a list of isolated cuts.
## Evidence
Use bank balances, facility terms, management accounts, customer orders, and site data as observed evidence. Label a forecast cost saving as an assumption until a contract, role change, or spend stop verifies it. Separate cash timing from annual P&L benefit. Treat management forecasts from an earlier failed plan as hypotheses requiring revalidation. Record union, regulatory, and lender constraints explicitly because they may determine feasibility.
## Checks
- The short-term cash bridge reconciles to facilities and covenant definitions.
- Each initiative has a quantified cash and P&L effect, timing, owner, and dependency.
- Portfolio choices are compared against a defined viable-core rationale.
- The plan includes downside triggers and actions if recovery lags.
- Board, lender, and operating milestones use one consistent timetable.
## Failure Modes
- Announcing a percentage cost target without changing the cost base.
- Treating asset sales as recurring operating recovery.
- Protecting every legacy business to avoid difficult portfolio decisions.
- Counting gross savings before severance, closure, or revenue loss.
- Sharing a plan with lenders before management has capacity to execute it.
