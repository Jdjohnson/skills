# Operational Due Diligence
## Purpose
Independently assess a deal target’s operational capability, cost position, capacity, and execution risk for investment decision. It tests whether the operating facts support the deal thesis before capital is committed.
## Use
- Assessing a target’s operating model, capacity, cost position, and process maturity pre-deal
- Validating operational synergy and investment assumptions before bid or close
- Identifying integration, capex, and risk items financial and commercial DD will miss
## Avoid
- The primary unknown is market demand and competitive position—use commercial-due-diligence
- You need continuous improvement design without a transaction—use value-stream-mapping or lean-manufacturing
- IT systems and cyber are the gating risks—use it-due-diligence
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Site footprint, capacity, utilization, and major process maps; cost structure, productivity KPIs, quality and service metrics; supply base criticality, inventory policy, and logistics constraints; capex backlog, maintenance condition, and known operational risks; deal thesis operational assumptions (volume, cost-out, footprint).

**Nice-to-have:** Customer complaints, union agreements, environmental permits, maintenance work orders, and an early integration hypothesis.

**When data is thin:** Use a focused site walk, management challenge session, and document request list to identify the high-value unknowns; give no false comfort from management-prepared slides.
## Procedure
1. **Translate the thesis into tests.** Convert volume growth, margin expansion, synergy, and footprint claims into operational questions with evidence thresholds. Output: diligence test plan.
2. **Build a fact base before visits.** Reconcile reported capacity, throughput, labor, quality, service, and cost information; flag contradictions for on-site testing. Output: issue log and site agenda.
3. **Walk the operation.** Observe flows, bottlenecks, staffing, maintenance condition, inventory, and safety controls; interview operators as well as management. Output: site observations tied to thesis claims.
4. **Stress the operating model.** Test whether stated capacity supports forecast volume, whether unit cost includes constraints, and whether suppliers or capex create downside. Output: risk-and-upside model.
5. **Form investment implications.** Rate issues by value, probability, timing, and mitigability; state bid, condition-precedent, integration, and capital implications. Output: decision-ready diligence report.
## Output Contract
Produce a transaction-timed operational report that traces each material investment assumption to evidence, a risk rating, and a financial or execution implication. Include site-level capacity and cost findings, supply vulnerabilities, capex needs, synergy feasibility, and a 100-day issue list. The report helps an investment committee decide price, protections, or walk-away conditions.
## Evidence
Classify management representations, data-room records, site observations, and independent calculations separately. Preserve the period and source behind every operational KPI. If production data cannot be reconciled, use a downside range rather than a central estimate. Keep value estimates distinct from operational facts, because a plausible saving is not a realized synergy.
## Checks
- Every deal thesis claim has a test, owner, evidence status, and conclusion.
- Site observations challenge at least the major capacity and cost assumptions.
- Capacity is tested against product mix, downtime, staffing, and maintenance constraints.
- Risk ratings translate into a bid adjustment, protection, integration action, or explicit residual exposure.
- Findings separate pre-close remedies from issues that can only be fixed after ownership changes.
## Failure Modes
- Accepting annual nameplate capacity while ignoring changeovers, yield loss, and labor availability.
- Counting cost synergies twice in both procurement and plant-consolidation cases.
- Touring the cleanest site and extrapolating it to a multi-site network.
- Treating deferred maintenance as ordinary operating expense when it requires immediate capital.
