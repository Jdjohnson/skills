# Minimum Viable Product
## Purpose
Design the smallest product customers can actually use that maximizes validated learning per unit of build effort. It turns a risky value hypothesis into a safe, usable release with instrumentation, rather than a demo that merely collects opinions.
## Use
- You need the smallest real offering that tests a critical value or growth hypothesis
- Scope must be cut to maximize learning per unit of build effort
- Early revenue, waitlist, or usage evidence is required before full product investment
## Avoid
- You only need concept feedback, not real usage—use rapid-prototyping or design-sprints
- Compliance or brand risk forbids a thin release—use stage-gate-process with a fuller pilot
- PMF is already proven and the need is multi-quarter sequencing—use product-roadmap-development
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Primary customer segment and job-to-be-done or problem statement; single riskiest product or value hypothesis to test; non-negotiable quality, legal, and brand constraints; success metrics that would justify further investment.

**Nice-to-have:** baseline behavior, willingness-to-pay evidence, support capacity, and a reachable early-adopter pool.

**When data is thin:** choose a narrower cohort and a reversible release mechanism. Do not replace actual use with a favorable focus-group reaction.
## Procedure
1. **Write the falsifiable bet.** State the customer, job, proposed value, and observable behavior that would support or weaken the hypothesis. Identify the one assumption whose failure makes further build wasteful.
2. **Map the minimum end-to-end journey.** Draw the path from entry to successful job completion. Keep only steps necessary for a customer to receive value; list manual operations and guardrails separately from product features.
3. **Cut scope by learning, not convenience.** Remove adjacent personas, integrations, preferences, and scale features. Preserve reliability, privacy, payment, and support obligations that make the release honest and safe.
4. **Instrument the release.** Define exposure, activation, task completion, repeat use, and qualitative follow-up events before development. Set a decision threshold and a time box.
5. **Ship to the cohort and decide.** Observe behavior, investigate drop-offs, and compare results with the threshold. Choose to persevere, revise the hypothesis, or stop; do not quietly turn the experiment into a broad launch.
## Output Contract
An MVP release brief with a falsifiable hypothesis, target cohort, minimum customer journey, explicit in-scope and excluded capabilities, operational workarounds, safety constraints, instrumentation plan, threshold, and decision date. It must define a real customer outcome and the evidence needed for the next investment decision.
## Evidence
Observed evidence is logged customer behavior, paid conversion, and support outcomes. Interviews explain behavior but do not substitute for it. Label assumptions about addressable demand, onboarding, and manual fulfillment. Do not count signups as value realization unless the hypothesis is specifically about acquisition. Preserve raw event definitions so later teams cannot redefine activation after seeing results.
## Checks
- One high-risk hypothesis governs scope.
- A customer can complete the promised job without staff pretending the product works.
- Exclusions do not violate legal, security, accessibility, or trust obligations.
- Metrics distinguish exposure from completed value and repeated value.
- The team has a pre-agreed stop or scale threshold.
## Failure Modes
- Releasing a clickable prototype and calling preference feedback validation.
- Adding every stakeholder’s feature until the learning cycle takes a year.
- Omitting the one reliability feature needed for the customer to finish the job.
- Manually rescuing every failed flow and reporting the journey as successful.
- Treating a vanity waitlist as evidence of retention or willingness to pay.
