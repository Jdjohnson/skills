# Disruptive Innovation
## Purpose
Diagnose whether a challenger can displace incumbents by serving overlooked or overshot customers along a different performance trajectory. The analysis distinguishes a true foothold-and-improvement path from any startup that happens to be novel or cheaper.
## Use
- Diagnosing whether a challenger follows a disruptive path (low-end or new-market)
- Assessing incumbent vulnerability to trajectory-based displacement
- Separating sustaining innovation races from disruptive threats
## Avoid
- You need to invent uncontested market space by redesigning value factors—use blue-ocean-strategy
- The issue is classical cost vs differentiation positioning among peers—use generic-strategies
- You only need a point-in-time industry attractiveness score—use five-forces
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Performance trajectories valued by mainstream vs non-consuming / low-end customers; Challenger business model, cost structure, and initial beachhead; Incumbent sustaining-innovation roadmap and margin structure; Evidence on overshoot of customer needs in the core market; Channel and resource-allocation patterns that favor high-end projects.

**Nice-to-have:** cohort retention, channel incentives, customer switching stories, and technical cost curves.

**When data is thin:** identify the foothold hypothesis but do not label it disruptive until customer and trajectory evidence is obtained.
## Procedure
1. Define the incumbent’s mainstream performance metric and plot what established customers need against what incumbents supply over time. Look for overshoot, not merely dissatisfaction.
2. Identify the challenger’s entry foothold: a low-end segment incumbents find unattractive or nonconsumers who could not previously participate. Describe the job, price, convenience, and initial compromise.
3. Test asymmetry of motivation. Examine whether incumbent margins, channels, and resource-allocation rules make the foothold rationally unattractive to pursue.
4. Compare trajectories: can the challenger improve performance while retaining its cost or access advantage, and can it eventually meet mainstream needs? Separate evidence from speculation.
5. Classify the threat as sustaining, disruptive, adjacent, or unproven; specify leading indicators and an incumbent response that fits the classification rather than reflexive imitation.
## Output Contract
An assessment with a trajectory chart, foothold definition, overshoot evidence, challenger economics, incumbent incentive analysis, disruption classification, confidence rating, and watch indicators. It must state why the entrant can or cannot move upmarket and identify the customer segment most likely to signal that move first.
## Evidence
Observed evidence includes customer performance requirements, prices, usage behavior, margin data, and actual adoption by a foothold. The projected improvement curve and future mainstream fit are inferences. Assumed cost declines, channel access, and regulatory permission require a testable condition. Unknown economics or product performance should result in “unproven,” not a dramatic disruption claim.
## Checks
- The challenger has a specific low-end or new-market foothold, not just a lower price.
- Overshoot is shown from customer requirements and incumbent supply data.
- The assessment distinguishes incumbent inability from a deliberate refusal driven by incentives.
- The challenger’s path to mainstream adequacy has identified technical or business mechanisms.
- Watch indicators can falsify the classification within a stated time period.
## Failure Modes
- Calling a premium entrant disruptive because it uses a newer technology.
- Mistaking an incumbent’s temporary feature gap for a trajectory that abandons overserved customers.
- Recommending a full-price imitation before proving that the new-market user will migrate.
