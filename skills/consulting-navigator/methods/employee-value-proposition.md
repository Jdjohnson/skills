# Employee Value Proposition
## Purpose
Design a distinctive, deliverable employee offer—rewards, work, growth, and purpose—that target talent will believe and the organization can actually keep. The work aligns the hiring promise with lived experience, so employer messaging is backed by operating choices rather than slogans.
## Use
- Defining the distinctive mix of rewards, work, growth, and purpose you will deliberately offer and deliver.
- Aligning talent attraction messaging with the lived employee experience.
- Prioritizing people investments against what target talent segments actually value.
## Avoid
- You need culture diagnosis before you know what experience you currently deliver—use culture-assessment first.
- You only need market-priced job grades—use job-analysis-and-evaluation.
- The problem is onboarding execution for new hires already accepted—use onboarding-framework.
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Target talent segments and competitor employer offers.
- Employee and candidate evidence on what attracts, retains, and frustrates.
- Current rewards, benefits, career, and culture reality (not just aspirations).
- Business constraints on cost and brand authenticity.
- Existing employer-brand assets and careers messaging.

**Nice-to-have:** offer acceptance data, tenure by segment, recruiting funnel loss reasons, and internal mobility patterns.

**When data is thin:** interview current employees and recent candidates before writing claims; restrict the promise to proof points that leaders can demonstrate now.
## Procedure
1. **Segment the talent market.** Identify scarce or strategically important talent groups rather than designing for an abstract “employee.” Capture their career stage, alternatives, and deal-breakers. Output: target-segment profiles.
2. **Audit the lived offer.** Map compensation, flexibility, leadership, purpose, growth, and daily work against actual employee evidence. Flag where policies differ by unit or manager. Output: experience reality map.
3. **Compare competing choices.** Review credible competitor offers and the reasons candidates accept, decline, or leave. Look for whitespace that is valuable and feasible, not merely fashionable. Output: differentiated opportunity set.
4. **Make offer choices.** Select a few pillars with a tangible employee benefit, supporting proof, and delivery owner. Explicitly identify what the firm will not promise. Output: EVP architecture.
5. **Test believability.** Ask employees, candidates, recruiters, and line managers whether each claim feels true and distinctive. Revise claims that need qualification or cannot survive a manager’s behavior. Output: validated message and proof library.
6. **Fund delivery.** Translate pillars into benefit, manager, career, and communication changes with milestones. Output: delivery roadmap and measures.
## Output Contract
An employee offer architecture with target segments, chosen pillars, specific employee benefits, proof points, messages, delivery owners, investment choices, and measures of credibility. It must state both the external promise and the internal changes required to make that promise true.
## Evidence
Use employee interviews, candidate feedback, offer data, and current policies as evidence. Mark competitor claims as unverified unless checked with multiple sources. Treat desired brand attributes as assumptions until employees can point to a lived proof. Do not present an aspirational leadership behavior as an existing benefit.
## Checks
- Each pillar matters to at least one target segment and is distinct from generic pay claims.
- Every message has a real, current proof point or a dated delivery commitment.
- Segment needs do not conflict with affordability or operating constraints without an explicit choice.
- Recruiter, manager, and careers-site language uses the same promise.
- Measures include credibility or retention, not just campaign reach.
## Failure Modes
- Rebranding ordinary benefits as a differentiator without evidence that candidates care.
- Promising flexibility while managers still reward physical presence.
- Designing one message for executives, engineers, and frontline staff despite different needs.
- Treating a glossy careers page as delivery of the employee offer.
- Copying a competitor’s purpose language that employees cannot substantiate.
