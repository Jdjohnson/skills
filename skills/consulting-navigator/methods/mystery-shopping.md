# Mystery Shopping
## Purpose
Capture objective, scenario-based evidence of live service quality by sending trained secret shoppers through defined customer journeys. It tests whether a standard is delivered under normal conditions, rather than asking employees how they believe service works.
## Use
- Customers complain about service but internal audits look fine
- You need comparable evidence of service execution across locations, channels, or competitors
- Brand or compliance standards must be observed during real customer interactions
## Avoid
- You need employees’ motivations or culture in depth—use ethnographic-research or interviews
- Customer consent or the required scenario would create an unacceptable ethical or legal risk
- The question is a single journey’s process redesign rather than compliance with a defined standard—use service-blueprinting
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined service standards or brand behaviors to score; customer journey scenarios and shopper eligibility rules; sample locations, channels, and time periods; scoring rubric and evidence requirements; legal, privacy, and safety constraints.

**Nice-to-have:** historical results, customer complaint themes, staffing patterns, and recordings or receipts allowed by policy.

**When data is thin:** sample fewer locations but repeat the same scenario across dayparts; never generalize one visit as the chain’s average experience.
## Procedure
1. **Turn standards into observable moments.** Convert statements such as “provide warm service” into behaviors a shopper can see or hear: greeting within a defined period, identity check completed, or recovery option offered.
2. **Write realistic scenarios.** Specify shopper profile, need, prompts, purchases, permitted deviations, and safety stop rules. Pilot the scenario so it does not reveal the study or demand impossible staff actions.
3. **Build a defensible sample.** Stratify locations and visits by geography, volume, channel, and daypart. Assign trained shoppers who meet the scenario criteria and have no conflict with the site.
4. **Field and document immediately.** Shoppers record timestamps, observable behavior, receipt or permitted supporting evidence, and verbatim facts before interpretation. Supervisors check completeness and scenario adherence.
5. **Score, calibrate, and act.** Apply the rubric, compare sites only when scenarios are comparable, investigate outliers, and translate patterns into coaching, process, or policy actions. Repeat sampling to confirm improvement.
## Output Contract
A service scorecard with scenario definitions, sample frame, visit dates, item-level observations, rubric scores, evidence references, confidence limits, site or channel comparisons, and corrective actions. It must distinguish what the shopper observed from what the analysis concludes and should not rank sites with incompatible scenarios.
## Evidence
Timestamped observations and permitted artifacts are observed evidence. A score is an interpretation under the rubric; retain the underlying note for review. State assumptions about representativeness, shopper consistency, and normal operating conditions. Protect employee privacy and follow local recording, disclosure, and labor rules. Do not infer intent from a missed greeting or use a disguised visit to investigate a serious safety allegation that requires formal escalation.
## Checks
- Every score item maps to a defined, observable standard.
- Scenario scripts and shopper training are calibrated before fieldwork.
- The sample covers the decision-relevant location and time variation.
- Evidence notes support every severe noncompliance finding.
- Results separate repeatable pattern from isolated service encounter.
## Failure Modes
- Asking shoppers to rate vague impressions without behavioral anchors.
- Comparing a Saturday rush visit with a quiet weekday appointment as if conditions match.
- Alerting store managers so heavily that the visits stop representing normal execution.
- Treating one shopper’s personal preference as a brand-standard failure.
- Publishing employee names in a report where coaching, not discipline, is the purpose.
