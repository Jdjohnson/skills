# Change Readiness Assessment
## Purpose
Diagnose whether and where an organization is willing and able to absorb a specific change so leaders can time, scope, and resource the effort realistically. The assessment tests the conditions for a particular rollout, not an organization’s culture in the abstract.
## Use
- Evaluating organizational willingness and capability to absorb a proposed change before committing scale and timing
- Comparing readiness across units, roles, or sites to sequence rollout
- Identifying structural barriers—sponsorship, capacity, history, skills—before detailed change design
## Avoid
- You already know readiness is low and need a full change program design—use organizational-change-management or kotters-8-step-process
- You need deep culture diagnosis independent of a specific change—use culture-assessment
- You need person-level adoption gap scoring mid-implementation—use adkar-model
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined change scope, timing, and affected populations; sponsorship map and recent change history; capacity signals (competing initiatives, workload, skills); stakeholder perception evidence (survey, interviews, or focus groups).

**Nice-to-have:** previous rollout results, local operational metrics, union or regulatory constraints, and planned communications.

**When data is thin:** interview a balanced sample from each affected unit and classify scores as provisional; do not turn a small vocal group into an organization-wide verdict.
## Procedure
1. **Set the decision boundary.** Specify the change, affected groups, intended launch window, and decision that the assessment will inform: proceed, sequence, reduce scope, or delay.
2. **Define readiness dimensions and anchors.** Use a small rubric covering sponsorship, understanding and willingness, capacity and competing load, skills and resources, and local history. Describe what low, medium, and high evidence looks like for each dimension.
3. **Collect comparable evidence.** Combine sponsor interviews, manager and employee input, change calendars, workload data, training records, and prior-program evidence. Sample each site or role consistently so a large site does not dominate the result.
4. **Score by population, then challenge the scores.** Rate each dimension with a confidence tag, look for contradictions, and test whether weak sponsorship or overload explains the pattern. Keep dissenting evidence visible rather than averaging it away.
5. **Translate the profile into rollout choices.** Create a heat map of units, identify hard gates and remediations, and recommend a sequence with conditions for moving to the next wave.
## Output Contract
Deliver a unit- and role-level readiness report with the defined change, scored dimensions, evidence citations, confidence, barriers, decision implications, and a go, adjust, or delay recommendation for each rollout wave. Include specific readiness conditions that must be met before launch; a single enterprise average is insufficient.
## Evidence
Record calendar load, staffing, sponsorship actions, and survey counts as observed. Treat interpretations of willingness or local trust as inferred and preserve contrary interview evidence. State assumptions about response representativeness and what a score threshold means. If the first wave is chosen for convenience rather than readiness, label that trade-off explicitly. Refresh the assessment after material scope, leadership, or timing changes.
## Checks
- Ratings compare like populations and use defined anchors.
- Every red rating has an owner, remedial action, and recheck date.
- Capacity includes concurrent initiatives, not merely headcount.
- Sponsor commitment is tested through visible actions and decisions.
- The launch recommendation changes when a material readiness condition is unmet.
## Failure Modes
- Averaging a ready headquarters with exhausted field sites until both appear moderately ready.
- Calling a communication-plan gap “resistance” without testing workload, skills, or sponsorship.
- Using a pulse survey after the launch decision is already irreversible.
- Treating an old failed program as proof that every new initiative will fail.
