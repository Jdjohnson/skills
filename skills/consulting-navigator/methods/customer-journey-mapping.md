# Customer Journey Mapping
## Purpose
Decompose the customer's end-to-end path into stages, actions, emotions, and pain points across touchpoints. It gives teams one current-state account of what a person actually does, including detours outside the company’s funnel.
## Use
- Decomposing the customer's end-to-end path across stages and channels
- Locating emotion peaks, friction, and ownership gaps before redesign
- Aligning cross-functional teams on a shared current-state picture
## Avoid
- You are ready to redesign the experience system—use customer-experience-design
- You need detailed internal process and support systems behind the curtain—use service-blueprinting
- You only need archetypal user profiles, not the path—use customer-persona-development
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined persona or segment for the map; Touchpoint and channel inventory across pre-purchase to loyalty; Qualitative evidence (interviews, support notes, observation); Quantitative stage metrics (conversion, time, drop-off, effort) where available.

**Nice-to-have:** Search terms, clickstream paths, call recordings, complaint categories, and competitor alternatives customers mention.

**When data is thin:** Build a clearly marked draft from five to eight customer accounts, then validate its riskiest stages with observation or interviews before acting on it.
## Procedure
1. **Choose one traveler and episode.** Specify whose journey is being mapped, their starting trigger, desired outcome, and endpoint. Do not combine buyers, users, and administrators into an “average” route.
2. **Reconstruct the chronological path.** Gather accounts of actions, questions, channels, waits, and workarounds. Name stages in customer language, including research done on third-party sites and pauses between company interactions.
3. **Make each stage inspectable.** For every stage, record customer goal, action, touchpoint, information received, elapsed time, emotion, friction, and internal owner. Attach a metric only to the stage it describes.
4. **Locate discontinuities.** Compare interview evidence with behavioral data; mark repeated contacts, abandoned steps, expectation gaps, and channel switches. Separate a symptom from the preceding cause where possible.
5. **Validate and prioritize.** Walk the map with customers and frontline staff, correct disputed sequence, then rank pain points by frequency, severity, and strategic importance. Preserve unresolved disagreements as research questions.
## Output Contract
A current-state journey map for one defined persona and episode, with stages, customer actions, touchpoints, goals, emotions, frictions, metrics, evidence references, and accountable internal owners. It includes a short list of high-priority opportunity areas and unanswered questions. It diagnoses the path; it does not claim to be a future experience design or a backstage operating map.
## Evidence
Use direct quotes, observations, and event data to support steps in the journey. Label team recollections and inferred emotions rather than presenting them as fact. Keep customer effort distinct from internal effort. If stages vary by segment or channel, map the important variants instead of smoothing them into a false single path.
## Checks
- The start trigger and successful endpoint are visible and meaningful to the customer.
- Stages are ordered by the customer’s time sequence, not department ownership.
- Every major pain point has supporting evidence or a validation flag.
- Emotional lows, waits, and rework are represented alongside conversions.
- An owner is named for each material touchpoint, even where ownership is contested.
## Failure Modes
- Drawing the company sales funnel and calling it a customer journey.
- Combining contradictory personas so that no real customer recognizes the map.
- Omitting offline research, waiting, or support contacts because they lack analytics.
- Turning the map into a solution backlog before validating the problem sequence.
