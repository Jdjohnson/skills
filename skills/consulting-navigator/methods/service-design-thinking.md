# Service Design Thinking
## Purpose
Apply design thinking across frontstage and backstage to create coherent multi-touchpoint service experiences.
## Use
- Redesigning multi-touchpoint services spanning people, process, and digital.
- Aligning frontstage experience with backstage operations and partners.
- Creating a coherent service concept before blueprinting or roadmap work.
## Avoid
- The offer is a pure physical or digital product with no service system—use design-thinking or minimum-viable-product.
- You only need a process map of an existing service—use service-blueprinting or customer-journey-mapping alone.
- The problem is cost efficiency of a stable process—use value-stream-mapping or lean-manufacturing.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined service journey and customer segments; evidence of pain points across channels and touchpoints; visibility into backstage roles, systems, and partner handoffs; constraints on brand, regulation, cost, and delivery capacity.

**Nice-to-have:** Diary studies, service complaints, staff ideas, prototype environments, and measures of channel-switching behavior.

**When data is thin:** Begin with exploratory observation and interviews. Treat early personas and journey hypotheses as working material, not requirements.
## Procedure
1. Frame a service challenge around a customer outcome and a delivery context, such as helping a small-business owner resolve a failed payment without repeating information.
2. Research people in context across channels. Capture their goals, workarounds, emotions, accessibility needs, and the staff or partner constraints behind the interaction.
3. Synthesize insights into journey tensions and “how might we” questions that connect customer value to operational feasibility. Choose a focused opportunity rather than redesigning the whole enterprise.
4. Co-create several service concepts using role-play, storyboards, service sketches, and rough prototypes. Include the backstage capability, information flow, and policy needed for each visible promise.
5. Test prototypes with customers and delivery staff. Observe comprehension, completion, trust, effort, and operational strain; revise the concept after each learning cycle.
6. Select a concept with explicit desirability, feasibility, and viability evidence, then hand it to service blueprinting and implementation planning.
## Output Contract
Deliver a validated service concept that includes the target segment, key journey moments, customer proposition, frontstage interactions, required backstage capabilities, prototype evidence, unresolved risks, and design principles. It should show why the chosen concept is preferable to alternatives and what must be proven next. It is a deliberately testable service proposition, not a finished operating manual or a map of the current process.
## Evidence
Keep direct participant observations separate from design-team interpretation. Recruit across materially different needs rather than averaging a single vocal customer. State which service promises are tested in a prototype and which depend on untested policy, technology, or partner change. Do not claim adoption from stated preference alone; record what participants actually attempted in the prototype.
## Checks
- Research covers both customer experience and delivery-side constraints.
- Concepts specify an exchange of value, not merely a new interface.
- At least one prototype tests the riskiest service assumption.
- Feedback changes the concept or documents a reason not to change it.
- The handoff identifies owners for people, process, technology, and partner work.
## Failure Modes
- Polishing a digital screen while leaving the handoff that causes the customer to call.
- Asking customers to design the service instead of observing their unmet need.
- Testing a concierge prototype and forgetting the labor required to scale it.
- Selecting the most novel concept before validating regulated or partner-dependent steps.
