# Blue Ocean Strategy
## Purpose
Generate value-innovation options that open new market space by reconstructing buyer factors rather than outperforming rivals on the same ones. The method deliberately changes the offer's value curve and cost structure together.
## Use
- Creating uncontested market space rather than beating rivals in existing demand.
- Reconstructing buyer value factors to break the cost-value trade-off.
- Searching for differentiation that also lowers cost structure.
## Avoid
- Industry is already attractive and you need classical positioning—use generic-strategies.
- The threat is low-end disruption of your core—use disruptive-innovation.
- You need portfolio cash prioritization across existing units—use bcg-matrix.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Industry strategic factors buyers currently compare; current offering profile vs key rivals on those factors; non-customer groups and reasons they refuse the industry; cost drivers tied to over-served factors; constraints on what can be eliminated, reduced, raised, or created.

**Nice-to-have:** willingness-to-pay research, adjacent-market analogues, unit economics, and prototype concepts.

**When data is thin:** use customer interviews and direct observation to validate factors; do not substitute an internal brainstorm for a buyer value curve.
## Procedure
1. **Draw the current strategy canvas.** List the factors the industry competes on and plot the offer and key rivals at their actual level of investment.
2. **Find noncustomer friction.** Examine soon-to-leave buyers, deliberate refusers, and unexplored adjacent users; ask which barriers make the category unattractive.
3. **Challenge the factor set.** Use the Four Actions Framework: eliminate taken-for-granted factors, reduce overdesigned ones, raise neglected value, and create factors the category does not offer.
4. **Build alternative value curves.** Combine the actions into a few coherent curves, each with a named new demand logic rather than a long feature list.
5. **Test strategic sequence.** Check exceptional buyer utility, price accessibility, target cost, and adoption hurdles; revise the curve if any link fails.
6. **Choose and mobilize.** Select the curve with evidence of both demand creation and cost release, then specify experiments, capabilities, and market-entry actions.
## Output Contract
A strategy canvas showing current and proposed value curves, an eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid, noncustomer insight, strategic-sequence tests, target economics, and a prioritized experiment roadmap. It must explain why the new curve attracts demand that existing competitors leave outside the market.
## Evidence
Use customer behavior, competitor offers, and cost data as observed evidence. Treat future demand, willingness to adopt, and cost release as hypotheses until tested. Keep stated preferences distinct from revealed behavior. Document which factors are industry conventions rather than customer requirements.
## Checks
- The canvas includes factors buyers actually encounter, not internal capabilities.
- At least one eliminated or reduced factor creates measurable cost headroom.
- Created and raised factors answer a noncustomer barrier.
- The proposed curve is visibly different, not a slightly improved incumbent profile.
- Price and target cost are tested before a full launch recommendation.
## Failure Modes
- Calling any product innovation a new market space without changing the competing factors.
- Raising every feature level and disguising a cost increase as value innovation.
- Treating noncustomers as a single undifferentiated survey segment.
- Eliminating a factor that is regulatory, safety-critical, or essential to trust.
- Presenting a dramatic curve with no route to target cost or adoption.
