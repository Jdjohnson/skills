# Divestiture Analysis
## Purpose
Evaluate sale, spin-off, or carve-out options for a non-core or underperforming unit and recommend the exit path. It compares hold value and transaction structures after accounting for separation, tax, stranded cost, and remaining-portfolio effects.
## Use
- Evaluating whether to sell, spin, carve out, or retain a business or asset set
- Comparing divestiture structures on value, speed, risk, and remaining-portfolio impact
- Preparing a decision package before banker processes or separation program design
## Avoid
- The goal is to fix underperformance while keeping the unit—use turnaround-strategy
- You only need standalone valuation without strategic exit design—use discounted-cash-flow-valuation or valuation-multiples
- Exit is decided and you need detailed separation execution playbooks only—use asset-divestiture-strategy
- Parenting fit is still untested as the root issue—use corporate-parenting-advantage first
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Unit financials, standalone economics, and intercompany dependencies; Strategic rationale for exit versus hold (fit, capital, risk, regulation); Indicative buyer universe or public-market alternatives and valuation ranges; Separation scope: shared services, IT, contracts, people, and stranded costs; Tax, legal, and regulatory constraints on sale, spin, or carve-out forms.

**Nice-to-have:** customer-transfer risks, management depth, TSA pricing, and synergy estimates for likely buyers.

**When data is thin:** retain a range for separation economics and do not launch a sale process before the perimeter is defined.
## Procedure
1. Establish the disposal perimeter: legal entities, assets, contracts, people, data, shared services, and liabilities. Document what remains with the parent and why.
2. Build a hold case and a standalone case, including lost parent subsidies, stranded overhead, dis-synergies, capex, taxes, and management requirements. Reconcile each to reported financials.
3. Develop feasible structures—trade sale, sponsor sale, spin-off, carve-out, partial sale, or wind-down—and screen them against legal constraints, buyer interest, timing, and strategic rationale.
4. Value each structure on net proceeds and parent value after separation costs, tax, TSA exposure, execution risk, and impact on retained operations. Use scenarios for uncertain buyer price and cost removal.
5. Recommend a path with decision gates, buyer-readiness requirements, high-risk dependencies, and a preliminary separation outline; distinguish the analytical recommendation from the execution program.
## Output Contract
A recommendation including perimeter, hold and exit economics, structure comparison, buyer or market hypotheses, tax and regulatory constraints, stranded-cost treatment, risk-adjusted value range, preferred path, and decision gates. The decision maker must be able to see net value to the parent, not just headline sale proceeds.
## Evidence
Reported financials, contracts, headcount, and dependency inventories are observed. Standalone allocations and buyer synergies are inferred. Sale multiple, tax outcome, cost-removal timing, and TSA duration are assumptions with scenario ranges. Unassigned contracts, disputed liabilities, and unverified separation costs are unknowns that can change the recommended structure.
## Checks
- The disposal perimeter matches the financial and operational analysis.
- Hold, sale, and spin options use comparable time horizons and treatment of costs.
- Stranded costs have named owners and a credible removal or retention plan.
- Headline proceeds are separated from tax, fees, separation spend, and working-capital adjustments.
- The recommendation remains viable under a downside buyer-price case.
## Failure Modes
- Comparing a gross sale offer to a hold case that quietly omits $18 million of corporate allocations.
- Calling a carve-out standalone while it still relies on parent identity, payroll, and data services.
- Selecting a bidder before deciding whether the unit’s contracts and licenses can transfer.
