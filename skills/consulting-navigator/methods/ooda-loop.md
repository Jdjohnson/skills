# OODA Loop
## Purpose
Compress competitive decision-making into a rapid observe–orient–decide–act learning cycle that outpaces rivals. It creates a repeatable tempo for making and revising moves when the environment changes faster than an annual plan.
## Use
- Competitive or crisis contexts where tempo and learning speed matter more than one-shot plans
- Designing a repeatable observe–orient–decide–act cadence for leadership teams
- Diagnosing why a slower decision system is losing to faster rivals
## Avoid
- You need a long-horizon investment valuation under uncertainty—use real-options-analysis or scenario-planning
- The problem is continuous process quality improvement on a stable operation—use pdca-cycle or kaizen
- You need a multi-year strategy cascade and metrics architecture—use strategy-map or balanced-scorecard
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Live or near-real-time observations of market, rival, and internal signals; explicit orientation frames (mental models, assumptions, competitive hypotheses); decision rights and act/experiment channels that can close within the cycle time; feedback on outcomes of prior acts to re-enter observe.

**Nice-to-have:** A competitor move log, preapproved response thresholds, simulation capability, and a decision-room facilitator who can keep the cycle moving.

**When data is thin:** Run shorter cycles with reversible acts and state what would falsify the current orientation; waiting for a complete data pack defeats the method’s purpose.
## Procedure
1. **Set the cycle clock and arena.** Name the competitor, market event, or crisis boundary, then set a cadence that fits the rate of change. Output: decision-loop charter.
2. **Observe without interpretation.** Capture fresh signals such as customer behavior, rival moves, capacity shifts, and field reports with source and timestamp. Output: signal board.
3. **Orient the team.** Compare signals with the current model of the situation, surface conflicting assumptions, and form a small number of actionable hypotheses. Output: orientation brief.
4. **Decide an intent and threshold.** Select the next move, its owner, authority limit, and the observation that will trigger reversal or escalation. Output: decision card.
5. **Act, capture consequences, and restart.** Execute quickly enough for learning to matter, log the response, and feed results into the next observation window. Output: updated loop log and next cycle time.
## Output Contract
Provide an operating decision loop with a visible signal board, orientation hypotheses, decision rights, time limits, active moves, and feedback measures. Each decision card states the intended effect, reversible boundary, owner, and next observation date. The deliverable is a live cadence, not a retrospective action register.
## Evidence
Time-stamp observations and preserve their origin; a late but accurate signal should not quietly overwrite what decision makers knew at the time. Label interpretation separately from the raw signal. Make orientation assumptions contestable, particularly assumptions about a rival’s intent. For emergency action, document the minimum evidence used and the condition for stopping it.
## Checks
- The cycle time is shorter than the relevant market or crisis change interval.
- Decision rights allow action without a second forum reopening the same choice.
- The orientation brief contains disconfirming evidence, not only supporting anecdotes.
- Each act has a measurable response signal and a predeclared review time.
- Lessons from one cycle alter the next orientation rather than accumulating in an archive.
## Failure Modes
- Treating observation as a dashboard refresh while no one challenges the assumptions used to interpret it.
- Escalating every move to a monthly committee, so the loop runs at planning speed.
- Choosing dramatic actions with no reversible boundary or learning threshold.
- Confusing rapid action with random action and skipping the orientation step entirely.
