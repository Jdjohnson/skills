# IT Cost Optimization
## Purpose
Redesign IT spend so run costs fall and remaining investment is directed to services that protect value and enable change. The work treats application, infrastructure, cloud, license, labor, and vendor spend as a service portfolio with explicit service-level guardrails.
## Use
- Reducing IT run cost while protecting services that drive revenue or risk control
- Rebalancing spend across applications, infrastructure, vendors, and labor
- Building a multi-wave IT cost roadmap with savings owners and service-level constraints
## Avoid
- You need full lifecycle economics of a single buy decision—use total-cost-of-ownership
- The problem is enterprise-wide cost structure beyond IT—use cost-optimization-framework or zero-based-budgeting
- You still lack a service or application inventory to cost—map the estate first
## Inputs
**Must-have:** IT cost baseline by tower (apps, infra, cloud, labor, vendors); Application and service inventory with business criticality; Contract and license terms with renewal dates; Demand drivers and growth assumptions for major services; Constraints on risk, SLA, and non-negotiable capabilities.

**Nice-to-have:** Unit-cost benchmarks, cloud billing tags, software-usage telemetry, architecture roadmaps, and supplier performance data.

**When data is thin:** Start with a reconciled top-down tower baseline and a focused deep dive on the largest two towers; label savings as directional until invoices and usage validate them.
## Procedure
1. **Create a cost baseline.** Reconcile general-ledger spend to towers, services, vendors, and run versus change categories. Keep pass-through charges and stranded costs visible.
2. **Connect cost to service value.** Map each material cost pool to applications or services, their criticality, users, demand, and agreed performance. A low-cost system can still be indispensable; an expensive one may be redundant.
3. **Diagnose cost drivers.** Inspect utilization, licenses assigned versus used, cloud consumption, infrastructure age, support tiers, contract commitments, and duplicate capabilities. Separate controllable rate, volume, and scope drivers.
4. **Develop levers and guardrails.** Build initiatives such as rightsizing, retirement, license reclaim, demand reduction, renegotiation, and sourcing changes. Specify the service-level, cyber, compliance, and transition conditions that make each lever safe.
5. **Quantify and sequence.** Estimate gross savings, one-time cost, timing, risk, dependency, and owner. Distinguish cash-releasing savings from cost avoidance and avoid counting a retirement and an outsourcing reduction twice.
6. **Govern realization.** Establish a monthly ledger that tracks committed, implemented, and realized savings against invoices and service measures.
## Output Contract
A cost-reduction roadmap by IT tower and initiative, with baseline spend, driver diagnosis, target service guardrails, gross and net savings, one-time costs, timing, dependencies, risk, owner, and realization evidence. It must make trade-offs explicit rather than presenting a percentage reduction target.
## Evidence
Invoices, contracts, asset inventories, and usage logs are observed; benchmark gaps and retirement feasibility are inferred. Document allocation rules when shared services are included. Use vendor quotes or termination clauses to validate savings claims. Report cash savings separately from avoided future spend and do not treat an unfilled vacancy as recurring savings without an approved operating-model change.
## Checks
- The baseline reconciles to finance and excludes double-counted pass-through cost.
- Every initiative has a named service impact and exit or transition condition.
- Savings use a common baseline period and do not overlap.
- Application retirement includes data retention, interface, and business-owner sign-off.
- Realization tracking tests actual spend and service health after implementation.
## Failure Modes
- Cutting support capacity before identifying services with thin resilience.
- Declaring cloud savings from list price rather than the billed account.
- Counting a license reduction while contractual minimums remain in force.
- Calling deferred maintenance savings when it merely transfers failure risk to the future.
