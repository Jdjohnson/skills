# Culture Assessment
## Purpose
Diagnose current versus preferred culture patterns so leaders can see the gap between stated values and how work actually gets done. A structured culture lens lets teams compare subcultures and decide which behaviors must change for strategy to work.
## Use
- Diagnosing current cultural patterns versus the culture the strategy requires
- Creating a shared, evidence-based culture baseline before change or integration work
- Comparing subcultures across units, sites, or merged organizations
## Avoid
- You only need pulse metrics on motivation and discretionary effort—use employee-engagement-surveying
- You need structural alignment of systems and skills, not culture typology—use 7s-framework
- The immediate need is resolving a specific interpersonal dispute—use conflict-resolution-methods
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Defined organizational unit(s) and respondent sample
- Strategy or operating-model context that implies preferred culture
- Instrument or structured interview protocol (e.g., competing-values items)
- Leadership hypotheses about current vs. desired culture
- Optional behavioral artifacts (recognition norms, decision patterns, stories)

**Nice-to-have:** segmentation by site, tenure, or role; prior survey trends; turnover patterns; and examples of desired behaviors.

**When data is thin:** use a small, representative interview sample and report directional patterns; never present a few leaders’ preferences as an organization-wide culture score.
## Procedure
1. **Frame the culture question.** Define the unit, strategic change, respondent groups, and the behavior or trade-off the assessment must illuminate. Output: culture-assessment charter.
2. **Choose and calibrate the instrument.** Use a consistent culture lens, such as competing values, with current and preferred items; test language so respondents interpret dimensions similarly. Output: instrument and sampling plan.
3. **Collect current and preferred views.** Administer the survey or protocol, protect anonymity, and gather behavioral artifacts that explain responses. Output: response set and evidence log.
4. **Score and plot culture profiles.** Aggregate current and preferred allocations across the culture dimensions, then compare total organization and subgroup profiles. Output: current-versus-preferred profile charts.
5. **Interpret gaps through behavior.** Test which leadership practices, rewards, stories, routines, and decision norms reinforce the profile; prioritize a small set of shifts. Output: culture-gap narrative and intervention priorities.
## Output Contract
A culture profile containing scope and response coverage, current and preferred scores by dimension, subgroup comparisons, observed behavioral evidence, priority gaps, and implications for leadership, rewards, decision making, and change design. It must show both the pattern and the strategic reason a shift matters. It is not an employee-satisfaction report or an all-purpose organization diagnostic.
## Evidence
Report sample size, response rate, instrument changes, and subgroup sizes beside results. Treat numerical profile differences as descriptive unless sampling supports stronger claims. Keep respondents’ preferred culture separate from what strategy actually requires. Use stories and artifacts to explain a score, but label them as qualitative evidence rather than proof of prevalence.
## Checks
- Current and preferred questions use the same dimensions and scoring scale.
- Response coverage is sufficient and gaps are disclosed by unit or role.
- Profile interpretation cites concrete routines, incentives, or decisions.
- Subculture differences are not averaged away when they affect implementation.
- Recommended shifts include a behavioral mechanism, not a new values poster.
## Failure Modes
- Treating the preferred profile as an aspirational vote instead of testing it against strategy.
- Averaging sharply different sites into a bland enterprise culture.
- Using engagement scores as though they measured culture type.
- Publishing a profile without changing leadership routines, rewards, or decision rights.
