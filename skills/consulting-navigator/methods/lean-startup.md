# Lean Startup
## Purpose
Shorten product and business-model development cycles by running build-measure-learn experiments that validate or kill assumptions before scale.
## Use
- Turning unproven product or business-model ideas into falsifiable experiments
- Shortening the cycle from idea to evidence before large build investment
- Steering pivots or perseveres with cohort and conversion data rather than opinions
## Avoid
- Regulated, capital-heavy programs need formal kill/go gates—use stage-gate-process
- You already have clear product-market fit and need delivery sequencing—use product-roadmap-development or agile-product-management
- The question is a one-shot forecast with no product to ship—use market-sizing or scenario-planning
## Inputs
**Must-have:**
- Explicit riskiest assumptions about customer, problem, and value proposition
- Ability to ship a thin experiment or MVP to real users
- Defined success metrics and kill/pivot thresholds
- Access to usage, conversion, or qualitative feedback loops

**Nice-to-have:** prior experiments, a recruitable audience, unit-cost estimates, and a sponsor able to stop a feature.

**When data is thin:** start with interviews or a concierge test, but write the claim that evidence could disprove. Do not treat enthusiasm, sign-ups, or a demo request as durable behavior.
## Procedure
1. **Write the leap-of-faith claims.** Separate the customer problem, promised value, acquisition channel, and revenue logic. Rank them by consequence if wrong and uncertainty. Output: an assumption backlog.
2. **Choose one testable claim.** State a falsifiable prediction, audience, exposure, observation window, and a pre-set pivot, persevere, or stop threshold. Output: experiment charter.
3. **Build the smallest honest test.** Use a landing page, manual service, prototype, price test, or thin product slice that exposes the promised behavior without building unrelated features. Output: test artifact.
4. **Measure a behavior chain.** Track exposure, activation, repeat use, and any payment or referral signal. Inspect the relevant cohort rather than blending early adopters with later traffic. Output: learning record.
5. **Make the learning decision.** Compare the observed result with the threshold, explain confounders, and either retain the hypothesis, change one element deliberately, or retire it. Output: pivot/persevere decision and next experiment.
## Output Contract
Produce a learning board with ranked assumptions, each test charter, the artifact used, cohort metrics, thresholds, results, and the resulting pivot, persevere, or kill call. Each call names the next assumption and owner. The board supports an investment decision; it is not a feature list or product schedule.
## Evidence
Label behavior captured from real users as observed and distinguish it from a stated preference. Record targeting, traffic source, dates, sample size, and instrumentation changes beside each metric. Treat a conversion threshold, cohort definition, and proxy metric as assumptions. If a result is noisy, extend or redesign the test rather than declaring a win from a favorable week. A negative result is useful only when the exposure and measurement were credible.
## Checks
- The top claim could genuinely invalidate the current product or business model.
- The test exposes users to a real trade-off, not only a survey question.
- Success and failure thresholds were fixed before results were reviewed.
- Cohort retention is separated from one-time acquisition or curiosity clicks.
- A pivot changes a named hypothesis; it is not a vague request to “iterate.”
## Failure Modes
- Shipping a polished MVP that tests five claims at once, leaving no diagnosis when it fails.
- Calling vanity traffic a learning metric while ignoring activation or repeat behavior.
- Moving the threshold after seeing weak results.
- Treating one friendly design partner as representative demand.
- Continuing to add features after the riskiest value claim has already failed.
