# Pyramid Principle
## Purpose
Structure any recommendation so the governing answer leads, with vertically logical, grouped supporting arguments beneath it. It turns analysis into an executive argument whose evidence supports each claim.
## Use
- Structuring a recommendation so the answer comes first, then grouped supporting arguments
- Editing a long analysis into an executive-ready argument pyramid
- Aligning a team on one governing thought before writing the storyline
## Avoid
- You still need to open with situation and tension before the answer—use scqa or scr for the narrative arc
- The problem has not been decomposed yet—use issue-tree and mece first
- You need a facilitated idea-generation session rather than a communication structure—use structured-brainstorming
## Inputs
**Must-have:** A clear recommendation or governing answer; evidence and arguments that support or challenge it; audience decision question and time constraint; logical groupings of supporting points (ideally MECE).

**Nice-to-have:** anticipated objections, a decision-rights map, detailed exhibits, speaking time, and an existing draft to restructure.

**When data is thin:** make the governing thought a conditional recommendation, state the critical unknown in the first layer, and ask for the decision needed to obtain the missing evidence.
## Procedure
1. **State the audience question.** Write the exact decision or belief the audience needs to resolve. Establish the answer before arranging evidence.
2. **Draft the governing thought.** Express one complete answer with its action, reason, and qualification. Test whether a senior reader could act from that sentence alone.
3. **Build the key line.** Identify the two to four major reasons that jointly support the governing thought. Group at one level of abstraction, ensure the reasons are collectively sufficient, and remove any point that merely repeats the conclusion.
4. **Test vertical logic.** For every parent claim, ask “Why should I believe this?” and make its children answer that question. If a child answers a different question, move it or rewrite the parent.
5. **Test horizontal logic.** Check whether sibling points are deductive steps in a chain or inductive items in a coherent group. Use parallel wording and a clear ordering rule such as importance, chronology, or mutually exclusive segments.
6. **Attach evidence and edit.** Put analysis, exhibits, facts, and caveats beneath the claim they support. Rewrite titles as assertions, strip history that does not alter the decision, and pressure-test the argument against likely objections.
## Output Contract
An executive argument structure with the governing thought, grouped supporting assertions, evidence beneath each assertion, qualifiers, and a decision request. Each lower layer must explain the layer above without burying the conclusion in background.
## Evidence
Facts, calculations, and quoted decisions are observed and need traceable exhibits. Analytical conclusions are inferences and must not be elevated to a headline without support. State assumptions as qualifiers at the relevant branch, not in a distant appendix. Do not use a logically tidy grouping to conceal material counterevidence.
## Checks
- The first sentence answers the audience’s decision question.
- Each key-line point is a complete assertion, not a topic label.
- Siblings share one organizing principle and do not overlap materially.
- Every major claim has evidence or a clearly marked assumption below it.
- The argument can be summarized in one minute without changing its logic.
## Failure Modes
- Leading with a chronology of analysis when the executive needs a recommendation.
- Grouping “costs, customers, and risks” under a parent that claims they prove the same thing.
- Using MECE labels while the underlying evidence overlaps or leaves a decisive gap.
- Writing slide titles as nouns, forcing readers to infer the conclusion from charts.
- Hiding a caveat in footnotes even though it changes the governing answer.
