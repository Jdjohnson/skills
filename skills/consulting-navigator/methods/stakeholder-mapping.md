# Stakeholder Mapping
## Purpose
Classify people and groups who can shape a decision or change by influence and interest, then turn that into differentiated engagement tactics. The output is a quadrant engagement map so politics and coalition risk become design inputs, not post-launch surprises.
## Use
- You are scoping who matters for a decision, change, or engagement and need a defensible engagement posture per actor.
- Communication has been one-size-fits-all and influential players feel ignored or ambushed.
- Sponsors appear supportive while mid-level actors stall progress; power must be separated from noise.
- You are checking coalition strength before a major recommendation or rollout.
## Avoid
- You need task-level ownership clarity—use a RACI matrix.
- You need a person-level adoption plan—use ADKAR or a resistance management plan.
- The immediate need is running a workshop process, not classifying actors—use facilitated workshops.
- The stakeholder set is trivial (one sponsor, no external parties) and influence is already explicit.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** the decision, project, or change in scope; a candidate stakeholder list (roles, units, external parties); evidence or working judgment on power/influence and interest/stance; known decision rights.
**Nice-to-have:** alliances and conflicts; escalation patterns; informal brokers; prior engagement history.
When evidence is missing, record ratings as provisional and mark high-uncertainty actors for validation interviews.
## Procedure
1. **Frame the mapping purpose.** State the decision or change, the question the map must answer ("Who can block go-live?"), and the time horizon. Output: purpose statement.
2. **Identify actors.** List individuals and groups able to affect outcomes, including quiet blockers and external parties. Output: actor list.
3. **Map relationships and motivations.** Note links among actors (reporting, dependency, coalition, conflict) plus each actor's interest and stance. Output: relationship and stance tags.
4. **Rate influence and interest.** Place each actor on a power–interest grid using evidence or explicit judgment. Output: positioned map.
5. **Derive engagement tactics.** Assign tactics by quadrant—manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor—and note coalition risks. Output: draft engagement map.
6. **Validate.** Pressure-test with the sponsor or a small cross-section; adjust thin-evidence ratings and tactics. Output: validated map ready for communication design.
## Output Contract
A stakeholder map containing: (1) the scoped mapping purpose, (2) an actor inventory with role and unit, (3) power/influence and interest/stance ratings with evidence tags, (4) relationship notes for material alliances or conflicts, and (5) a differentiated engagement tactic per actor or segment. It must support decisions on who to engage, how, and with what priority before workshops, communications, or rollout.
## Evidence
Label each rating observed (decision rights, budget authority, formal veto), inferred (blocking or sponsorship patterns), assumed (org-chart default), or unknown. Never treat loudness as power. When evidence is thin, keep the actor on the map, widen contact, and flag validation needed. Revise when decision rights or coalitions change.
## Checks
- Every actor with material influence appears; no surprise veto holders.
- Power and interest are rated separately; complaint volume is not a proxy for power.
- Each quadrant or segment gets a distinct tactic, not generic "communicate more."
- Material alliances and conflicts are noted.
- Ratings carry evidence tags; provisional judgments are marked.
## Failure Modes
- A pretty grid with no change to who is contacted or when.
- Confusing RACI task ownership with influence strategy.
- Over-weighting formal hierarchy while missing informal brokers.
- A one-time map never revised after politics shift.
- Treating the map as the communication plan instead of its input.
