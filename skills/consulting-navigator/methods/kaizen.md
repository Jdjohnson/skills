# Kaizen
## Purpose
Institutionalize small, frequent, employee-driven experiments that raise process performance and lock gains into updated standards. The emphasis is a daily improvement habit: people closest to the work identify, test, and retain better ways of working.
## Use
- You need sustained, incremental performance gains owned by people who do the work
- Standards exist but drift, and local problems should be fixed at the source daily
- Culture and capability for continuous improvement matter as much as a one-time redesign
## Avoid
- The process is fundamentally broken and needs radical redesign—use business-process-reengineering or value-stream-mapping first
- You only need a single closed-loop experiment structure without shop-floor culture work—use pdca-cycle
- The issue is strategic prioritization of which processes to improve—use hoshin-kanri
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined target process or work cell and current standard work; baseline performance metrics (quality, cost, delivery, safety); frontline participation time and a facilitator or team lead; authority boundary for local changes versus capital or policy changes; a way to capture before/after results and update standards.

**Nice-to-have:** a visual problem board, maintenance support, and a local budget for low-cost changes.

**When data is thin:** use a short observation sheet for the next shifts and test reversible changes first; do not make an anecdote a new standard.
## Procedure
1. **Make abnormality visible.** At the work area, compare actual work to the current standard and display a small number of safety, quality, delivery, and effort measures.
2. **Select a local problem.** Let operators describe the obstacle in observable terms: where it occurs, how often, and how it affects the work. Keep the target within the team’s authority.
3. **Find the immediate cause at the gemba.** Observe the sequence, collect examples, and ask why until the team can change a condition rather than blame a person.
4. **Try a bounded countermeasure.** Run a low-risk experiment for a stated period. Record the prior condition, the alteration, expected effect, and result; escalate capital or policy barriers separately.
5. **Standardize and share.** If the result holds, revise standard work, train the next shift, update the visual control, and review the metric. If it does not hold, retain the learning and choose the next experiment.
## Output Contract
Maintain an improvement register for the work cell with problem statements, observed causes, experiments, before-and-after results, owner, status, and revised standard-work references. The operating plan also defines the daily review rhythm and escalation path for barriers beyond the cell. It demonstrates repeatable improvement capability, not a one-off list of suggestions.
## Evidence
Use direct observation and time-stamped measures for the baseline. Mark a cause as a hypothesis until the experiment changes it and the predicted measure moves. Record operator judgments alongside measurements rather than replacing either. Safety, quality, or regulatory changes require formal approval even when the local result looks favorable.
## Checks
- The improvement owner is a participant in the work, not only a staff sponsor.
- Each experiment names its expected mechanism and review date.
- A successful trial changes the documented standard and is checked on another shift.
- Escalated obstacles remain visible rather than disappearing from the board.
## Failure Modes
- Running a themed event, then returning to unchanged daily management.
- Counting suggestions submitted instead of conditions improved and sustained.
- Declaring a time saving while moving work or burden to the next station.
- Letting supervisors approve changes without the operators who must use them.
