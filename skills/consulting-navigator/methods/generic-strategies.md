# Generic Strategies
## Purpose
Design a coherent competitive position by choosing cost leadership, differentiation, or focus and the trade-offs that sustain it. The framework forces a firm to decide both the advantage it will pursue and the scope in which it can credibly deliver that advantage.
## Use
- Choosing a coherent competitive position: cost leadership, differentiation, or focus.
- Designing strategy after industry structure is understood.
- Forcing explicit trade-offs so the firm is not stuck in the middle.
## Avoid
- You need uncontested new market space rather than a position in current rivalry—use blue-ocean-strategy.
- You still lack industry structure diagnosis—use five-forces first.
- The problem is portfolio cash allocation across units—use bcg-matrix or ge-mckinsey-matrix.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Industry structure and basis of competition; cost position and drivers vs rivals; sources of uniqueness customers will pay for; segment structure if focus is under consideration; organizational ability to sustain one pure strategic logic.

**Choice rule:** a niche is not focus unless activities fit it better than broad rivals can.
## Procedure
1. Define the arena: customer needs, geographic scope, channel, product boundary, and relevant competitors. Use industry evidence to identify which purchase criteria and cost drivers determine rivalry.
2. Assess relative cost position by activity, not only total margin. Identify scale, utilization, procurement, process, design, distribution, and overhead drivers that could support a low-cost offer without destroying acceptable quality.
3. Assess differentiation candidates by testing whether customers perceive and pay for a distinct benefit, whether competitors can imitate it, and which activities, skills, or assets make it repeatable.
4. Test focused segments for distinct needs, economics, access, and defensibility. Decide whether the focused offer wins through lower cost for that segment or differentiated value for that segment.
5. Compare the required activity systems and trade-offs. Reject combinations that demand incompatible choices, such as maximum customization, universal coverage, and the lowest operating cost without a credible mechanism.
6. Select the position and express it as a strategic statement: target scope, advantage, value proposition, key activities, capabilities, metrics, and things the firm will stop doing. Stress-test it against likely rival response and changing industry conditions.
## Output Contract
Deliver a positioning choice with scope, advantage, value proposition, activity system, capability gaps, trade-offs, rival-response risks, and measures. State why alternatives were rejected. It is a set of choices, not a label without operational consequences.
## Evidence
Prices, cost data, customer behavior, and competitor offerings are evidence. Relative cost advantage and willingness to pay are inferences when based on partial data and need a stated confidence level. The selected scope, future demand, and rival response are strategic assumptions. If the company cannot measure a claimed cost advantage or customer premium, treat it as a hypothesis to validate rather than its strategy.
## Checks
- The target customer and competitive set are explicit enough to evaluate fit.
- The advantage has a mechanism in activities, assets, or capabilities, not a slogan.
- Cost leadership does not rely on quality claims that require an unpriced differentiation investment.
- Differentiation identifies a customer-valued benefit and a willingness-to-pay test.
- Focus includes a narrower scope and does not simply rename a small business unit.
## Failure Modes
- Claiming differentiation because the brand uses different language while customers do not pay more or choose more often.
- Promising the lowest price and highest customization without an activity system that resolves the cost conflict.
- Choosing a niche already served more efficiently by a specialist with stronger access.
- Treating a temporary discount campaign as evidence of durable cost leadership.
