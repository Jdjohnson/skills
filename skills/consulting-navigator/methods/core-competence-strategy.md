# Core-Competence Strategy
## Purpose
Design a growth strategy that deploys distinctive core competencies into chosen markets under a unifying strategic intent. It turns a validated advantage into a committed architecture of capabilities, offers, market entries, and investments rather than a loose catalogue of opportunities.
## Use
- Designing growth architecture that deploys distinctive competencies into new product or market arenas.
- Aligning business-unit initiatives around shared strategic intent rather than standalone plans.
- Translating identified core competencies into a concrete multi-year growth and investment agenda.
## Avoid
- You still need to identify and validate what the core competencies are—use core-competencies first.
- The question is industry structure or positioning versus rivals only—use five-forces or generic-strategies.
- You need a pure portfolio role map for businesses without competence logic—use bcg-matrix or ge-mckinsey-matrix.
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Validated core competencies and the customer value they uniquely enable; current product/market footprint and white-space opportunities linked to those competencies; investment, talent, and partner constraints for building or extending competencies; competitive moves that recombine similar capabilities; strategic intent or ambition statement for the planning horizon.

**Nice-to-have:** technology roadmaps, M&A pipeline, capital envelope, capability maturity measures, and scenarios for market evolution.

**When competence proof is weak:** return to core-competencies; strategic architecture cannot rescue a strength that does not travel or matter to customers.
## Procedure
1. State a time-bound strategic intent that names the future customer problem and the distinctive advantage the enterprise intends to compound.
2. Map each validated competence to current offerings, enabling technologies, expertise locations, and the customer benefits it creates.
3. Generate market and product arenas where the competence could be reused or combined. Reject options that merely share a customer or category label.
4. Design the architecture: required competence upgrades, core platforms or products, entry sequence, shared assets, and milestones linking today’s businesses to the ambition.
5. Choose a focused set of growth bets using value, strategic fit, time to build, competitive defensibility, and resource interdependence; explicitly stop unrelated initiatives.
6. Allocate corporate and business-unit responsibilities, investment tranches, talent moves, partnerships, and governance measures that protect cross-unit learning.
## Output Contract
Provide a multi-year strategic architecture with strategic intent, competence-to-arena logic, selected growth bets, capability-building roadmap, resource allocation, sequencing, owners, and kill criteria. It must show how shared learning generates offerings across markets and where the organization will decline to compete. A validated competence inventory or an adjacency scorecard alone is insufficient.
## Evidence
Treat validated customer value and observed deployment history as evidence. Mark future market adoption, partner access, investment availability, and competitor response as assumptions. Do not assume a skill transfers simply because two businesses use similar technology. Make the confidence and dependencies of every major arena explicit.
## Checks
- Each growth bet names the competence it reuses and the customer benefit it extends.
- The intent constrains choices rather than functioning as a slogan.
- Required capability investments precede or accompany the market commitment.
- Cross-business dependencies have one accountable enterprise owner.
- Stopping rules prevent a politically popular but unrelated bet from consuming shared capital.
## Failure Modes
- Renaming every existing business a “core platform” without testing whether it supplies multiple offers.
- Funding ten adjacent experiments with no shared learning agenda.
- Treating a market forecast as proof that the firm can deploy its competence there.
- Leaving essential talent migration to business-unit negotiation after the budget is approved.
- Calling a product pipeline a strategy while omitting the competencies that must deepen.
