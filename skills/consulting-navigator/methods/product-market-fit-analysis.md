# Product-Market Fit Analysis
## Purpose
Measure the degree to which a product satisfies a defined market's demand and whether scale investment is justified. It triangulates repeat behavior, expressed value, and willingness to pay for one segment instead of treating acquisition growth as proof of fit.
## Use
- Judging whether a live product sufficiently satisfies a defined market before scaling
- Diagnosing weak retention, referral, or willingness-to-pay signals
- Setting a go/hold/kill threshold for further product or GTM investment
## Avoid
- There are no real users yet—use lean-startup and minimum-viable-product first
- You need feature trade-offs inside a known PMF—use feature-prioritization or kano-model
- The issue is industry structure, not product fit—use five-forces or market-sizing
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Defined target segment and alternative solutions customers use; usage, retention, and engagement metrics for a live product or MVP; qualitative evidence of value (interviews, support, sales notes); pricing, conversion, or willingness-to-pay indicators where available.

**Nice-to-have:** cohort acquisition source, cancellation reasons, referral data, win/loss interviews, competitor switching evidence, and segment-level gross margin.

**When data is thin:** narrow the claim to one active cohort, collect structured interviews from retained and churned users, and defer scale spending until behavioral evidence catches up.
## Procedure
1. **Declare the market cell.** Define the customer type, use case, geography, price point, and alternative being displaced. Do not aggregate enterprise buyers and self-serve users into one fit verdict.
2. **Choose the value event.** Identify the repeat action that indicates the promised outcome occurred, such as a report shared, order completed, or workflow finished. Calculate activation and repeat use from that event.
3. **Read cohorts, not averages.** Compare retention, frequency, and time-to-value by acquisition month and segment. Look for stable or improving repeat behavior after onboarding rather than total registrations.
4. **Test felt indispensability.** Interview recent adopters, low-engagement users, and churned users with the same questions: problem, alternative, outcome, and reaction to loss of the product. Code evidence by segment and use case.
5. **Check economic pull.** Examine conversion, renewal, expansion, referral, sales cycle, and discount dependence. A loved free tool with no credible payment path may have usage fit but not a scaleable business case.
6. **Set the decision.** Establish a segment-specific threshold and issue go, hold, or kill. Name the binding gap—activation, retention, value proposition, pricing, or acquisition quality—and the next experiment only if it can change the verdict.
## Output Contract
A segment-specific fit scorecard showing market definition, value event, cohort retention and engagement, qualitative evidence, economic signals, confidence limits, and a go/hold/kill recommendation. It must state whether scale is justified and what evidence would reverse that call; it is not a feature wish list.
## Evidence
Product event logs and paid renewals are observed but require a documented definition of active use. Interview themes are inferred from a sample and must retain contradictory responses. State selection bias when only successful customers were reachable. Do not call a vanity metric evidence of fit unless it links to the declared value event or payment behavior.
## Checks
- The verdict applies to a named segment and use case, not “the market.”
- Retention is cohort-based and measured after a meaningful time-to-value interval.
- At least one behavioral, qualitative, and economic signal informs the decision.
- Churned or non-adopting users are represented in the evidence.
- The scale threshold is set before interpreting the results.
## Failure Modes
- Calling high trial volume fit when users abandon the product after onboarding.
- Averaging segments so a loyal niche conceals failure in the intended target market.
- Using a promoter score without investigating whether promoters actually retain or refer.
- Treating aggressive discounts as willingness to pay.
- Launching a feature program before identifying whether the core value event happens at all.
