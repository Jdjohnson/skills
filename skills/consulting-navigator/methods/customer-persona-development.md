# Customer Persona Development
## Purpose
Synthesize research into a small set of fictional-but-evidence-based customer archetypes that focus design and go-to-market choices. A useful persona represents a recurring decision context and its constraints, not a decorative demographic profile.
## Use
- Creating evidence-based archetypes that guide product, content, and sales conversations
- Decomposing a heterogeneous market into a few decision-useful customer types
- Aligning cross-functional teams on goals, contexts, and barriers of target users
## Avoid
- You need job outcomes and switching triggers more than character sketches—use jobs-to-be-done
- You need the path across touchpoints—use customer-journey-mapping
- Personas would be pure fiction with no research base—gather interviews/data first via ethnographic-research or voice-of-the-customer
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Qualitative research (interviews, field notes, support themes); Behavioral or usage patterns that distinguish groups; Demographic/firmographic context only as secondary descriptors; Goals, pains, triggers, and decision criteria per candidate segment.

**Nice-to-have:** Win/loss records, product telemetry, sales-call recordings, lifecycle stage, and accessible-design needs.

**When data is thin:** Publish provisional hypothesis personas with the evidence gaps printed on each profile and fund research before using them for high-cost segmentation.
## Procedure
1. **Set the decision the personas must improve.** Define whether the users will shape positioning, product choices, sales plays, or onboarding. This determines which behavioral differences matter.
2. **Collect and code evidence.** Interview and observe customers across outcomes and contexts; code goals, triggers, constraints, language, decision criteria, and workarounds. Retain disconfirming cases.
3. **Cluster by decision pattern.** Group people whose motivation, environment, and trade-offs recur together. Use demographics only when they explain behavior; reject clusters based solely on age, title, or company size.
4. **Draft archetype profiles.** Give each archetype a memorable descriptive name, context, primary goal, trigger, barriers, evaluation criteria, information sources, and evidence-backed quote or behavior.
5. **Validate and operationalize.** Test whether sales, product, and actual customers recognize the distinctions. Assign a primary persona to key offerings and document decisions that should vary by persona.
## Output Contract
A compact persona set, usually three to five profiles, with the research sample, behavioral basis for each grouping, goals, contexts, triggers, barriers, decision criteria, and implications for product or go-to-market choices. Each profile includes confidence and disconfirming evidence. The artifact supports a choice about whom to serve; it is not a statistical market segmentation claim.
## Evidence
Separate direct customer statements, observed behavior, analyst synthesis, and invented illustrative details. Names and portraits are mnemonic devices, not evidence. Do not infer needs from protected or demographic characteristics. Refresh personas when product use, buyer roles, or market conditions materially change.
## Checks
- Each persona differs in a decision-relevant pattern, not merely biography.
- Evidence includes both qualitative depth and behavior or usage where available.
- A team can name a different message, feature trade-off, or sales approach for each profile.
- Profiles do not stereotype people or imply unsupported prevalence.
- The set covers priority customers without becoming a catalogue of every exception.
## Failure Modes
- Creating a cheerful stock-photo character whose goals came from internal opinion.
- Treating job title as a proxy for a person’s purchase authority or context.
- Making ten personas so no team can remember which one guides a decision.
- Freezing profiles after a product launch changes who uses the service.
