# Jobs to be Done
## Purpose
Diagnose the job customers are trying to get done so offerings compete on progress-making outcomes rather than product attributes. It explains a choice in its triggering circumstances, including the alternative a buyer would use if the offer disappeared.
## Use
- Diagnosing the functional, emotional, and social progress customers hire a product to make
- Reframing competition around job alternatives, not just category rivals
- Grounding value propositions and roadmaps in demand-side jobs rather than demographics
## Avoid
- You only need stated satisfaction scores on known attributes—use kano-model or voice-of-the-customer
- You need journey moments and touchpoints mapped—use customer-journey-mapping
- Evidence streams already conflict and need reconciliation first—use customer-evidence-triangulation
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Recent switchers, users, or non-consumers to interview; context of hire/fire moments (when and why a solution was chosen or abandoned); current offering and known alternatives customers consider; outcome metrics customers use to judge job progress.

**Nice-to-have:** purchase chronology, abandoned-cart or cancellation records, and samples of workarounds.

**When data is thin:** interview a deliberately varied set of recent decisions and retain competing explanations; do not infer a job solely from a persona label or feature request.
## Procedure
1. **Choose a decision window.** Recruit people who recently switched, renewed, rejected, or assembled a workaround. Reconstruct what happened before, during, and after the decision rather than asking what features they want.
2. **Elicit the timeline.** For each story, capture the first struggling moment, the event that made inaction intolerable, alternatives considered, anxieties about change, and habits that preserved the old approach.
3. **Separate forces.** Code evidence into push from the present situation, pull toward a new solution, anxiety about the new choice, and inertia. A recurring feature request may be a symptom, not the desired progress.
4. **Write job statements.** State the actor, circumstance, progress sought, and success criterion. Distinguish the functional task from emotional reassurance and social consequences; merge statements only when their circumstances and success measures match.
5. **Map the real competitive set.** Include manual work, delay, internal tools, and non-consumption. Identify underserved outcomes, switching barriers, and messages or offer changes that reduce those barriers.
## Output Contract
Deliver a job portfolio with decision timelines, evidence-backed job statements, forces-of-progress maps, alternative solutions, and an opportunity assessment. Each job must name its context and success criteria, not merely a demographic or product category. The final brief should indicate which proposition, onboarding, or roadmap hypotheses deserve testing and which remain unsupported.
## Evidence
Treat verbatim decision stories, timestamps, and observed workaround behavior as direct evidence. Treat the force assigned to a quote as an interpretation and retain the quote reference beside it. Mark a proposed job as tentative when only one interview supports it. Do not convert importance ratings into proof of causation; seek a contrasting case where the same buyer chose a different alternative.
## Checks
- Every job is anchored to a specific circumstance and a measurable definition of progress.
- At least one rejected option or workaround appears for each important job.
- The timeline explains both the trigger to switch and the friction that delayed switching.
- Functional, emotional, and social dimensions are not collapsed into a feature list.
## Failure Modes
- Interviewing loyal power users only, then mistaking usage preferences for market demand.
- Treating a customer segment such as “small businesses” as a job.
- Coding the product category as the competitive set and missing spreadsheets, assistants, or doing nothing.
- Writing an aspirational outcome that no observed decision story supports.
