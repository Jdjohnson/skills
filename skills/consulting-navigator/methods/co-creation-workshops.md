# Co-creation Workshops
## Purpose
Generate solution concepts by facilitating customers and stakeholders to design together rather than react to finished proposals. It makes participant experience an active design material and creates ownership before investment decisions harden.
## Use
- Designing offerings or experiences with customers, partners, or frontline staff in the room
- Building shared ownership of solution concepts before investment
- Turning insight into concrete concepts through facilitated collaboration
## Avoid
- You need a time-boxed five-day prototype and test cycle—use design-sprints
- Stakeholders are unavailable and pure expert design is required—use design-thinking or service-design-thinking without co-creation
- The need is only structured ideation without users present—use structured-brainstorming or facilitated-workshops
## Inputs
**Must-have:** Clear design challenge and success criteria; recruited participants (customers, partners, or employees) with relevant lived experience; facilitator agenda, prompts, and co-design materials; prior insight or constraints to ground the session.

**Nice-to-have:** accessibility needs, incentive plan, decision maker attendance, current journey evidence, and prototype materials.

**When data is thin:** use the workshop to generate hypotheses, not validation; recruit participants to fill the largest experience gaps before interpreting the artifacts.
## Procedure
1. **Frame a shared design challenge.** Turn the business problem into a participant-readable question, show the non-negotiable constraints, and define what tangible artifacts must exist by the end of the session.
2. **Recruit for relevant difference.** Build a mixed group of users, frontline staff, partners, and decision makers with complementary lived experience. Avoid a room composed only of advocates or only of internal experts.
3. **Create a common evidence base.** Share short journey fragments, quotes, objects, or service facts, then let participants add missing moments. Use warm-ups and silent reflection so status does not set the first idea.
4. **Make concepts together.** Have mixed tables sketch, storyboard, role-play, or assemble service blueprints. Rotate ownership of the pen, capture assumptions on the artifact, and require each concept to show the user moment it improves.
5. **Select what advances.** Gallery-walk the concepts, gather participant criteria, identify unresolved trade-offs, and choose concepts for prototyping, testing, or further research. Return the artifact record and explain which decisions remain open.
## Output Contract
Deliver a jointly authored concept set containing the challenge, participant mix, evidence used, artifacts, concept descriptions, participant criteria, points of agreement and dissent, and next experiments. Each proposed concept must visibly connect to a participant need and a feasible next step; a list of facilitator ideas is not a co-created outcome.
## Evidence
Treat workshop artifacts, votes, and participant statements as observed contributions, not market-proof. Record who participated, whose perspective is absent, and any constraints supplied by the sponsoring team. Mark the facilitator’s synthesis and feasibility interpretations as inferred. Do not attach a customer’s name or sensitive story to a concept without consent. Validate ideas with a broader sample before treating workshop enthusiasm as demand.
## Checks
- Participants have direct experience of the problem, not only authority over it.
- The agenda gives quieter participants individual making time before group critique.
- Artifacts show user, context, and value rather than abstract feature labels.
- Dissent and unresolved constraints are retained in the concept record.
- Every shortlisted concept has a named test, owner, and decision date.
## Failure Modes
- Inviting only friendly customers who endorse an internal solution already chosen.
- Turning the session into a presentation with a short question-and-answer period.
- Counting dot votes as proof that a concept is feasible or economically viable.
- Losing authorship and consent information when workshop photographs are circulated.
