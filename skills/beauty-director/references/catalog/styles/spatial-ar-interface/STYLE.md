# Spatial AR Interface

## Defining grammar

Spatial AR Interface connects digital information to a camera view or physical environment. Anchors, labels, guides, outlines, occlusion, depth cues, and controls maintain a believable relationship to real surfaces and objects. The defining evidence is registration: a mark stays with the thing it describes as viewpoint, distance, or orientation changes. Floating futuristic graphics over an unrelated photograph do not satisfy the style.

Begin with a real task such as placement, navigation, repair, measurement, identification, training, or collaborative annotation. Show the physical scene clearly enough that the viewer understands where digital content belongs. The overlay should reveal what to notice or do rather than simply announce advanced technology.

## Composition and type

Compose in depth as well as on the screen plane. Keep essential controls in a predictable edge zone while world-linked labels sit near their targets. Use leader lines, brackets, surface meshes, reticles, and measured offsets to clarify attachment. Respect occlusion: digital marks should pass behind or in front of real objects according to their position. Avoid crowding the center of view or attaching content to the viewer's head when it should live in the environment.

Typography should be short, high-contrast, and stable across changing backgrounds. Use a clean sans with clear numerals for distance, angle, quantity, or status. Put labels on subtle scrims when the scene is visually busy. Do not extrude body text into deep space; depth shifts make reading tiring and imprecise.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Choose one registration color, one alert color, white or dark label ink, and transparent support tones. Cyan, lime, or amber can work, but their meaning should be consistent. Lines need enough weight to survive camera noise while remaining lighter than the physical subject. Translucent planes should reveal surfaces rather than hide them.

The camera image provides material and light. Match virtual shadows, scale, and reflections when rendering placed objects. For instructional overlays, clarity is more important than photorealism. Meshes and outlines can be schematic, but they should respond to actual geometry. Include tracking, searching, lost-anchor, and confirmation states in the system.

## Common exits

The style becomes a generic sci-fi HUD when labels have no world target. It becomes Glassmorphism when flat frosted panels dominate. It becomes Operational Dashboard when telemetry tiles replace spatial attachment. Static wireframes, arbitrary crosshairs, and decorative code create atmosphere without AR behavior. Other failures include labels hidden by their targets, virtual objects at implausible scale, conflicting depth cues, excessive head-locked content, and motion that continues after tracking is lost.

## Medium adaptation

For mobile AR, design for a narrow field, one-handed use, camera movement, variable light, and clear coaching before tracking begins. Spatial headsets need comfortable depth, restrained peripheral motion, generous target spacing, and minimal refocusing. Motion-video references should show viewpoint change so registration and occlusion can be judged; a still image needs especially clear anchors and physical surfaces. Presentations can pair a clean annotated still with a short tracked clip. Product visualization should preserve real dimensions and state whether scale is fixed. Wayfinding must include environmental fallback when tracking or location confidence drops. Brand systems may shape reticles and labels, but standard controls and safety signals should remain familiar.
