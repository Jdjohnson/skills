# Byzantine Mosaic

## Defining grammar

Byzantine Mosaic refers to Eastern Roman and Eastern Christian monumental mosaic programs, not a generic gold religious look. Small tesserae of colored glass, stone, and gold-bearing glass create a faceted surface whose image changes with architecture and light. Figures are often frontal, hieratic, and arranged in shallow symbolic space. Gold ground dematerializes ordinary depth while scale and placement establish sacred order. The image belongs to a dome, apse, wall, or liturgical setting; its architectural location is part of the grammar.

## Composition and type

Use axial or radial organization, frontal figures, strong halos, stable gestures, and bands or medallions that respond to the supporting architecture. Perspective is intentionally shallow; overlapping and scale communicate hierarchy more than naturalistic recession. Do not add modern typography inside sacred scenes. When the visual language informs a contemporary layout, keep text separate in a restrained serif or sans and let tessellated fields carry image structure. Borders should use documented geometric or vegetal ornament from the selected monument or period.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Gold is built from angled tesserae and should flicker unevenly rather than read as a flat yellow background. Deep blue, green, red, white, black, and flesh tones appear as discrete units with visible grout and hand-set variation. Imagery may include Christ, the Virgin, saints, angels, rulers, biblical narratives, crosses, or ornamental programs only when the application has appropriate religious and cultural context. Natural or simulated raking light should reveal the changing reflective field and curved architecture.

## Common exits

The style fails when sacred iconography becomes a luxury texture, a decorative avatar, or a fantasy-game backdrop. It exits into ordinary mosaic when frontal iconography, gold ground, and sacred architectural placement disappear. It exits into Art Deco when tessellation becomes polished geometric ornament. Avoid mixing Byzantine, Roman, Islamic, Gothic, and contemporary Orthodox symbols into an ahistorical composite. Do not alter the identity or attributes of sacred figures for novelty, and do not use devotional images to sell unrelated products.

## Medium adaptation

For education, exhibition, or religious contexts, name the monument, tradition, date, and figure, and preserve the relationship between image and architecture. For editorial and presentation work, use details of tessera, border rhythm, or gold-ground light without extracting a sacred face as decoration. For interiors, abstract the material logic into nonfigurative tessellated fields when devotional content is inappropriate. For motion, simulate small light shifts across the faceted surface rather than spinning or fragmenting icons. Image generation should specify Eastern Roman or a named Eastern Christian context and avoid inventing hybrid liturgical symbols.
