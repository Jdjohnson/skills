# Concrete Poetry

## Defining grammar

Concrete poetry treats words, letters, punctuation, and silence as spatial material. Meaning emerges from placement, repetition, orientation, density, and the physical shape of language on a field. The term does not prescribe cement or architecture. Use this style when verbal content can carry both semantic and visual weight, especially for cultural, editorial, literary, or contemplative work.

## Composition

Start with the poem’s operation: accumulation, dispersal, mirroring, rotation, compression, or a shaped field. Every gap must participate. A few marks can establish tension across a large page; many repeated words can form a rhythm or image. Avoid placing an ordinary stanza inside an arbitrary silhouette. The spatial arrangement should change how the language is read and understood.

## Type

Choose type for repeatability, scale contrast, and the character of individual glyphs. Monospaced, grotesk, serif, or typewriter faces can all work if the letterform behavior serves the poem. Preserve exact spacing and line endings. Type may rotate or fragment, but not merely for decoration. Do not introduce multiple expressive fonts when position alone can create the needed voice.

## Color and material

Black ink on white or warm paper is the clearest base because figure and interval remain visible. A single color can distinguish a second voice or reading path. Letterpress impression, carbon ribbon, photocopy, or crisp digital type each changes the tone. Material concrete, architectural photography, and stone texture are false shortcuts unless the actual text explicitly concerns them.

## Imagery and light

The letterform is usually the image. If photography appears, it should interact with reading—masking a word, establishing scale, or creating a counter-field—rather than illustrate the poem literally. Flat, even capture protects spacing in reproductions. A generated approximation should request recognizable movement-level spatial typography using original nonquoted language, disciplined white space, and a clear conceptual operation.

## Common exits

Concrete poetry exits into expressive typography when composition merely dramatizes a slogan, into calligraphy when hand movement is the primary subject, and into editorial layout when a conventional poem is only styled attractively. It also fails when legibility disappears without producing another meaningful reading. Dada collage uses found fragments and discontinuity; this style asks the linguistic unit itself to become structure.

## Medium adaptation

On posters, one spatial operation should survive from across a room and reward close reading. In books, sequence can alter density or position page by page. Websites should preserve spacing responsively, perhaps allowing controlled rearrangement at defined widths rather than fluid collapse. Motion may reveal a reading path, accumulate repeated units, or shift a word between semantic roles. Brand systems can derive patterns from a short original vocabulary, but should not reduce the practice to random scattered letters.
