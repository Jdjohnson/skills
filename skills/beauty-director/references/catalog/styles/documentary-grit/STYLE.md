# Documentary Grit

## Core grammar

Documentary Grit is observational image-making with environmental truth, available light, and visible material conditions. “Grit” describes an unpolished surface and present-tense encounter, not a license to aestheticize hardship. Use it for reporting, field research, civic storytelling, behind-the-scenes work, or editorial narratives that need people and place to remain connected.

## Composition and hierarchy

Allow the environment to explain what is happening. A subject may sit off-center, be partly interrupted by foreground activity, or occupy less of the frame than a commercial portrait would permit. Imperfection should result from responsive observation: a quick angle, real clutter, mixed light, or movement at the edge. Preserve enough spatial evidence to understand labor, community, weather, housing, or infrastructure without turning background detail into spectacle.

## Type and graphic language

Use neutral caption typography, simple dates, locations, and short factual labels. The graphic layer should clarify provenance and sequence. Avoid stencil fonts, fake typewriter distress, torn overlays, and dramatic pull quotes that instruct the audience what to feel before the image has been understood.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Muted chroma, mixed practical light, matte surfaces, dust, worn paint, and ordinary clothing can all appear naturally. Do not desaturate every scene into brown-gray sameness. Grain should relate to capture or a consistent editorial treatment; artificial scratches and dirty borders are decorative simulation. Protect readable faces while accepting blocked shadows, window glare, or color casts when they belong to the location.

## Use across media

Photo essays and short documentaries should sequence wide context, human action, and close material evidence. Editorial pages need captions and enough scale for environmental detail. Presentations can use one image per claim with a factual note rather than a cinematic montage. In brand or social work, obtain appropriate permissions and avoid recasting real people as anonymous symbols for a message they did not choose.

## Common exits

Posed fashion with added grain is not documentary. Heroic low angles and dramatic relighting can convert observation into advertising. Repeated images of decay without people or context become ruin aesthetics. Selective framing can mislead even when a photograph is technically unaltered, so sequence and captions matter.

## Direction recipe

Enter with a defined subject and learn the setting before composing. Make one contextual frame, one action frame, and one detail while disturbing the scene as little as possible. Retain available-light character, write a precise caption, and reject any image whose visual drama depends on stripping away the subject’s actual circumstances.
