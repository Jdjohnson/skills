# Studio Pottery

## Object and process

Studio Pottery centers the individual or small workshop making ceramic objects whose form, clay body, glaze, and firing are designed as one expression. It spans functional ware and sculptural vessels, so the direction should not be reduced to a single rustic bowl. Show the intelligence of throwing, hand-building, trimming, joining, glazing, and kiln variation through silhouettes that feel authored rather than factory-uniform.

## Composition and scale

Arrange one lead vessel with enough space to read its profile, foot, rim, and balance. A small group can reveal a family of forms, but every object should contribute a different proportion or surface treatment. In studio scenes, let shelves, tools, glaze tests, or a wheel explain process without burying the finished work. Use low eye levels or three-quarter views for vessels; a straight catalog lineup works only when subtle differences across a series are the point. Reject crowded craft-market displays.

## Type, color, and material

Typography plays a supporting role: an understated serif or plain grotesk can label maker, clay, firing, and dimensions with the economy of a gallery record. Avoid script faces that turn craft into sentiment. Let the clay body establish the palette—iron red, buff stoneware, porcelain white, charcoal, or grogged gray—then add glaze colors such as celadon, tenmoku brown, ash green, cobalt, or oxide blue. Surface can be matte, satin, glossy, incised, or volcanic, but it should follow the vessel rather than disguise it.

## Imagery and light

A strong lead combines unmistakable vessel character with evidence of a studio practice. Raking side light should reveal wall thickness, throwing rings, carved lines, glaze pooling, and kiln flashes while keeping the overall object readable. Hands may demonstrate scale or process, but anonymous lifestyle portraiture should not replace the ceramic. Include dust, kiln shelves, test tiles, or tools selectively. Neutral grounds help glaze depth register without making the image look like commodity tableware advertising.

## Common exits

Avoid generic boho ceramics, perfectly identical slip-cast sets, restaurant place settings, glossy homeware catalogs, and pottery-class snapshots where no finished form is visible. Excessive cracks and weathering drift toward Wabi-Sabi; pristine porcelain vitrines drift toward luxury product display. Random glaze effects are not enough—shape, surface, and firing must feel intentionally related. Do not imply that visible throwing rings are required for every studio potter; refined and smoothed surfaces can still be handmade.

## Medium adaptation

For identity and packaging, translate the vessel's profile, maker's mark, or glaze boundary into a restrained graphic device and use tactile stock. Editorial pages should pair generous object photography with concise process captions. Digital galleries need consistent scale and color rendering, with close views available for surface. Presentation decks can use specimen-like sequences to explain form development. In spatial or product work, display fewer pieces with room around them and lighting that honors silhouette. Motion is strongest when it shows a concise transformation—centering clay, pulling a wall, applying glaze, or opening a kiln—before returning to the resolved object.
