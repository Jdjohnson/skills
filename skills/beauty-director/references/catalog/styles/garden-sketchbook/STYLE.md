# Garden Sketchbook

## Observation in progress

Garden Sketchbook records looking, testing, and remembering in the field. Several plant or garden studies share a page with changes of scale, incomplete contours, crop fragments, color swatches, dates, measurements, or brief marginal notes. Pencil pressure, erasure, wash, and page wear reveal decisions as they happen. The direction is more personal and provisional than Botanical Watercolor and less systematic than a published botanical plate.

## Page composition

Treat the spread as a working field rather than a finished illustration centered on one specimen. Place one larger plant gesture beside smaller leaf, seed, flower, tool, or spatial studies. Let an image run toward the gutter or stop at a page edge. Notes can sit in the spaces created by drawings instead of following a rigid grid. Maintain enough quiet paper to separate observations; “messy” should mean accumulated attention, not arbitrary scatter. A visible binding edge or page turn helps establish the notebook object.

## Mark, notation, and color

Use graphite with varied pressure, selective fine ink, and small transparent watercolor washes. Some lines can remain exploratory or doubled, while decisive contours clarify important structure. Handwritten notation should look practical and compact, not like a decorative script font. Dates, plant names, weather, scale, or color tests may be partly readable when rights and generation constraints allow. Keep color local: leaf greens, soil and terracotta, one flower accent, and warm paper. Full-page saturated wash belongs elsewhere.

## Imagery and light

A strong lead shows an open sketchbook with at least three distinct observations made from life: an overall plant or garden view, a close structural study, and a note or color test. The viewer should recognize the working process even at small scale. Photograph the page in diffuse outdoor or desk light, preserving graphite and paper tone without dramatic shadows. A hand, pencil, clipped stem, or portable paint box may provide context, but no prop should hide the spread.

## Common exits

Avoid one polished garden painting, finished floral stationery, clip-art bees and trowels, faux scrapbook collage, children's doodle styling, or a stock notebook beside coffee. Dense calligraphy can turn the page into journaling content rather than visual observation. Perfect vector lines and digital watercolor brushes erase field immediacy, while random stains and torn paper fake wear without adding knowledge. Keep botanical anatomy plausible, but do not polish every study into scientific-plate completeness.

## Medium adaptation

For editorial and document work, use a spread-like hierarchy with one dominant observation, small supporting studies, and concise marginal notes. Identity systems can borrow the irregular crop, pencil line, and color swatch without manufacturing fake handwriting at scale. Websites should preserve the layered page feeling through grouped images and annotations while keeping interaction orderly. Packaging can use one partial study and a small material note. In presentations, build from rough observation to resolved idea across slides. Motion is especially natural: a line searches for form, a wash enters one area, a note appears, and the page turns to a related study.
