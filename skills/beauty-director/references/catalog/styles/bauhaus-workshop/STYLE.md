# Bauhaus Workshop

## Defining grammar

This node treats design as an experiment in form, material, function, and production. Reach for it when the work should feel constructed through study rather than polished through styling. Elemental geometry is important, but circles, bars, planes, and primary colors are means rather than a costume. The stronger signal is the visible relationship among parts: type behaves as form, materials reveal their properties, and composition tests balance, contrast, rhythm, texture, and utility.

## Composition and type

Build an asymmetric field from a few clear shapes. Let circles oppose straight bars, solids meet open space, and large forms control smaller functional details. Use rotation, overlap, and cropping with intention. Typography can be geometric or industrial, but it must participate in the construction instead of sitting above it as a label. Prefer concise sans lettering, varied scale, and alignment to visible planes. Avoid ornamental alphabets, retro pastiche, and the assumption that all Bauhaus work uses the same typeface.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Start with black, white, and natural material tones; introduce red, blue, or yellow as structural accents only when they clarify relationships. Paper, wire, wood, glass, metal, textile, and photographic experiments can coexist if each material remains legible. Imagery should show studies, prototypes, workshop arrangements, stage forms, or functional objects rather than generic abstract paintings. Use clear studio light that reveals edge, thickness, grain, transparency, and join. Hard shadows may become compositional shapes, but glossy commercial spectacle is out of character.

## Common exits

A primary-color geometry pattern by itself is not enough. The style exits into De Stijl when rectangles and pure color become an autonomous formal system; into Constructivist poster design when diagonal propaganda energy and photomontage dominate; and into Memphis Design when geometry turns knowingly decorative and exuberant. It also fails when aged paper, German captions, or famous building silhouettes are used as shortcuts. Historical reference should support the method, not imitate an artifact.

## Medium adaptation

For brand systems, develop a kit of shapes, typographic relationships, and material applications that can recombine without becoming a logo stamp. For editorial work, expose the grid and let diagrams, photographs, and type share the page. For interfaces, translate workshop logic into clear components and experimental interaction without adding faux vintage texture. In motion, animate assembly, rotation, balance, and transformation. In objects or environments, allow construction and material junctions to remain visible. Every adaptation should feel like a purposeful exercise that could teach how the result was made.
