# Art Deco

## Defining grammar

Art Deco combines decorative luxury with machine-age order. Its strongest signals are stepped profiles, fan and sunburst motifs, chevrons, repeated parallel lines, faceted geometry, symmetry, and streamlined curves. Materials and surfaces matter as much as shape: lacquer, polished metal, mirrored glass, rich wood, stone, enamel, and glossy print create controlled glamour. Deco is not one fixed motif set; it moves from zigzag exuberance to later streamlined restraint. In every form, geometry organizes ornament rather than merely decorating it.

## Composition and type

Use strong axes, tiered hierarchy, symmetrical framing, and deliberate vertical emphasis. A centered hero may rise through steps or radiating lines, while secondary elements repeat in narrow bands. Keep geometry crisp and proportions elongated. Display type can be geometric, high-waisted, inline, or sharply contrasted, but it should not become a novelty alphabet on every line. Pair one distinctive title with restrained supporting type. For editorial layouts, use borders, rules, and stepped blocks sparingly enough that reading order remains clear.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Black, ivory, deep emerald, cobalt, burgundy, and warm metallic gold or silver are reliable foundations. Brighter travel-poster color works when keyed to flat geometry. Use reflection and sheen with precision: one lacquer plane, chrome edge, or glass highlight can establish luxury better than a field of fake gold gradients. Imagery may include skyscraper crowns, ocean liners, speed, nightlife, stylized figures, cosmetics, jewelry, or engineered objects. Light should carve facets and polished edges while maintaining graphic clarity.

## Common exits

Art Deco becomes Art Nouveau when whiplash curves and botanical growth replace faceted symmetry. It becomes Empire Style when Napoleonic emblems, mahogany mass, and state monumentality take over. It becomes generic “Gatsby” styling when black and gold are applied without geometry, proportion, or material logic. Avoid Egyptian, African, Asian, or Indigenous motifs as free-floating exotic decoration; Deco historically appropriated many sources, so modern use should not repeat that flattening. Chrome diner nostalgia belongs more naturally to Roadside Americana unless the design maintains Deco order.

## Medium adaptation

For brand and packaging, use one structural motif—step, fan, or facet—across the system and reserve metallic finish for hierarchy. For web, translate tiered geometry into section framing and motion, not tiny decorative lines around every component. For presentations, build symmetrical title pages and calmer content slides. For interiors and product work, prioritize real proportion, joinery, sheen, and repeated geometry. Motion can radiate, telescope, or glide in streamlined bands. Image generation should specify the Deco phase, medium, and material rather than asking only for “1920s luxury.”
