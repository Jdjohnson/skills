# Mexican Tinwork

## Defining grammar

Mexican Tinwork centers hand-cut, punched, embossed, folded, and shaped metal used for intimate devotional or narrative objects. Retablos place painted sacred or votive imagery on metal; nichos create shallow shrine-like enclosures with reflective frames, glass, wood, paint, or small figures. The style's force comes from the meeting of humble sheet material, saturated image, handwork, faith, family memory, and regional continuity across Mexico and the U.S. Southwest. Begin with the specific object type and community context rather than a generic “rustic metal” mood.

## Composition and type

Build a compact frontal object around a clear center: a painted scene, saint, figure group, or narrative panel. The tin frame may step, arch, scallop, radiate, or carry punched rosettes, but it should remain visibly constructed from folded and joined pieces. Symmetry can organize the surround while handmade variation keeps it alive. Any inscription should come from the documented object or be written accurately for the project; invented Spanish devotional copy is not decoration. Keep external typography quiet so it does not compete with the image and worked metal.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Use silver-gray tin, darkened seams, soft oxidation, painted red, blue, yellow, green, cream, and occasional glass or wood. Raking light should catch repoussé relief and punched texture while avoiding jewelry-advertising polish. Painted imagery may be simple, direct, and emotionally intense; proportion and perspective can be subordinate to narrative and devotion. Preserve evidence of cutting, soldering, crimping, repainting, and touch. Do not replace metal with a metallic gradient or treat patina as a universal brown filter.

## Common exits

The direction exits into Folk Tilework when glazed ceramic units and grout build architectural pattern. It exits into Byzantine Mosaic when tesserae, monumental sacred space, and gold field dominate. It exits into generic Southwestern décor when suns, milagro-like icons, turquoise, and distressed metal are mixed without a specific practice. A silver frame around any colorful picture is not Mexican Tinwork; the material process and object context must be visible.

## Medium adaptation

For editorial or exhibition design, use object photography at true intimate scale with captions that name maker, region, date, and function. For identity or packaging, translate edge shapes, punched rhythm, and reflective contrast without lifting sacred imagery. For digital work, let a restrained metal surround frame content while interaction remains modern and legible. Motion may reveal how a flat sheet becomes a raised frame or let light travel across punched relief. When the project touches devotional traditions, collaborate with knowledgeable makers or cultural partners, keep sacred figures in context, and do not turn votive forms into novelty branding.
