# Culture Review

Culture review is an essay-led periodical voice for criticism, interviews, reviews, and arts reporting. It suits publications that discuss books, film, music, theatre, exhibitions, architecture, and public ideas for an attentive general reader. Its character comes from the coexistence of argument and cultural material: writing has real authority, but images, excerpts, captions, and section identities keep the issue varied and contemporary.

## Defining grammar

Construct the issue around recognizable departments such as essays, reviews, interviews, and shorter notices. Each section can have its own pacing, yet masthead, folios, bylines, captions, and recurring rules should make the publication coherent. A lead essay may receive a generous opener; a review page should be more compact; a portfolio or interview can change the image-text balance. The result should feel edited across an issue, not like unrelated article layouts sharing a logo.

## Composition and type

Use a flexible editorial grid capable of supporting long reading, short criticism, quotations, and art reproduction. Typography should distinguish publication voice from the title of the work under review. A readable text face carries essays, while a controlled display face or sectional device provides personality. Pull quotes are occasional points of entry, not filler. Keep captions and credits close to their images, and allow reproductions enough scale to be meaningfully inspected. On digital pages, retain department identity and reading measure across varied article types.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Choose a quiet paper or screen ground, dark readable ink, and a small set of section accents. Imagery should be specific to the cultural work or subject: book covers, production stills, installation views, portraits, documents, or commissioned illustration. Reproductions need truthful aspect ratios and visible crediting. Avoid generic library, gallery, or audience stock photographs as substitutes for the work being discussed. Texture may suggest print history, but should not overwhelm contemporary criticism.

## Common exits

The style fails when intellectual seriousness is represented only by tiny serif type and beige paper. It also fails when every article receives the same grand opener, when the visual material is unrelated to the criticism, or when quotation fragments replace argument. Academic journal carries more formal research apparatus; literary journal is usually quieter and less departmental; magazine feature is more commercially theatrical. News urgency, dashboard metrics, or nightlife interface effects confuse the review's measured editorial voice.

## Medium adaptation

For print, demonstrate at least three departments so issue rhythm is evident. For websites, maintain section landing pages, article metadata, credits, and related reading without turning the experience into a content grid alone. For social promotion, present a precise review claim or credited cultural image rather than a vague inspirational quotation. For newsletters, condense the hierarchy but keep a clear distinction among criticism, listing, and promotion. The hero should show a culture issue containing a lead essay spread, a compact review page, and a distinct arts section, with art and text visibly in dialogue.
