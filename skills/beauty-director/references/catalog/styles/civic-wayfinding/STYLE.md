# Civic Wayfinding

## Defining grammar

Civic Wayfinding helps people orient, choose a route, and confirm progress through public space. Its visual character comes from viewing distance, decision sequence, standardized symbols, directional consistency, and environmental placement. Use it for transit, campuses, hospitals, museums, civic buildings, and districts where many people with different languages or abilities must find their way. It is a coordinated sign system, not a page of icons.

## Composition and type

Map the user journey before designing signs. Identify where people enter, decide, continue, and arrive; then assign the right information to each moment. Group destinations by direction and order them consistently. Arrows must behave as one family and point unambiguously. Choose a highly legible sans with distinguishable characters and size it for real viewing conditions. Pictograms should share construction, scale, and alignment. Keep messages brief, and use hierarchy to separate destination, route, facility, and reassurance information.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Use color as a code for lines, zones, floors, or services rather than as decoration. Contrast must hold under glare, shadow, distance, and common forms of color-vision difference. Enamel, painted metal, vinyl, glass, and digital displays can all work when finish, edge, and mounting suit the environment. Photography is normally unnecessary; maps and pictograms carry the visual information. Lighting should make signs readable without hotspots. Consider ambient conditions, nighttime use, and how surfaces age.

## Common exits

The node becomes Isotype Statistical Graphics when repeated pictograms represent quantities. It becomes Museum Label when interpretation of an object matters more than navigation, and it becomes a branding exercise when visual personality outranks route clarity. Common failures include ambiguous arrow placement, excessive destinations, low-contrast color, tiny type, inconsistent naming, decorative icons, signs installed after the decision point, and standalone mockups that never show the environment.

## Medium adaptation

For physical systems, test full-size prototypes at actual distances and from multiple approach angles. For maps, match terminology, route color, and landmarks to the signs people will encounter next. For digital kiosks and apps, preserve the same names and colors while adding search and current location. In presentations, show the complete journey and sign family, not only the most attractive panel. For temporary events, simplify the permanent grammar without inventing a competing system. Accessibility, multilingual requirements, maintenance, and change over time are part of the visual direction from the beginning.
