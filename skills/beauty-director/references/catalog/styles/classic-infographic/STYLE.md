# Classic Infographic

## Defining grammar

Classic Infographic makes a visual argument on one page. It encodes quantity, direction, comparison, sequence, geography, or causation through proportional marks and concise annotation. The sheet should answer a specific question faster than prose alone while remaining inspectable: a reader can see what each line, band, area, symbol, or number means. Charles Joseph Minard's flow maps are an important anchor because band width, route, direction, labels, and geography work together instead of appearing as separate decorative charts.

## Composition and type

Lead with the primary comparison or flow and arrange title, explanatory note, legend, labels, and source around it. Use position, length, width, area, sequence, and restrained color consistently. Small multiples can support comparison when every panel shares a scale. Annotation should sit near the evidence it explains, with a separate caption only for context that cannot fit locally. Typography must be compact and editorial, and numbers should align and carry units. Avoid scattering pictograms, arrows, and percentages with no shared visual logic.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Use few colors, each assigned a stable meaning. Warm-cool pairs, dark-light variation, or one accent against black and cream can separate flows and categories. Paper, engraved line, lithographic tint, and hand lettering can contribute historical character, but data marks must remain primary. Illustration should clarify subject or scale, not fill empty space. Light and depth are generally unnecessary; flat marks preserve measurement. Do not use glossy 3D charts, unnecessary shadows, or decorative gradients that distort perceived quantity.

## Common exits

The style exits into Cartographic Sheet when terrain and map reading are the main purpose rather than encoded statistics. It exits into Isotype Statistical Graphics when repeated standardized pictograms become the core quantitative method. It exits into Operational Dashboard when live metrics, controls, and repeated interface cards replace one composed argument. A poster with facts, a timeline with icons, or a collection of unrelated charts is not a classic infographic without a coherent explanatory structure.

## Medium adaptation

For presentation, preserve the full argument on one slide, then use later slides to enlarge evidence rather than rebuilding it inconsistently. For web, allow responsive stacking only if scales, legends, and relationships survive. For social graphics, reduce to one comparison and cite the source. Motion can trace a route, grow a proportional band, or reveal sequence, but the final frame must still make sense as a static record. Verify every value, unit, date, category, and source; visual elegance cannot rescue ambiguous or fabricated data.
