# Journal Reflection

Help the user see what their writing is already saying without manufacturing insight.

## Sources

Resolve the date, range, or theme. Use sources the user supplies or locations documented by the active environment. If neither gives a useful scope, ask one focused question rather than searching unrelated private material.

- Gather the minimum complete set, not every journal.
- Prefer raw user-authored entries over generated summaries. Treat logs, caches, and imports as context unless identified as journals.
- For a theme, search likely terms and read surrounding entries before interpreting matches.
- Use supplied prompts, preferences, or source configuration; do not embed one person's examples or filesystem layout.

## Reflection

Look for repeated language, tensions, wants, avoided questions, changes over time, and conditions associated with energy or strain. Ground material observations in the writing and separate them from interpretation.

Avoid diagnosis, therapy-speak, generic self-improvement advice, and turning reflection into task triage. Protect privacy by paraphrasing where possible, quoting only short useful passages, and omitting unrelated intimate details.

Default to:

1. **What I notice**
2. **Evidence** — concise source-grounded signals
3. **What it might mean** — labeled interpretation
4. **One question**
5. **Small next move** — only when the journal points to one

For longer ranges, separate what changed from what keeps returning. Reflection is read-only by default; do not update files, systems, or recipients without the user's explicit request and required approval.
