# Film Noir

## Core grammar

Film Noir is a crime-night image system, not a generic black-and-white filter or a collection of vintage props. Its visual force comes from low-key exposure, hard directional light, uncertain urban space, and people caught in morally charged situations. Use it when a scene should feel watchful, compromised, or fated. Period wardrobe can help, but lighting and staging must communicate the genre before a fedora or pistol does.

## Composition and hierarchy

Build frames on diagonals, partial occlusion, and unequal pools of visibility. A foreground post, banister, blind shadow, or passing silhouette can split the image without becoming decoration. Keep a readable subject, then let deep black remove explanatory context around that subject. Street scenes benefit from wet pavement, compressed alley depth, smoke, or a distant practical light. Interiors should feel bounded and off-balance rather than spacious or orderly.

## Type and graphic language

Typography is supporting evidence, not the source of the style. Condensed grotesks, severe serif caps, and restrained credit-scale type can fit, especially in title graphics. Keep alignment decisive and spacing tense. Avoid distressed novelty fonts that advertise “detective” without carrying the image’s actual visual logic.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Monochrome is canonical, but near-black blue, nicotine amber, dirty ivory, and a restrained red accent can extend the grammar. Preserve broad black masses and narrow highlights. Film grain should unify tonal transitions rather than imitate damage. Faces need selective detail; evenly filled portraits become publicity photography, while bright painted posters become retro advertising. Reflections, glass, smoke, and rain work because they complicate sightlines.

## Use across media

For photography and motion, motivate light from streetlamps, desk lamps, windows, or unseen hard sources. For editorial covers, retain one dominant image and limit caption furniture. In presentation or web work, translate noir into cropped photography, stark tonal blocks, and deliberate concealment; do not turn every component black or add ornamental crime icons.

## Common exits

Soft studio portraiture loses the spatial threat. Saturated pulp collage becomes vintage poster design. Clean neon nightlife moves toward Neo-Noir Neon, while shape-only black-and-white work belongs to Graphic Black & White. Excess grain, crushed facial detail, and random dutch angles are symptoms, not grammar.

## Direction recipe

Stage an off-axis urban encounter at night. Use one hard key, one small reflected practical, and enough atmospheric depth to separate planes. Protect the largest black shape, keep the subject’s intention ambiguous, and reject any frame that reads as costume photography before it reads as danger.
