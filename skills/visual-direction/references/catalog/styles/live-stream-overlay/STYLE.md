# Live-Stream Overlay

## Defining grammar

Live-Stream Overlay is the audience-facing composition wrapped around live video. It combines a primary content window, webcam frame, alerts, chat-adjacent information, labels, goals, badges, and state changes into one broadcast surface. The style is defined by how those parts coexist during performance, not by a screenshot of broadcasting software or a photograph of cameras.

Design the system as a family of scenes: live gameplay or presentation, conversation, starting soon, short break, and ending. The same color, type, line, and shape logic should connect every state while allowing the content window to remain dominant. Alerts are temporary events, not permanent decoration.

## Composition and type

Reserve the largest uninterrupted region for the stream's subject. Place the webcam where it will not cover critical game or presentation information, then use edge zones for chat, goals, names, or recent activity. Frames should organize rather than cage the picture. Safe margins must account for platform controls, varied crops, and vertical excerpts.

Typography needs to read through video compression and at small viewing sizes. Use a sturdy screen sans, short labels, tabular numbers, and clear weight contrast. Keep usernames and alerts within predictable lengths. A display face can carry the channel personality, but chat and status copy should use a simpler companion.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Choose a dark, light, or transparent base that remains distinct over varied content. One primary accent and one event color are usually enough. Use gradients, neon strokes, or illustrated motifs as a coherent frame system, not as unrelated stickers. Ensure that success, warning, and live states are not distinguished by color alone.

The live image supplies changing light and texture, so overlay surfaces need stable opacity and contrast. Webcam treatment may include a subtle key light or background shape, but the person should not disappear into decorative glow. Supporting illustration belongs in waiting scenes and empty modules, where it will not compete with live action.

## Common exits

The style fails when the creator tool interface is mistaken for the finished broadcast, when equipment replaces screen furniture, or when every available module is visible at once. It becomes Operational Dashboard when metrics and status panels outweigh performance content. It becomes Neon Night UI when nightlife controls replace live-video hierarchy. Overly thin lines, tiny chat text, and alerts positioned over faces are practical failures even if the still image looks polished.

## Medium adaptation

For streaming software, construct reusable scenes and sources with consistent names, exact pixel dimensions, and transparent assets. Test at the platform's encoded output, not only on a large design canvas. Motion should be brief, readable, and interruptible; looping decorations must not exhaust attention. Vertical clips need a reduced layout with larger captions and reframed camera content. Brand identity can extend through badges, panels, emotes, and social cards while preserving the core mark outside the overlay. Presentations and webinars should use a quieter version with speaker, slides, and question state clearly separated. Event screens may enlarge alerts and goals, but must retain broadcast-safe areas. Print applications are secondary and should document the system rather than imitate a live screen with meaningless modules.
