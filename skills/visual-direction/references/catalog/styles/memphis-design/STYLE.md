# Memphis Design

## Defining grammar

Memphis Design is the early-1980s postmodern object and surface language associated with Ettore Sottsass and the Memphis collective. It rejects restrained modernist good taste through clashing laminate color, asymmetrical geometric furniture, squiggles, dots, grids, terrazzo-like fields, kitsch references, and deliberately unstable scale. The style is not a generic 1990s confetti background. Objects, patterns, and industrial materials collide in three dimensions, creating a designed environment that is playful, provocative, and knowingly artificial.

## Composition and type

Use off-balance stacks, cantilevered shapes, circles against diagonals, thin rods beside chunky volumes, and patterns that ignore expected material boundaries. Preserve enough space around each object for its improbable silhouette to register. A flat graphic can combine pattern swatches and object-like forms, but should imply a system of surfaces and construction. Type is secondary: geometric sans, eccentric display forms, or compact labels work when they share the object's angles and rhythm. Avoid making every letter a separate novelty shape.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Use high-chroma red, yellow, cobalt, turquoise, pink, black, and white, sometimes balanced by a dusty pastel or neutral laminate. Materials should look manufactured: plastic laminate, printed glass, lacquered wood, terrazzo, metal tube, neon, fiberglass, and patterned textile. Imagery should show furniture, lamps, ceramics, room sets, or coherent product families—not only floating triangles. Light is clean and studio-like, with enough directional shadow to reveal odd construction and object scale.

## Common exits

The style becomes Pattern Playground when repetition fills a continuous surface without object logic. It becomes Geometric Toy when primary wood pieces are genuinely buildable and child-scaled. It becomes Vaporwave when gradients, classical busts, and digital nostalgia replace industrial objects. Avoid labeling any pastel squiggle pattern Memphis, and do not use “90s” as the primary era cue. Minimal white space with a few confetti marks misses the movement's clash of material, shape, and cultural quotation.

## Medium adaptation

For interiors and product work, choose a small family of clashing forms and two or three patterns, then repeat them across furniture and surfaces. For identity and packaging, translate the object language into asymmetrical panels and laminate-like fields while preserving a clear brand hierarchy. For web and presentations, use bold frames and section markers rather than patterned backgrounds behind all text. Motion can stack, pivot, and snap forms into improbable balance. Image generation should specify an original postmodern room or object system, manufactured materials, asymmetrical construction, and early-1980s Italian design context.
