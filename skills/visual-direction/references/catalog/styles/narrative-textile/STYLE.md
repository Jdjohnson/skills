# Narrative Textile

## Defining grammar

Narrative Textile carries events across cloth through weaving, embroidery, appliqué, quilting, or a combination of processes. The field may read left to right, snake between registers, gather episodes around a center, or use borders and labels to connect time. Figures and scenery are simplified to survive the material, but thread direction, seams, and repeated stitches hold emotional and historical weight. Always name the specific tradition and maker where possible. Hmong paj ntaub, Chilean arpilleras, European narrative tapestry, and other forms are not interchangeable examples of one anonymous folk style.

## Composition and type

Establish a readable path among several events. Repeat protagonists, landscape markers, vehicles, homes, plants, or borders so the viewer can follow change over time. Scale may indicate narrative importance rather than optical distance. Registers can be explicit, or scenes can share one continuous ground. Use stitched or woven labels only when documented within the selected tradition; otherwise keep captions outside the object. Avoid imposing modern comic boxes on a field whose sequence is carried by movement, overlap, and material transitions.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Let cloth, thread, yarn, and appliquéd pieces define edge and color. Bright thread against a dark ground can separate many small figures; scrap fabrics can encode clothing, terrain, or architecture; woven color may create softer transitions. Light should show raised stitch, folds, seams, and slight distortion without flattening the object into a scan. Imagery must reflect the chosen community's recorded subjects—migration, work, memory, conflict, celebration, genealogy, or sacred story—rather than adding generic costumes and symbols.

## Common exits

The style exits into Folk Embroidery when a single garment zone or ornamental motif dominates. It exits into Quilting Pattern when modular block repetition matters more than unfolding events. It exits into Narrative Woodcut when carved black line and printed paper replace cloth construction. A tapestry texture over a conventional illustration does not qualify, and neither does labeling every pictorial textile a “story cloth” without identifying its distinct origin.

## Medium adaptation

For editorial work, show a full textile beside details and provide maker, place, date, material, and reading guidance. For identity or packaging, adapt sequencing, border rhythm, or material layering without copying a community's historical trauma or sacred story. Digital narratives can use a scrolling field that follows the textile's actual reading order, while controls and captions remain plain. Motion may trace a thread between episodes or reveal appliquéd layers in construction order. For living traditions and diaspora narratives, prioritize maker voice, consent, attribution, and context; do not turn testimony into decorative content.
