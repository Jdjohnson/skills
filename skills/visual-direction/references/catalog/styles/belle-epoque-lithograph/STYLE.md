# Belle Époque Lithograph

## Defining grammar

Belle Époque Lithograph is a complete fin-de-siècle public poster composition shaped by color lithography. Hand-lettered headlines, lively figures, flat keyed color, broad silhouettes, and the compressed energy of theater, cabaret, cycling, travel, or urban entertainment share one sheet. The print process matters: color areas, drawn contour, paper ground, and slight registration behavior create the look. A cropped artwork or isolated portrait is not enough; message, figure, lettering, and public scale must work together.

## Composition and type

Use one dominant figure or gesture, often cropped boldly, with secondary silhouettes or venue cues establishing context. Let lettering participate in the drawing—arching above a performer, stacking beside a figure, or locking into negative space. Headlines should be short, large, and hand-shaped; supporting date, venue, and attraction copy can form smaller bands. Preserve an immediate street read. Avoid placing a modern centered text box on top of a period illustration.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Use a limited set of keyed lithographic colors: black contour with red, yellow, blue, green, orange, or paper cream. Flat fields and selective overprint matter more than smooth gradients. Keep paper tooth, drawn crayon texture, and minor registration variation visible but controlled. Imagery may include performers, nightlife, bicycles, cafés, fashion, products, and urban crowds when grounded in the period. Lighting is graphic and implied through flat color rather than modeled photographically.

## Common exits

The style exits into Art Nouveau when whiplash botany and total-design ornament dominate instead of public entertainment and lithographic message. It exits into Carnival Poster when wood type, attraction lists, and hyperbolic showman density take over. It becomes Victorian Ephemera when multiple small cards and sentimental scraps replace one complete poster. Avoid using a museum photo of a cropped drawing as if it were a poster, adding random French phrases, or combining unrelated period stereotypes. Historic portrayals of performers and nightlife require context rather than nostalgic flattening.

## Medium adaptation

For contemporary posters, preserve the single-sheet hierarchy: one figure, one headline, one supporting information band. For identity and packaging, extract the keyed color, hand-drawn title, and silhouette without pretending the object is an antique. For social and web, crop responsively but keep type and figure integrated; do not separate them into unrelated modules. Motion can animate the drawing process, shift printed color plates, or bring venue copy on in sequence. Image generation should request a complete lithographic poster, named event type, approximate date, limited inks, legible invented copy, and no copied protected composition.
