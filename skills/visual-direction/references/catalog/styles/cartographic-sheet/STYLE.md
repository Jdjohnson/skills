# Cartographic Sheet

## Defining grammar

Cartographic Sheet translates measured terrestrial space into a consistent symbol system. Terrain, water, routes, structures, boundaries, vegetation, names, coordinates, scale, and legend must coexist without losing their distinct roles. Contour lines connect equal elevations and change spacing with slope; line, point, area, color, and type each encode different feature classes. The sheet's authority comes from relational accuracy and complete marginal information, not from antique decoration or a fashionable topographic texture.

## Composition and type

Choose projection, extent, orientation, and scale before styling. Let the map body occupy most of the sheet, with title, legend, scale, coordinate notes, publication data, and index arranged in stable margins. Use hierarchy so major towns, water bodies, peaks, routes, and boundaries read in the intended order. Labels should follow features when useful, avoid collisions, and use consistent face, size, color, and halo rules. Contours may be dense but should not overpower roads, hydrography, or names. Every symbol visible in the map should be interpretable from convention or legend.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Use a restrained conventional palette: brown for contour, blue for hydrography, green for vegetation, black or gray for structures and many labels, and selected red or purple for route or revision information where historically appropriate. Paper scans can retain fold, ink, and print variation, but current work should favor clarity over faux age. Shaded relief may support contours without replacing them. Avoid perspective lighting, glossy 3D terrain, decorative compass roses, and satellite imagery that has not been integrated into the map's symbol hierarchy.

## Common exits

The style exits into Astro Chart when celestial coordinates and star fields replace land. It exits into Classic Infographic when the geographic base mainly carries statistical flow or argument. It exits into Civic Wayfinding when navigation through a built environment matters more than measured terrain. Fantasy maps, travel posters, trail badges, and abstract contour-line backgrounds are adjacent forms, not cartographic sheets, unless scale, legend, symbols, and spatial relationships remain meaningful.

## Medium adaptation

For editorial and presentation work, simplify feature classes but keep the legend and scale honest. For interfaces, preserve zoom-dependent hierarchy and prevent labels from colliding as the view changes. For identity, borrow line weight, grid, and marginal notation without presenting fictional measurements as real data. Motion can reveal layers, routes, or elevation in a controlled sequence while maintaining orientation. Test the sheet in monochrome, at print size, and at reduced card scale; the main terrain structure should remain clear even when the smallest labels disappear.
