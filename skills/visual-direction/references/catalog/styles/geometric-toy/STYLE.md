# Geometric Toy

## Core grammar

Geometric Toy uses solid primitives as physical, recombinable parts. Arches, blocks, cylinders, tracks, wedges, and curves create cheerful structures that visibly could be taken apart and rebuilt. Use it for learning, family products, playful identity, physical-digital metaphors, and imagery that needs constructive optimism without licensed toy branding.

## Composition and hierarchy

Build a clear object-scale arrangement with stable contact between pieces. One larger arch, ramp, or tower can establish hierarchy while smaller parts reveal the modular system. Leave enough negative space to read each component’s shape. Slight asymmetry and an unfinished edge invite continued play. Avoid perfect city dioramas with locked camera axes; those move toward Isometric Play.

## Type and graphic language

Simple rounded sans or sturdy geometric lettering can echo the parts. Type may stack or align to a block grid, but it should not mimic a familiar toy company. Keep labels large, few, and friendly. Outline-only fonts and rainbow letters can become preschool cliché if the object system is already colorful.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Use a limited family of solid colors—red, blue, yellow, green, warm wood, and one softer neutral—distributed by shape rather than randomly. Matte paint, visible wood grain, cork, or dense paperboard make the pieces tactile. Light from one broad side source so rounded and flat forms remain distinct. Shadows should show contact and weight without turning the scene into dramatic product luxury.

## Use across media

Product photography and 3D illustration are primary. Motion can show pieces joining, rolling, balancing, or reconfiguring. Brand systems may derive icons and layouts from the primitive inventory. Web and presentation work should keep the constructive metaphor focused; scattering blocks around unrelated content weakens it. Packaging should show scale and possible combinations.

## Common exits

Glossy translucent objects belong to Candy Plastic. A fully projected room or street becomes Isometric Play. Repeating primitives without physical assembly become Pattern Playground. Avoid trademarked stud systems, recognizable character pieces, and impossible stacks that deny the buildable premise.

## Direction recipe

Choose six to ten primitives with a consistent scale and material. Build one readable structure with an obvious base, a curved or spanning element, and two pieces left available nearby. Use broad soft light, preserve contact shadows, and confirm that the image suggests more than one possible configuration.
