# Couture Lookbook

## Defining grammar

Couture lookbook design presents a collection as an ordered sequence of silhouette, construction, styling, and attitude. It is finished fashion communication rather than a record of studio process. Each image contributes to an authored world while letting garments remain distinct. Use it when the viewer must understand both the individual look and the collection’s progression across several frames.

## Composition

Plan a rhythm of full figures, three-quarter views, details, pauses, and occasional environmental images. Maintain one visual law—consistent set, horizon, crop family, or light—while changing pose and scale. Spreads should create comparison without squeezing every look together. Avoid unrelated editorial tableaux, repetitive centered portraits, and chaotic image mosaics that prevent the collection’s silhouette system from emerging.

## Type

Use editorial serif or refined sans typography with clear look numbers, credits, materials, and collection statements. Type can be quiet because imagery carries much of the narrative, but navigation through the sequence must remain obvious. Avoid perfume-like poetic copy that obscures the garments, fake handwritten notes that imply unfinished work, and excessive fashion masthead styling unrelated to the specific collection.

## Color and material

The palette follows the garments and set, supported by a restrained page field. Preserve accurate black, white, skin, textile, and metallic values. Paper, binding, and screen backgrounds should complement drape and surface without competing. Avoid universal cream filters, crushed shadow, and glossy effects that flatten fabric differences. Couture credibility depends on seeing construction, not simply sensing expense.

## Imagery and light

Show complete silhouettes, movement, backs, closures, handwork, and fabric behavior. Lighting may be crisp studio, directional theatrical, or soft daylight if it remains consistent enough to compare looks. Poses should reveal design rather than hide it. Generated references should request recognizable movement-level couture presentation using original garments and models, a coherent collection logic, precise editorial sequencing, and a consistent casting and set language.

## Common exits

The style becomes silk editorial when one material and sensual image rhythm dominate, atelier process when muslin, patterns, and unfinished construction lead, or quiet luxury when a single restrained object replaces collection sequence. It fails as a generic fashion shoot when striking pictures do not explain relationships among looks. Preserve order, variation, and enough descriptive evidence for each garment to stand on its own.

## Medium adaptation

Print lookbooks can use binding, paper changes, foldouts, or gatefolds to control pacing. Websites should offer a strong sequence plus accessible individual-look pages, credits, and alternative text. Presentations benefit from one look per slide and comparison spreads for motifs or development. Social campaigns should publish a deliberate series rather than isolated crops. Motion can use measured walks, turns, fabric movement, and detail intercuts; frenetic editing makes the clothes unreadable.
