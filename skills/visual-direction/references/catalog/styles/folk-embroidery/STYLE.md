# Folk Embroidery

## Defining grammar

Folk Embroidery is drawn with thread rather than merely illustrated with motifs. Begin by naming a regional or community tradition, because stitch families, cloth grounds, colors, and placement carry local knowledge. The visual grammar comes from the direction and density of stitches, slight changes in tension, layered thread, and the relationship between ornament and a useful textile. A flower may be reduced to counted geometry in one tradition and built from freehand satin stitch in another. Preserve that specificity. The style should make the viewer feel the resistance of cloth and the accumulated labor of the hand.

## Composition and type

Build composition from the textile's actual logic: a hem strengthened by a band, a neckline surrounded by repeated units, a sampler divided into registers, or an isolated medallion balanced by open cloth. Borders may turn corners imperfectly, and motifs may shift to accommodate seams or garment construction. When type is necessary, keep it outside the stitched field or treat it as restrained archival labeling. Do not convert letters into faux cross-stitch unless the chosen tradition genuinely includes alphabets. Large digital layouts can crop tightly enough to reveal individual stitches, then pull back to show how the decorated zone belongs to the whole object.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Choose thread and ground as a material pair: dyed wool on linen, silk on cotton, bright floss on dark cloth, or whitework whose relief appears through shadow. Color should follow the selected practice rather than a generic rainbow palette. Let fibers catch soft raking light so raised stitches, puckering, repair, and wear remain visible. Imagery may include named floral forms, animals, protective signs, domestic scenes, or geometric borders, but each set must remain coherent within one tradition. Avoid glossy plastic surfaces, printed simulations, and clean vector edges that erase the physical act of stitching.

## Common exits

The direction exits into Narrative Textile when sequential events and figures carry the main message across a continuous field. It exits into Quilting Pattern when pieced modules, batting, seams, and repeated blocks become dominant. It exits into Natural Textile when weave, fiber, and dye matter more than stitched motifs. Generic Scandinavian flowers, tourist-shop symbols, or a collage of unrelated “ethnic” marks do not make Folk Embroidery. Neither does a photographic fabric texture placed behind ordinary graphic design.

## Medium adaptation

For identity or packaging, derive a limited border, stitch rhythm, or color relationship from one documented tradition and credit that source in the working rationale. For editorial work, pair a full-object view with macro details and concise captions. For interfaces, use thread-like dividers and measured motif intervals sparingly; controls should remain legible and should not pretend to be handmade. In motion, reveal the order in which stitches accumulate or let a border grow along its real construction path. If the project concerns a living community, prefer collaboration, attribution, and commissioned work over anonymous borrowing, and keep sacred, ceremonial, or identity-bearing motifs in their proper context.
