# Analog Cinema Titles

## Core grammar

Analog Cinema Titles treats the film frame as a graphic surface for names, credits, and transition effects. Screen-centered display type, optical spread, printed cards, leader marks, limited color timing, grain, and frame-edge awareness distinguish it from a generic vintage poster. Use it for motion titles, event graphics, repertory programming, editorial openers, and identities that should feel projected rather than simply old.

## Composition and hierarchy

Design to a specific aspect ratio. Main titles can center with strong vertical balance, while credits may align to corners, baselines, or image contours. Preserve safe areas but let grain, registration, or leader evidence acknowledge the physical frame. Sequence matters: scale changes, dissolves, wipes, and cuts establish rhythm across multiple cards. A single still should imply that before-and-after relationship.

## Type and graphic language

Choose one display voice with period-aware proportions, then pair it with a plain credit face. Optical reproduction can soften edges, create slight bloom, or reveal uneven density. Keep letter spacing deliberate and readable. Avoid assembling unrelated “retro” fonts, fake marquee bulbs, or poster badges. The type must behave like photographed, printed, or optically composited information on screen.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Black, cream, faded red, cyan-blue, warm yellow, and timed green are useful starting points. Limit the palette per sequence. Film grain, dust, gate weave, and halation may appear lightly, but damage should never become the entire effect. Photographic backgrounds should leave enough tonal calm for credits. Flat cards can use paper texture, ink spread, or optical contrast to retain material character.

## Use across media

Motion is the natural home: build a title rhythm, not a single decorative logo. For posters and social crops, preserve the screen ratio or crop marks so the source grammar remains visible. Presentation chapter cards can borrow centered type and timed color. Web headers should avoid autoplay gimmicks and keep text accessible even when the visual treatment simulates optical softness.

## Common exits

Mid-century advertising layouts become Modular Poster or Mid-Century Advertising. Busy film-poster collage is not a title sequence. Perfect vector type over a fashionable grain overlay feels digitally nostalgic rather than optically produced. Excess scratches turn archival material into a damage cliché.

## Direction recipe

Select an aspect ratio and create a three-card progression: production credit, main title, and first narrative image. Use one display family, one utility family, and one optical transition. Add restrained grain and color timing, then review each card at actual screen duration as well as a still thumbnail.
