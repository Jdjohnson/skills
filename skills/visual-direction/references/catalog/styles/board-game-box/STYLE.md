# Board Game Box

## Core grammar

Board Game Box combines retail packaging with the promise of a playable system. A strong title world, illustrated premise, player and age cues, component previews, and rule iconography must work together at shelf distance. Use it for original tabletop concepts, game-related campaigns, playful kits, and packaging that needs to communicate both story and structured activity.

## Composition and hierarchy

The box front needs one dominant title, one immediate premise image, and a compact set of utility cues. Borders, corner badges, or panel bands can organize information, but they must not crowd the hero. Show enough components—cards, tokens, board fragments, dice, or tiles—to establish how the world becomes a game. Side and back panels should extend the hierarchy, not repeat the cover unchanged.

## Type and graphic language

Display lettering can carry genre and tone, paired with a highly legible utility sans for player count, time, and age. Icons should form a consistent rule language. Avoid using a famous game’s title proportions, color system, or trade dress. Novelty lettering must still read in a small retail thumbnail and alongside required information.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Saturated color, matte cardboard, spot gloss, foil accents, and illustrated depth can all support shelf presence. Limit special finishes to hierarchy-bearing elements. Component photography or rendering should use consistent scale and light. The illustration needs a coherent world, not a collage of every mechanic. Black, cream, red, green, and gold can suggest classic packaging, but the palette should fit the specific premise.

## Use across media

Packaging and product visualization are primary. Social imagery can reveal components or explain a turn without redesigning the whole identity. Motion may animate pieces from box to table. Web pages should carry the same title, icon, and component system while giving rules more room. Accessibility requires clear contrast and redundant labels for critical play information.

## Common exits

An illustrated poster without player or component cues does not prove a game. A regular product grid becomes Toy Catalog or supermarket packaging. Dense faux-vintage ornament obscures play structure. Avoid presenting a recognizable commercial game with altered names or copying an existing rules icon set.

## Direction recipe

Define the premise in one sentence and the core mechanic in one verb. Build a box front where title, illustration, and three utility cues survive at thumbnail size. Place two or three components beside the package, then verify that their shapes and symbols visibly belong to the same rule system.
