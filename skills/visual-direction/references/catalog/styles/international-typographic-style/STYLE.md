# International Typographic Style

## Defining grammar

Use this direction when information must feel objective, ordered, and public-minded. Its character comes from a repeatable system rather than a decorative look: an asymmetric grid, flush-left ragged-right type, disciplined scale relationships, objective photography, and white space that actively separates levels of meaning. Red can provide a decisive accent, but the style is not defined by a mandatory red-black-white palette. The test is whether many kinds of content can enter the same framework without weakening its logic.

## Composition and type

Establish columns, margins, and a baseline before placing content. Let major elements begin and end on grid lines, but avoid symmetry that makes the page ceremonial. Create hierarchy through size, weight, position, and interval. Headlines, captions, folios, and body text should share a clear proportional logic. Choose a neutral grotesk or related sans with a broad working family. Set text flush left and preserve a natural rag; centered headings and ornamental rules usually break the system. White space is load-bearing structure, not leftover area.

## Color, material, imagery, and light

Keep color economical: black and off-white, with one or two functional accents. Paper should read matte and printing direct. On screens, use flat fields and crisp edges rather than glow, glass, or soft atmospheric gradients. Photography should behave as evidence. Favor documentary subjects, architecture, objects, and people shown without theatrical styling. Crop photographs firmly to the grid and allow a single image block to carry weight. Light is even or naturally directional, with enough tonal range to preserve detail.

## Common exits

The direction fails when every object floats independently, type is centered for prestige, color becomes expressive decoration, or imagery turns sentimental. It also exits into Modular Poster when a single loud surface replaces a durable information system; into Swiss Phototypography when giant letterforms and the image become the main event; and into Neo-Grotesk Editorial when quiet reading pace matters more than visible modular order.

## Medium adaptation

For identity systems, define a flexible grid, type scale, image crop rules, and restrained accent logic. For websites and products, translate columns into responsive tracks while keeping hierarchy and alignment stable. For presentations, reduce each slide to one claim and one supporting visual block. For reports, use consistent running elements and caption structure. For spatial graphics, enlarge the same relationships for distance rather than inventing a separate visual language. Across media, preserve the system first; specific colors or typefaces can change without losing the style.
